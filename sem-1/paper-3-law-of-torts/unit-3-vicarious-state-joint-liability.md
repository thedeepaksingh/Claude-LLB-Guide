## 1. Background

1. **General rule.** A person is liable for his **own** wrongful acts. The law departs from this rule in three groups of cases, which make up this unit: (a) **vicarious liability** (one person answers for another's tort because of a relationship); (b) **liability of the State** for the torts of its servants; (c) **joint tortfeasors** (several persons answerable for the same damage).
2. **Why a person is made liable for another.** Three reasons are given: (i) *qui facit per alium facit per se* (he who acts through another acts himself); (ii) *respondeat superior* (let the superior answer), because the employer controls the work and takes the profit, so he should bear its risk ("enterprise liability"); (iii) the employer is **better able to pay** and to insure, so the victim is more likely to be compensated.
3. **Vicarious liability is liability without personal fault** of the master, but there must be a **tort by the servant**. The master and servant are **jointly and severally liable**, and the master may in theory claim an indemnity from the servant (rarely done).
4. **Indian background.** Indian law follows the English rules for private employers. For the State, Art. 300 refers back to the position of the East India Company, and the courts have moved from the old rule of sovereign immunity (*P. & O. Steam Navigation Co.*, 1861; *Kasturilal*, 1965) to a rule that confines immunity to a small group of sovereign acts and gives public-law compensation for violation of fundamental rights.

## 2. Vicarious liability

### 2.1 Relationships in which one person is liable for another

| Relationship | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Master and servant** | Master liable for torts of a servant done **in the course of employment** | Common law; the main topic |
| **Principal and agent** | Principal liable for the agent's torts done within his **authority** (express, implied or ostensible), e.g. fraud by an agent in the principal's business | *Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank* (1867); *Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.* [1912] AC 716 |
| **Partners** | Each partner is liable for a wrongful act of another partner in the ordinary course of the firm's business, jointly and severally | Indian Partnership Act, 1932, ss. 25–26 |
| **Company and its officers** | The company is liable for the torts of its servants and agents; directors are not liable for the company's torts unless they took part | Company law; *Lloyd v. Grace, Smith* |
| **Employer and independent contractor** | **Generally not** liable, with the exceptions in §2.5 | *Bower v. Peate*; *Honeywill & Stein v. Larkin Bros.* |
| **Owner and driver / bailor and bailee** | Owner liable if the driver drives **for the owner's purposes** or as his agent | *Morgans v. Launchbury* [1973] AC 127; *Ormrod v. Crosville* [1953] |
| **Authorisation, ratification, abetment** | A person who orders, authorises or later ratifies a tort is liable as if he had done it | *Eastern Counties Rly v. Broom* (1851) |
| **State and its servants** | See §3 | Art. 300; *Vidhyawati* |
| **Parent and child; husband and wife; guardian and ward** | **No** vicarious liability merely from the relationship | Common law |

### 2.2 Who is a servant? (the tests)

A **servant** works under a **contract of service**. An **independent contractor** works under a **contract for services** (Dec 2023 B6, Dec 2025 A3).

| Test | Idea | Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Control test** | Does the employer control not only *what* work is done but *how* it is done? | *Yewens v. Noakes* (1880); *Dharangadhara Chemical Works v. State of Saurashtra* AIR 1957 SC 264; *Shivanandan Sharma v. Punjab National Bank* AIR 1955 SC 404 |
| **Integration (organisation) test** | Is the worker an integral part of the business? | Denning L.J., *Stevenson Jordan & Harrison v. MacDonald & Evans* [1952] |
| **Multiple (mixed) test; economic reality** | Look at everything: control, ownership of tools, chance of profit or risk of loss, payment of wages, power to dismiss, who controls hiring, tax and insurance, and whether the worker is in business on his own account | *Ready Mixed Concrete (South East) v. Minister of Pensions* [1968] 2 QB 497; *Market Investigations v. Minister of Social Security* [1969] 2 QB 173; *Silver Jubilee Tailoring House v. Chief Inspector of Shops* (1974) 3 SCC 498 |
| **Indian approach** | The control test is the main test but not the only one; the courts look at the whole relationship | *Dharangadhara*; *Silver Jubilee*; *Hussainbhai v. Alath Factory Thezhilali Union* AIR 1978 SC 1410 (real employer) |

**Differences between a servant and an independent contractor**

| Point | Servant (contract *of* service) | Independent contractor (contract *for* services) |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Employer controls what and how | Controls only the result |
| Payment | Wages or salary | Fee or price for the job |
| Tools and risk | Employer's | Contractor's own |
| Hire and dismissal | By the employer | Contractor chooses his own staff |
| Vicarious liability | **Master is liable** | **Employer generally not liable** |
| Examples | Driver, clerk, shop assistant | Builder, lawyer, doctor in private practice, taxi on hire for a trip |
| Statutes | Labour laws apply; the CPA 2019 excludes **personal service** from "service" (Unit 5) | Labour laws do not apply; the CPA covers services *for* a consideration |

### 2.3 "In the course of employment"

The master is liable for (i) acts **authorised** by him, and (ii) **unauthorised modes** of doing authorised acts. He is not liable for acts which are quite outside the employment.

**Salmond's test.** A wrongful act is within the course of employment if it is either (a) a wrongful act **authorised** by the master, or (b) a wrongful and unauthorised **mode of doing some act authorised** by the master.

| Case | Facts | Held |
|---|---|---|
| ***Limpus v. London General Omnibus Co.*** (1862) 1 H & C 526 | A driver raced his bus against a rival's to get ahead, against the company's instructions, and caused a collision | Company liable: the driver was doing the authorised act (driving to pick up fares) in a prohibited manner |
| ***Beard v. London General Omnibus Co.*** [1900] 2 QB 530 | A conductor drove the bus, which was not his job, and injured the plaintiff | Not liable: driving was outside the conductor's employment |
| ***Bayley v. Manchester, Sheffield & Lincolnshire Rly*** (1873) LR 8 CP 148 | A porter forcibly pulled a passenger from the wrong train, thinking he was acting in the company's interest | Company liable |
| ***Poland v. John Parr & Sons*** [1927] 1 KB 236 | A carter struck a boy suspected of stealing sugar from a wagon | Employer liable: the act was for the protection of the employer's goods |
| ***Century Insurance Co. v. N.I. Road Transport Board*** [1942] AC 509 | A petrol-lorry driver lit a cigarette while delivering petrol and caused a fire | Employer liable: negligent performance of the duty of delivery |
| ***Twine v. Bean's Express*** (1946) | A driver gave an unauthorised lift to a hitchhiker, who was injured | Employer not liable: the lift was forbidden and for the driver's own purposes (the claimant was a trespasser) |
| ***Rose v. Plenty*** [1976] 1 WLR 141 | A milkman took a boy to help with deliveries against his employer's ban; the boy was injured | Employer liable: the boy was helping with the work and it was an unauthorised mode of doing the job |
| ***Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.*** [1912] AC 716 | A managing clerk fraudulently got a client's property transferred to himself | The firm was liable, because the fraud was committed in the course of the clerk's authority to deal with clients |
| ***Warren v. Henlys*** [1948] 2 All ER 935 | A petrol attendant attacked a customer after a dispute over payment | Employer not liable: the assault was personal revenge and not an unauthorised way of doing the job |
| ***Mohamud v. WM Morrison Supermarkets*** [2016] UKSC 11 | A petrol-station employee abused and attacked a customer who asked for a printout | Employer liable: **close connection** between the job (dealing with customers) and the wrong |
| ***WM Morrison Supermarkets v. Various Claimants*** [2020] UKSC 12 | An employee leaked colleagues' data out of personal spite against the employer | Employer **not** liable: the employee was pursuing his own purposes, not the employer's business (even if the job gave the opportunity) |

**Indian cases.** *State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati* AIR 1962 SC 933 (Government's jeep driven rashly: the State is liable like any master); *Rajasthan State Road Transport Corporation v. Kailash Nath Kothari* (1997) 7 SCC 481 † (a transport corporation's liability for the acts of its drivers and conductors in a collision claim; the Supreme Court applied the "course of employment" test). *Pushpabai Purshottam Udeshi v. Ranjit Ginning & Pressing Co.* AIR 1977 SC 1735 † is often cited on whether a servant using the master's vehicle was acting within the course of employment.

### 2.4 Borrowed servant and the "akin to employment" idea
- ***Mersey Docks & Harbour Board v. Coggins & Griffith*** [1947] AC 1: a crane-driver was lent by the Board to a stevedore, who gave him directions but did not control how he handled the crane. The Board stayed liable: the burden of shifting liability from the general employer is **heavy** and depends on who controls the manner of doing the work.
- ***Various Claimants v. Catholic Child Welfare Society*** [2012] UKSC 56: vicarious liability extends to a relationship **akin to employment** (a religious order and its brothers who taught in a school), where the wrong is closely connected with the activity done for the defendant. ***Cox v. Ministry of Justice*** [2016] UKSC 10 applied it to a prisoner working in a prison kitchen. ***Barclays Bank v. Various Claimants*** [2020] UKSC 13 refused vicarious liability for a doctor engaged as an independent contractor by the bank.

### 2.5 Employer and independent contractor: liability exceptions
An employer is **not liable** for the torts of an independent contractor *except* where:
1. he **authorised** or **ratified** the wrongful act;
2. he was **negligent in selecting** the contractor, or failed to take care in giving instructions;
3. the work is **inherently dangerous or hazardous**, so the employer has a **non-delegable duty** (*Honeywill & Stein v. Larkin Bros.* [1934] 1 KB 191: a photographer's magnesium flash set the cinema curtains on fire; the employer was liable);
4. the work interferes with **support** or with a **right of the highway** (*Bower v. Peate* (1876) 1 QBD 321; *Dalton v. Henry Angus* (1881) 6 App Cas 740);
5. there is a **statutory duty** that cannot be delegated;
6. the tort is one of **strict liability** (*Rylands v. Fletcher*; the Indian absolute-liability rule);
7. the employer **interferes** with the contractor and takes control.

### 2.6 Indian statutes of note
- **Employees' Compensation Act, 1923** (formerly the Workmen's Compensation Act): no-fault statutory compensation for employment injury, independent of the tort action.
- **Motor Vehicles Act, 1988**: ss. 146–149 (compulsory third-party insurance; insurer pays to the third party), s. 166 (claims for compensation), s. 164 (fixed compensation) ‡.
- **Doctrine of common employment** (*Priestley v. Fowler*, 1837: a servant could not sue his master for injury caused by a fellow-servant) was abolished in England by the Law Reform (Personal Injuries) Act, 1948; textbooks treat it as obsolete in India (†).

## 3. Liability of the State

### 3.1 The rule of sovereign immunity (history)
1. **English law.** "The King can do no wrong"; the Crown could not be sued in tort without its consent. The **Crown Proceedings Act, 1947** (UK) made the Crown liable like a private person.
2. **India under the Company and the Crown.** The East India Company was a trading company with sovereign powers. *Peninsular & Oriental Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India* (1861) 5 Bom HCR App 1 (Peacock C.J.): the Secretary of State was liable when a horse of the plaintiff was injured by the negligence of Government servants working in the Government dockyard at Kidderpore (Calcutta) †, because the act was done in the conduct of an **undertaking which a private person could carry on**, but he would not have been liable if it was done in the exercise of **sovereign** powers (waging war, taking prisoners, etc.). This is the origin of the sovereign / non-sovereign distinction in India.
3. **After 1861.** *Secretary of State v. Hari Bhanji* (1882) 5 Mad 273 † and *Nobin Chunder Dey v. Secretary of State* (1875) 1 Cal 11 † gave the Government liability for non-sovereign acts (I could not confirm the facts of either, so the model answers do not rely on them). The Government of India Acts of 1858, 1915 and 1935 (s. 176) kept the position, and **Art. 300(1)** of the Constitution continued it: the Union and State may sue and be sued **in the like cases as the Dominion and the Provinces** might have been, subject to any law made by Parliament or the State legislature. No such general law has been made.

### 3.2 The Supreme Court's cases

| Case | Facts | Held |
|---|---|---|
| **State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati** AIR 1962 SC 933 | A Government jeep, being driven back from a workshop for the Collector's use, ran into and killed a pedestrian through the driver's rash driving | **State liable**, as any employer. The State's liability is the same as the Company's was; the driver's work was not sovereign power. After the Constitution the old English doctrine of immunity has no application |
| **Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P.** AIR 1965 SC 1039 | Police seized gold and silver from the plaintiff, a dealer in Meerut. The goods were kept in the police store, and the head constable misappropriated them and fled to Pakistan. The dealer sued the State | **State not liable.** The police were exercising **statutory powers of arrest, search and seizure**, which are **sovereign powers** that a private person cannot exercise. Gajendragadkar C.J. said that the law needed change and recommended legislation |
| **Saheli, Women's Resources Centre v. Commissioner of Police, Delhi** (1990) 1 SCC 422 | A child was beaten to death by a police officer | State liable to pay compensation to the mother. *Kasturilal* did not apply where the police acted unlawfully and beyond their statutory powers |
| **Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa** (1993) 2 SCC 746 | A young man died in police custody (his body was found on a railway track) | **Compensation under Art. 32**, a **public-law remedy** against the State for violation of Art. 21; the defence of sovereign immunity **does not apply**. Compensation is without prejudice to a civil or criminal action |
| **N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v. State of A.P.** (1994) 6 SCC 205 | Foodgrains seized by officials under a control order were left to rot for want of care; the owner sued | **State liable**. *Kasturilal* is confined to its facts. In the changed context sovereign powers are only the acts of State and a few high-level functions; **negligent storage** is not such. |
| **State of A.P. v. Challa Ramakrishna Reddy** (2000) 5 SCC 712 | A prisoner was killed in a sub-jail by a gang after the authorities failed to protect him † | State liable; sovereign immunity cannot be pleaded where the State fails to protect the right to life of a person in its custody |
| **Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das** (2000) 2 SCC 465 | A Bangladeshi national was raped by railway employees in the Railways' Yatri Niwas at Howrah | Union (Railways) liable to pay compensation; fundamental right to life protects **non-citizens**; the Court awarded ₹10 lakh |
| **Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of W.B.** (1996) 4 SCC 37 | A man injured in a train accident was refused treatment by government hospitals | Failure to give emergency treatment violated Art. 21; State to pay compensation (₹25,000) |

**The "public-law remedy".** From *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 (illegal detention for 14 years after acquittal; ₹30,000 as interim compensation) and *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677 (a legislator illegally detained; ₹50,000), the courts award compensation under Arts. 32 and 226 for a violation of fundamental rights, treating it as a remedy in **public law** that is in addition to a civil claim in **private law**. *D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal* (1997) 1 SCC 416 laid down guidelines for arrest and detention and said that compensation for custodial death is available irrespective of the sovereign function. Some writers call this "constitutional tort".

### 3.3 The position now (the answer to Dec 2024 C10)
1. The distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions is **no longer used to decide the State's liability for ordinary torts** (negligence of drivers, hospitals, storage, public works, railways, municipal corporations).
2. **Sovereign** immunity survives, if at all, only for **core sovereign acts**: acts of State, waging war, legislative acts, judicial acts and some high-level policy decisions. *Kasturilal* has not been overruled but has been **confined to its facts**; in *Nagendra Rao* the Court said it could not be applied to every act of a government servant.
3. For **fundamental-rights violations** there is **no** sovereign immunity (*Nilabati*, *Challa Ramakrishna*).
4. **Procedure.** A suit against the Government needs **two months' prior notice** under **CPC s. 80** (the court may allow an urgent suit with leave; s. 80(2)). The suit lies against the Union of India or the State (Art. 300; CPC s. 79).
5. **Law reform.** The Law Commission's First Report (1956) recommended that the State be liable as a private employer; a **Government Liability Bill** (1965 and 1967) was introduced but lapsed (†). The matter still rests on judicial decisions.

### 3.4 Comparison: sovereign v. non-sovereign functions

| Sovereign (immunity possible) | Non-sovereign (liable) |
|---|---|
| Waging war, defence, foreign relations, acts of State | Running railways, hospitals, schools, transport, post, electricity, public works |
| Making laws and administering justice | Carrying on trading or commercial undertakings |
| Exercise of statutory police powers of arrest and seizure (*Kasturilal*; now doubted) | Negligent driving of a Government vehicle (*Vidhyawati*), negligent storage (*Nagendra Rao*) |

## 4. Joint tortfeasors

### 4.1 Meaning
**Joint tortfeasors** are two or more persons who are responsible for the **same tort** (one wrong, one damage), so that the cause of action against each is the same. Winfield: persons are joint tortfeasors when the cause of action against each of them is the same, namely the same tort.

### 4.2 When are persons joint tortfeasors? (*The Koursk* [1924] P 140, Scrutton L.J.)
1. **Agent and principal**, or **master and servant**, where the master is liable vicariously for the servant's tort.
2. **Breach of a joint duty** (two persons under a joint duty of care or a joint statutory duty).
3. **Concerted action / common design** (two or more persons act in furtherance of a common design or plan, e.g. a joint trespass, a conspiracy to defame, a joint assault; ***Brooke v. Bool*** [1928] 2 KB 578: two persons looked for a gas leak with a naked flame; both liable).
Persons whose independent acts contribute to the same damage are **not** joint tortfeasors but **concurrent (several, independent) tortfeasors** (*Thompson v. London County Council* [1899] 1 QB 840; *Drinkwater v. Kimber* [1952] 2 QB 281: two negligent drivers who collided with a pedestrian). In practice the plaintiff can recover the whole damage from each.

### 4.3 Nature and extent of liability
1. **Joint and several liability.** Each tortfeasor is liable for the **whole damage**. The plaintiff can sue all together, or any one, and recover the full amount from anyone (but only once).
2. **Judgment.** At common law judgment against one barred an action against the others (*Brinsmead v. Harrison* (1872) LR 7 CP 547). This rule was removed in England by the Law Reform (Married Women and Tortfeasors) Act, 1935 and the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act, 1978 (s. 3). India has no statute on the point, and the Indian courts' practice is not settled in the sources I could check †.
3. **Release.** A **release** of one joint tortfeasor releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor* (1614); *Duck v. Mayeu* [1892] 2 QB 511 †), but a **covenant not to sue** one does not release the others. A settlement with one tortfeasor that is accepted in full satisfaction of the whole claim extinguishes the claim against the others (*Jameson v. CEGB* [2000] 1 AC 455).
4. **Contribution.** The old rule was **no contribution** between wrongdoers (*Merryweather v. Nixan* (1799) 8 TR 186). The English 1935 and 1978 Acts allow a tortfeasor who has paid more than his share to claim contribution from the others, the court deciding what is "just and equitable" having regard to the responsibility of each. India has **no general statute** on contribution between tortfeasors (†); the courts apply the English principle of equitable contribution among those who are not knowing wrongdoers.
5. **Indemnity.** A master who is made liable vicariously for his servant's tort has a right to be indemnified by the servant, and an agent who commits a tort at the principal's request is entitled to an indemnity unless he knew it was a wrong.

### 4.4 Composite negligence in motor-accident claims (India)
Where two vehicles collide and both drivers are negligent, a third party injured can sue either or both and recover the **whole** amount from any; the tribunal need not apportion between the two (*T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan* (2008) 3 SCC 748; *Khenyei v. New India Assurance Co.* (2015) 9 SCC 273 †). This differs from **contributory negligence**, where the plaintiff's own fault reduces the damages.

### 4.5 Tables

| | Joint tortfeasors | Concurrent / independent tortfeasors |
|---|---|---|
| Cause of action | One, the same tort | Separate wrongs, one damage |
| Examples | Master-servant; common design; joint duty | Two drivers colliding with a pedestrian |
| Liability | Joint and several; whole damage | Several; each for the whole of the same damage (or his share if separable) |
| Release of one | Releases all (not a covenant not to sue) | Does not release the others, unless it was full satisfaction |

## 5. "Who may sue and be sued for a tort to and by another?" (Dec 2025 C12)

The question asks for the **classes of cases and the grounds** on which a person is liable for a wrong done by another, and on which a person may sue for a wrong done to another. Use this plan:

**A. Sued for a tort by another (liability)**
1. Master for servant (course of employment).
2. Principal for agent (authority; ratification).
3. Partners for partners (Partnership Act ss. 25–26).
4. Company for officers and servants.
5. Employer for independent contractor (the seven exceptions).
6. Owner for driver (driving for the owner's purposes).
7. Persons who authorise, abet or ratify; joint tortfeasors.
8. The State for its servants (Art. 300; *Vidhyawati*; *Nagendra Rao*).
9. Special cases: guardians and parents are **not** liable merely by relationship; a husband is not liable for his wife's tort.

**B. Suing for a tort to another (standing)**
1. **Dependants** (spouse, parent, child) under the **Fatal Accidents Act, 1855**, s. 1A, and **legal representatives** under the MV Act, s. 166.
2. **Estate** of the deceased: the cause of action survives for property damage (Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306 †; but not defamation, assault or other personal injury not causing death).
3. **Next friend / guardian** for a minor or a person of unsound mind (CPC O. XXXII).
4. **Master** for loss of his servant's services (*per quod servitium amisit*) and a husband for loss of consortium (limited and rarely used; †).
5. **Consumer** associations or one consumer on behalf of many (CPA 2019, s. 35).
6. **Public-interest litigant** in constitutional courts for a class of persons who cannot approach the court (Art. 32/226).

## 6. Current developments (to November 2026)

1. **The doctors-and-lawyers service question (relevant to Unit 5).** In *Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases* (2024 INSC 410, decided 14 May 2024) the Supreme Court held that advocates' services are not "service" under the Consumer Protection Act, because the profession is *sui generis* and the relationship is a contract of personal service. It also said *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha* (1995) should be reconsidered by a larger bench ‡. On 10 February 2026 a bench led by the Chief Justice issued notice on a petition seeking to exclude qualified doctors from the Act ‡ (Unit 5).
2. **Vicarious liability in England** has expanded through the "close connection" test (*Mohamud*, 2016) and relationships "akin to employment" (*Catholic Child Welfare Society*, 2012), and been checked in *Barclays Bank* and *Morrison* (2020). Indian courts cite them as persuasive authority. 
3. **State liability for digital harms and data breaches** is still developing. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 puts duties on data fiduciaries but also exempts State bodies in listed cases (s. 17 †), so the question is not settled.

**Quick-revision box**
- Vicarious liability needs: (i) a tort by the servant; (ii) a master-servant (or similar) relationship; (iii) the tort in the course of employment.
- Salmond's test: authorised act, or unauthorised mode of an authorised act.
- Independent contractor: no liability, except for authorisation, hazardous or non-delegable duty, interference, support, highway and strict liability.
- State: *P&O* (1861) → *Vidhyawati* (1962) → *Kasturilal* (1965) → *Nilabati* (1993) → *Nagendra Rao* (1994). Immunity is only for core sovereign acts; fundamental-rights violations have no immunity.
- Joint tortfeasors: agent-principal, joint duty, common design; liable jointly and severally; contribution (no Indian statute †).
