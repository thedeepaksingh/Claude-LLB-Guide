<a id="a-85"></a>
**A-85 · Nuisance** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q1*

Nuisance is an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or of some right over it (Winfield). It is of two kinds: public nuisance, which affects a class of people and is a crime, and private nuisance, which is a tort. It needs unreasonable interference and substantial damage. Examples: smoke, noise, smell, vibration (*Sturges v. Bridgman*, 1879).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-86"></a>
**A-86 · Public nuisance** · *Related: B-37, C-17*

An act or omission that materially affects the reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of people (BNS s. 270 †). It is a crime, and a tort only for a person who suffers special damage different in kind from the public (*Rose v. Miles*, 1815). The Advocate-General or two persons with leave may sue (CPC s. 91). Examples: obstructing a highway, a dangerous quarry.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-87"></a>
**A-87 · Private nuisance** · *Related: B-37, C-17*

An unreasonable interference with a person's use or enjoyment of his land, causing substantial damage, such as smoke, noise or smell, damage to property, or interference with an easement. Only a person with a right in the land can sue (*Hunter v. Canary Wharf*, 1997). Locality matters for comfort (*Sturges*) but not for physical damage (*St Helen's v. Tipping*, 1865). Remedies: damages, injunction, abatement.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-88"></a>
**A-88 · Public and private nuisance compared** · *Related: B-37*

Public nuisance affects a class of people and is a crime, a tort only with special damage, and is prosecuted or sued by the Advocate-General. Private nuisance affects one person's land, and is a tort only, for which the occupier sues for damages or an injunction. Both are indirect interferences needing proof of damage in tort.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-89"></a>
**A-89 · Defences to nuisance** · *Related: B-38, C-17*

Valid: prescription (20 years: Easements Act, 1882, s. 15), statutory authority, consent, act of a stranger or of God, reasonable user, the plaintiff's abnormal sensitivity (*Robinson v. Kilvert*). Not defences: coming to the nuisance (*Bliss v. Hall*), public benefit (*Adams v. Ursell*), reasonable care, that others also contribute (*Lambton v. Mellish*), planning permission alone.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-90"></a>
**A-90 · Prescription as a defence to nuisance** · *Related: B-38*

A right to commit a nuisance can be acquired as an easement by 20 years' uninterrupted use as of right (Indian Easements Act, 1882, s. 15; *Sturges v. Bridgman*). The period runs from the time the plaintiff could first sue. Once acquired, the activity is lawful and cannot be stopped; a public nuisance cannot be acquired by prescription †.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-91"></a>
**A-91 · Coming to the nuisance** · *Related: B-38*

It is no defence that the plaintiff came to the nuisance, that is, moved near the defendant's activity after it began (*Bliss v. Hall*, 1838; *Sturges v. Bridgman*, 1879). *Miller v. Jackson* (1977) and *Coventry v. Lawrence* (2014) show the courts may, however, refuse an injunction and award damages. The reasoning is that a nuisance remains a nuisance whoever suffers.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-92"></a>
**A-92 · Remedies for nuisance** · *Related: B-38, C-17*

(1) Damages; (2) injunction, prohibitory or mandatory, though damages may replace it if an injunction would be oppressive (*Shelfer*; *Coventry v. Lawrence*); (3) abatement, a self-help remedy: the occupier may remove the nuisance after notice, using no more force than necessary (*Lemmon v. Webb*, 1895). For public nuisance there is also prosecution and a magistrate's order for removal (BNSS s. 152 †).

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-93"></a>
**A-93 · Trespass** · *Related: B-40, C-18*

Trespass is a direct and unjustified interference with a person, land or goods, intentional or negligent. It is actionable *per se*, without proof of damage. It is distinguished from trespass on the case, which concerns indirect harm. Its forms are trespass to the person (assault, battery, false imprisonment), to land and to goods.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-94"></a>
**A-94 · Trespass to land** · *Related: B-40, C-18*

The direct, unjustified interference with another's possession of land, by entering, remaining on it, or placing something on it. The plaintiff need only have possession. It is actionable *per se*. Mistake is no defence (*Basely v. Clarkson*, 1681). Defences: licence, justification by law, necessity, easement. *Entick v. Carrington* (1765): officials need lawful authority to enter.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-95"></a>
**A-95 · Trespass *ab initio*** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q2*

A person who enters land by authority of the law but then commits a positive wrongful act is treated as a trespasser from the beginning. *The Six Carpenters' Case* (1610): refusal to pay for wine was a mere omission, so they were not trespassers *ab initio*; a positive act would have made them so. It applies only to entry under legal authority, not by licence.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-96"></a>
**A-96 · Assault** · *Related: B-42, C-18*

An act by which the defendant intentionally or recklessly makes the plaintiff reasonably apprehend immediate unlawful force. No contact is needed. Words alone may be enough (*R v. Ireland*, 1998), but may also negate a threat (*Tuberville v. Savage*, 1669). *Stephens v. Myers* (1830): a man advanced with a clenched fist towards the chairman of a meeting. It is actionable *per se* (BNS ss. 128–131 †).

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-97"></a>
**A-97 · Battery** · *Related: B-42, C-18*

The direct and intentional application of force to another without lawful justification. The slightest unwanted touch suffices (*Cole v. Turner*, 1704); a continuing act is enough (*Fagan*, 1969). Treatment without consent is a battery (*Samira Kohli*, 2008). Defences: consent, self-defence, lawful authority, necessity. It is actionable *per se* and is also a crime (BNS ss. 128–131 †).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-98"></a>
**A-98 · False imprisonment** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q3*

Total restraint of a person's liberty without lawful justification. Ingredients: (1) total restraint, partial obstruction is not enough (*Bird v. Jones*, 1845); (2) no lawful authority; (3) intentional act; (4) the plaintiff's knowledge is unnecessary (*Meering*, 1919). Remedies: damages, *habeas corpus*, compensation under Arts. 21/32 (*Rudul Sah*, 1983).

*Word count: 48*

<a id="a-99"></a>
**A-99 · Malicious prosecution** · *Related: B-44, C-18*

Prosecution of the plaintiff maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause, ending in his favour, and causing damage to his reputation, person or property (*Hicks v. Faulkner*, 1878; *Glinski v. McIver*, 1962). Unlike false imprisonment, the process may be lawful but the prosecution is malicious, and damage must be proved. Limitation: one year (Art. 74) ‡.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-100"></a>
**A-100 · Conversion** · *Related: B-43, C-18*

A dealing with another's goods inconsistent with his rights, so as to deny his title: selling, destroying, refusing to return, or using as one's own. Good faith is no defence: *Hollins v. Fowler* (1875), an innocent broker who sold stolen cotton was liable. A mere temporary interference that does not deny title is not conversion (*Fouldes v. Willoughby*, 1841). Remedies: damages, return.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-101"></a>
**A-101 · Deceit** · *Related: B-45, C-21*

A false statement of fact made knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly, with intent that the plaintiff act on it, which he does and suffers damage (*Derry v. Peek*, 1889). An honest belief, however unreasonable, is no fraud. Remedy: damages for all direct loss (*Doyle v. Olby*). Contract Act, 1872, ss. 17 and 19: a contract induced by fraud is voidable.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-102"></a>
**A-102 · Defamation** · *Related: B-46, C-19*

The publication of a statement that tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society or to make them shun him (*Sim v. Stretch*, 1936). Essentials: a defamatory statement, referring to the plaintiff, published to a third person. It is a tort (libel or slander) and a crime (BNS s. 356). Defences: truth, fair comment, privilege.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-103"></a>
**A-103 · Libel and slander** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q1*

Libel is defamation in permanent form (writing, print, picture, film) and is actionable without proof of damage. Slander is in transient form (spoken words, gestures) and needs proof of special damage, except where it imputes a crime punishable with imprisonment, a loathsome disease, unchastity in a woman, or damages a person in his profession or trade (English law). In India both are offences (BNS s. 356).

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-104"></a>
**A-104 · Innuendo** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q4*

Words that appear harmless but carry a defamatory meaning because of extrinsic facts known to the audience. A true innuendo must be pleaded and proved with those facts: *Tolley v. Fry* (1931), a golfer in a chocolate advertisement; *Cassidy v. Daily Mirror* (1929), "Mr C and Miss X, whose engagement is announced", defamed Mrs C. A false innuendo is an inference from the words alone (*Lewis v. Daily Telegraph*, 1964).

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-105"></a>
**A-105 · Justification (truth)** · *Related: B-48, C-19*

In civil law, truth is a complete defence even if the motive is malicious; the defendant must prove the substance (the "sting") of the statement to be true (*Alexander v. North Eastern Railway*, 1865). If the plea fails, damages may be higher. In criminal law, truth is a defence only if the publication was for the public good (BNS s. 356, Exception 1).

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-106"></a>
**A-106 · Fair comment** · *Related: B-48, C-19, C-20*

Honest opinion on a matter of public interest, based on true or privileged facts, expressed without malice (*Campbell v. Spottiswoode*, 1863; *Kemsley v. Foot*, 1952). It must be recognisable as opinion. Indian Exceptions to BNS s. 356 include the conduct of public servants, public questions and the merits of a public performance (Exception 6: book problem).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-107"></a>
**A-107 · Absolute privilege** · *Related: B-49, C-19*

A privilege under which no action lies even if the statement is false and malicious. It covers statements by members in Parliament and State legislatures (Arts. 105(2), 194(2)); statements in judicial proceedings by judges, advocates, parties and witnesses; communications between officers of State on State business (*Chatterton*, 1895); and fair reports of legislative proceedings (Art. 361A †).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-108"></a>
**A-108 · Qualified privilege** · *Related: B-49, C-19*

Protection for statements made honestly in discharge of a legal, social or moral duty, or to protect an interest, to a person with a corresponding duty or interest (*Toogood v. Spyring*, 1834; *Adam v. Ward*, 1917), and for fair reports of judicial proceedings. It is lost by malice (*Horrocks v. Lowe*, 1975). Indian Exceptions 8, 9 and 10 to BNS s. 356 are similar.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-109"></a>
**A-109 · Criminal defamation: BNS s. 356** · *Related: B-50, C-19*

BNS s. 356 (replacing IPC ss. 499–502 from 1 July 2024) punishes defamation with simple imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both, or community service ‡, subject to ten Exceptions (truth for the public good, public servants, public questions and so on). *Subramanian Swamy* (2016) upheld the offence, treating reputation as part of Art. 21.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-110"></a>
**A-110 · Publication in defamation** · *Related: C-19*

Publication is communication of the defamatory statement to at least one person other than the plaintiff. Dictation to a typist is publication (*Pullman v. Hill*, 1891). Each repetition is a fresh publication; each sale of a newspaper is a publication (*Duke of Brunswick v. Harmer*, 1849). A statement made only to the plaintiff is not published.

*Word count: 56*
