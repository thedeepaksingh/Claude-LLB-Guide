<a id="a-41"></a>
**A-41 · Vicarious liability** · *Related: B-21, C-10*

Vicarious liability is the liability of one person for a tort committed by another because of a relationship between them, though he is himself without fault. The main case is master and servant: the master is liable for torts of the servant in the course of employment. Others: principal and agent, partners, company, owner and driver, and the State.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-42"></a>
**A-42 · *Qui facit per alium facit per se* and *respondeat superior*** · *Related: B-21*

*Qui facit per alium facit per se*: he who acts through another acts himself. *Respondeat superior*: let the superior answer. They explain why a master is liable for his servant's torts: the master controls the work, takes its profit and is better able to pay and to insure, so that the injured person is more likely to be compensated.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-43"></a>
**A-43 · Tests to identify a servant** · *Related: B-21, C-10*

(1) Control test: does the employer control what is done and how (*Dharangadhara*, 1957)? (2) Integration test: is the worker part of the business (Denning L.J.)? (3) Mixed or economic reality test: control, tools, profit and risk, power to dismiss (*Ready Mixed Concrete*, 1968; *Silver Jubilee Tailoring House*, 1974). Indian courts treat control as the main test.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-44"></a>
**A-44 · Contract of service and contract for service** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q3*

A contract of service is between employer and servant, who works under the employer's control for wages; the employer is vicariously liable. A contract for services is between a client and an independent contractor, who controls his own work for a fee; the client is generally not liable. Under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, personal service is excluded from "service".

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-45"></a>
**A-45 · Course of employment** · *Related: B-22, C-10*

A master is liable for a servant's tort only if it was done in the course of employment, that is, an act authorised by the master or an unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act. *Limpus* (1862): a driver racing a rival bus; liable. *Beard* (1900): a conductor driving the bus; not liable. A fraud closely connected with the job can be within it (*Lloyd v. Grace, Smith*, 1912).

*Word count: 69*

<a id="a-46"></a>
**A-46 · Salmond's test of course of employment** · *Related: B-22*

A wrongful act is within the course of employment if it is (a) a wrongful act authorised by the master, or (b) a wrongful and unauthorised mode of doing some act authorised by the master. A prohibition on the manner of doing the work does not take the act outside the employment (*Limpus*), but an act of a different kind does (*Beard*).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-47"></a>
**A-47 · Liability for independent contractors** · *Related: B-23, C-10*

An employer is generally not liable for the torts of an independent contractor. Exceptions: he authorised or ratified the wrong; was negligent in choosing the contractor; the work is inherently dangerous (*Honeywill v. Larkin*, 1934); it affects support or the highway (*Bower v. Peate*); there is a non-delegable statutory duty; strict liability applies; or the employer interfered.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-48"></a>
**A-48 · Principal and agent** · *Related: C-10*

A principal is liable for the torts of his agent committed within the agent's actual, implied or apparent authority, including fraud in the course of the principal's business (*Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.*, 1912; *Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank*, 1867). He is also liable for acts he authorised or ratified. An agent who commits a tort is personally liable as well.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-49"></a>
**A-49 · Partners and companies** · *Related: C-10*

Each partner is liable, jointly and severally, for a wrongful act of another partner done in the ordinary course of the firm's business (Partnership Act, 1932, ss. 25–26). A company is liable for torts of its servants and agents in the course of their employment; directors are liable only for their own part. A registered trade union may sue and be sued (Trade Unions Act, 1926).

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-50"></a>
**A-50 · Borrowed servant** · *Related: C-10*

Where a servant is lent to another employer, the general employer remains liable unless control of the manner of work passes to the hirer; the burden of shifting liability is heavy (*Mersey Docks v. Coggins & Griffith*, 1947). In England liability also extends to relationships "akin to employment" (*Various Claimants v. Catholic Child Welfare Society*, 2012).

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-51"></a>
**A-51 · Sovereign immunity** · *Related: B-24, C-11*

At common law "the King can do no wrong", so the Crown could not be sued in tort. In India, liability for sovereign functions was excluded, but not for non-sovereign ones (*P. & O.*, 1861). The Supreme Court has since confined immunity to core sovereign acts (acts of State, defence, legislation, judicial functions); it is no defence to violation of fundamental rights (*Nilabati Behera*, 1993).

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-52"></a>
**A-52 · *P. & O. Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India* (1861)** · *Related: B-24, C-11*

A horse of the plaintiff was injured by negligence of Government servants in the Government dockyard at Kidderpore, Calcutta †. Peacock C.J. held the Secretary of State liable, as the act was done in an undertaking that a private person could carry on, but not for acts of sovereign power. It is the origin of the sovereign and non-sovereign distinction in India.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-53"></a>
**A-53 · Article 300 of the Constitution** · *Related: B-24, C-11*

Art. 300(1): the Government of India and the State Governments may sue and be sued by the name of the Union of India or the State, and in the like cases as the Dominion and the Provinces might have been, subject to any law made by Parliament or the legislature. No such law has been made, so the courts apply the pre-Constitution rule, as developed by case law.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-54"></a>
**A-54 · *Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P.* (1965)** · *Related: B-24, C-11*

Police seized gold and silver from a Meerut dealer; the head constable misappropriated it and fled to Pakistan. The Supreme Court held the State not liable, because seizure is an exercise of sovereign power. Gajendragadkar C.J. said the law needed change by legislation. The case has been confined to its facts (*Nagendra Rao*, 1994) and not followed in custodial violence cases.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-55"></a>
**A-55 · *State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati* (1962)** · *Related: B-24, C-11*

A Government jeep, driven back from a workshop for the Collector, ran over and killed a pedestrian through the driver's rash driving. The Supreme Court held the State liable like any employer, because the driver's work was not an exercise of sovereign power and the English doctrine of immunity did not apply after the Constitution. It was affirmed in later cases.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-56"></a>
**A-56 · Public-law compensation** · *Related: B-25, C-11*

Under Arts. 32 and 226 the Supreme Court and High Courts award compensation against the State for violation of fundamental rights, as a remedy in public law apart from any civil claim, without the defence of sovereign immunity: *Rudul Sah* (1983), *Bhim Singh* (1985), *Nilabati Behera* (1993), *D.K. Basu* (1997), *Chandrima Das* (2000).

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-57"></a>
**A-57 · Notice under CPC s. 80** · *Related: C-11*

No suit may be instituted against the Government or a public officer for an act done in official capacity until two months after written notice stating the cause of action, name and residence of the plaintiff and the relief claimed. With the court's leave an urgent suit may be filed without notice (s. 80(2)). The notice gives the Government an opportunity to settle.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-58"></a>
**A-58 · Joint tortfeasors** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q4*

Joint tortfeasors are two or more persons responsible for the same tort, so that the cause of action against each is the same. They include master and servant, principal and agent, persons under a joint duty, and persons acting in concert or in furtherance of a common design (*Brooke v. Bool*, 1928). Each is liable for the whole damage, jointly and severally.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-59"></a>
**A-59 · Test in *The Koursk* (1924)** · *Related: B-26, C-12*

Scrutton L.J. identified joint tortfeasors as cases of agent and principal, master and servant, breach of a joint duty, or persons acting in concert in furtherance of a common design. Persons whose independent acts cause the same damage are not joint tortfeasors but concurrent tortfeasors (*Thompson v. LCC*, 1899; *Drinkwater v. Kimber*, 1952).

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-60"></a>
**A-60 · Contribution between tortfeasors** · *Related: B-26, C-12*

The old rule was no contribution between wrongdoers (*Merryweather v. Nixan*, 1799). In England the Law Reform (Married Women and Tortfeasors) Act, 1935 and the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act, 1978 let a tortfeasor who has paid more than his share recover a just and equitable contribution. India has no general statute †; courts apply equitable principles.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-61"></a>
**A-61 · Release of a joint tortfeasor** · *Related: B-26, C-12*

At common law a release of one joint tortfeasor releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor*, 1614), because the cause of action is single. A covenant not to sue one does not release the others. A settlement accepted in full satisfaction of the whole claim also extinguishes the claim against the others (*Jameson v. CEGB*, 2000).

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-62"></a>
**A-62 · Composite negligence** · *Related: B-26, C-12*

Where the negligence of two or more persons contributes to one accident, as in a collision of two vehicles, the injured third party may sue either or both and recover the whole compensation from any (*T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan*, 2008; *Khenyei*, 2015 †). It differs from contributory negligence, where the plaintiff's own fault reduces damages.

*Word count: 54*
