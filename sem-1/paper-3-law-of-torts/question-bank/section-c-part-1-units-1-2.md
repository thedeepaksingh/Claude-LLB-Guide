## C-01 · Define tort. Discuss its nature and characteristics, and distinguish it from crime and contract

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 and 10 marks: Dec 2023 Q1 (A-02, "Define Tort and point out its main characteristics"), Dec 2022 Q2 (A-05, tort and crime), Dec 2022 Q7 (B-02, tort and contract), Dec 2024 Q2 (A-07, pigeon-hole theory). Related: B-01, B-03, B-06.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** definitions 4 · nature and characteristics 4 · constituents 3 · theories (pigeon-hole v. general principle) 2 · tort v. crime 3 · tort v. contract 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The word *tort* comes from the Latin *tortum*, "twisted" or "wrong", and reached English through Norman French. It means a civil wrong for which the law gives a remedy in damages. The law of torts grew from the medieval writs of trespass and trespass on the case. India has no code of torts; the courts apply English common law, adapted to Indian conditions under the rule of "justice, equity and good conscience".

**1. Definitions**
- **Salmond:** a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common-law action for **unliquidated damages**, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation.
- **Winfield:** tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty **primarily fixed by law**; such duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages.
- **Fraser:** an infringement of a **right *in rem*** of a private individual giving a right of compensation at the suit of the injured party.
- **Limitation Act, 1963, s. 2(m):** "tort" means a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or the breach of a trust.

Salmond defines by remedy, Winfield by the source of the duty, and Fraser by the right. Together they show that a tort is (i) a civil wrong, (ii) with a duty fixed by law and owed to the world, and (iii) a remedy in damages.

**2. Nature and characteristics**
1. **A civil wrong, not a crime.** The injured person sues for compensation; the State does not prosecute.
2. **The duty is fixed by law** and owed to **persons generally** (a right *in rem*). In contract the duty is created by agreement and owed to a party.
3. **The usual remedy is unliquidated damages.** The court fixes the amount. Injunction and restitution may also be available.
4. **It is not exclusively a breach of contract, trust or equity.** The same facts may be both a tort and a breach of contract (negligence by a doctor treating a paying patient).
5. **Liability may arise with or without fault.** Intention (assault), negligence (careless driving), or no fault at all (strict liability, vicarious liability, absolute liability).
6. **The law protects interests**: person, reputation, property, use of land, economic interests.
7. **The law is mostly uncodified** in India, and developed by judges. Statutes deal only with parts (Fatal Accidents Act, 1855; Limitation Act, 1963; Consumer Protection Act, 2019).

**3. Constituents of a tort.** (i) A **wrongful act or omission**; (ii) a **legal duty** owed to the plaintiff and its breach; (iii) **legal damage**. Some torts are actionable *per se* (trespass, libel, battery); others only on proof of damage (negligence, nuisance, slander). The old maxim *ubi jus ibi remedium* ("where there is a right there is a remedy") means that if the law recognises a right, it will also provide a remedy. See C-02 on *damnum sine injuria* and *injuria sine damno*.

**4. Pigeon-hole or general principle?** *Salmond* described the law as a list of torts: there is liability only if the wrong falls within one of the established **pigeon-holes** (trespass, nuisance, defamation and so on), so "the law of torts". *Winfield* and *Pollock* argued for a general principle: any harm caused without lawful justification is actionable, so "the law of tort". Courts have taken a middle course. The named torts are the starting point, but the law expands by analogy: *Donoghue v. Stevenson* (1932) created a general duty of care; *Wilkinson v. Downton* (1897) recognised intentional infliction of harm; and in India *M.C. Mehta* (1987) created absolute liability. The better view is that the list of torts is not closed.

**5. Tort distinguished from crime**

| Point | Tort | Crime |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of wrong | Wrong to a private person | Wrong to society |
| Duty | Owed to people generally, fixed by law, and enforced by the injured party | Owed to the State |
| Parties | Injured person v. wrongdoer | State v. accused (private complaint in some cases) |
| Purpose | Compensation | Punishment and deterrence |
| Remedy | Unliquidated damages; injunction | Imprisonment, fine, community service |
| Mental element | Not always needed | *Mens rea* usually needed |
| Compromise | The plaintiff may settle freely | Only compoundable offences |
| Law | Mostly common law | Codified (BNS, 2023) |

One act may be both: assault, defamation (BNS s. 356) and negligent driving causing death (BNS s. 106). The civil and criminal actions are separate.

**6. Tort distinguished from contract**

| Point | Tort | Contract |
|---|---|---|
| Source of duty | Fixed by law | Created by the parties |
| Duty owed to | Persons generally | A party to the contract |
| Privity | Not required | Required (with exceptions) |
| Damages | Unliquidated | Liquidated or unliquidated (ss. 73–74, Contract Act) |
| Motive | Sometimes relevant | Irrelevant |
| Capacity | A minor may be liable | A minor's contract is void (*Mohori Bibee*, 1903) |

**Breach of trust and quasi-contract.** A breach of trust is an equitable wrong with equitable remedies. A quasi-contract (Contract Act, ss. 68–72) imposes a duty to restore a benefit and is based on unjust enrichment, not on a wrong.

**7. The Indian setting.** Torts law is applied through (i) the common law as adapted (e.g. the Indian rule of absolute liability); (ii) statutes; and (iii) a **public-law remedy**: compensation under Arts. 32 and 226 for the violation of fundamental rights (*Rudul Sah*, 1983; *Nilabati Behera*, 1993). The Limitation Act, 1963 gives one year for most torts (false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, libel and slander).

**Conclusion.** Tort is the civil law of wrongs against persons in general. It is distinguished from crime by its aim (compensation) and from contract by the source of its duty (the law). The list of torts is growing, and in India the courts have extended it to hazardous industries and to constitutional wrongs by public bodies.

## C-02 · "The mere causing of actual loss to another is not necessarily a tort, but the mere wrong without any actual loss is a tort." Explain with decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q9 and Dec 2024 Q9 (both 20 marks; the same wording). Short forms: Dec 2023 Q7 (B-05, *injuria sine damno*), Dec 2024 Q4 (A-10), Dec 2022 Q5 (A-11, the principle behind *damnum sine injuria*). Related: B-04, B-05, A-12, A-13.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning of the two maxims 3 · *damnum sine injuria* with cases 6 · *injuria sine damno* with cases 6 · principle and test 3 · Indian cases and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The statement joins two Latin maxims. ***Damnum sine injuria*** means damage without legal injury: a person may suffer loss, but if no legal right of his has been violated he has no action. ***Injuria sine damno*** means legal injury without damage: a person's legal right has been violated, though he has suffered no actual loss, and he can sue. The first part of the statement is the first maxim; the second part is the second. Tort is concerned with the violation of **legal rights**, not with loss as such.

**1. *Damnum sine injuria*: loss without legal wrong.** *Damnum* is actual harm (loss of money, trade, comfort, health); *injuria* is the infringement of a legal right. If only a moral or social right is affected, or the defendant uses his own legal right, the loss is not actionable. The law tolerates such losses as the cost of competition and of the lawful use of property.

*Cases*
1. ***Gloucester Grammar School Case*** (1410). The defendant opened a rival school near the plaintiff's school, and the plaintiff's fees fell. It was held that the plaintiff could not sue: competition is lawful, and loss through it is *damnum sine injuria*.
2. ***Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co.*** (1889) 23 QBD 598; [1892] AC 25. A group of shipowners cut freight rates to drive a rival out of the China tea trade. The rival lost business. The House of Lords held the combination lawful: competition, even to ruin a rival, is not a tort if no unlawful means are used.
3. ***Allen v. Flood*** [1898] AC 1: a lawful act is not made wrong by a bad motive. ***Bradford Corporation v. Pickles*** [1895] AC 587. Pickles, who owned land above underground water that fed the Corporation's reservoir, dug a shaft in his own land and diverted the water, hoping to force the Corporation to buy him out. It was held that his motive was immaterial: he was using his own property lawfully.
4. ***Chasemore v. Richards*** (1859) 7 HLC 349 and ***Acton v. Blundell*** (1843): a landowner may draw underground percolating water from his own land even if neighbours' wells dry up.
5. ***Ushaben v. Bhagyalaxmi Chitra Mandir*** AIR 1978 Guj 13. The petitioners sought to stop the film *Jai Santoshi Maa* because it portrayed Hindu goddesses in a way that hurt their feelings. The High Court refused: hurt to religious sentiments, without violation of a legal right, is *damnum sine injuria*.

**2. *Injuria sine damno*: legal wrong without loss.** Here the plaintiff's legal right is violated. The law presumes damage, because rights must be protected whether or not loss follows. The plaintiff is entitled at least to **nominal damages**.

*Cases*
1. ***Ashby v. White*** (1703) 2 Ld Raym 938; 92 ER 126. Ashby was a qualified voter at Aylesbury. The returning officer, White, wrongly refused to accept his vote. The candidate Ashby wanted won anyway, so Ashby lost nothing. The Queen's Bench (Holt C.J. dissenting) refused a remedy, but the House of Lords adopted Holt's view that he could sue: the right to vote is a legal right and "every injury imports a damage, though it does not cost the party one farthing".
2. ***Marzetti v. Williams*** (1830) 1 B & Ad 415. A banker wrongly dishonoured the customer's cheque though he had funds. No special damage was shown. The customer recovered nominal damages for breach of the banker's duty.
3. ***Constantine v. Imperial Hotels Ltd.*** [1944] KB 693. A hotel refused to take in the West Indian cricketer Learie Constantine because of his colour. He found rooms elsewhere and could show no special loss, but the court awarded nominal damages (£5) for the breach of the innkeeper's duty.
4. **Torts actionable *per se*.** Trespass to land, assault, battery, false imprisonment and libel are actionable without proof of damage. A walk across another's field, causing no harm, is a trespass.
5. ***Bhim Singh v. State of J&K*** (1985) 4 SCC 677: a legislator was unlawfully detained to stop him from attending the Assembly; the Supreme Court awarded compensation for the violation of his rights.

**3. The underlying principle.** The two maxims state the same principle from both sides: **liability depends on violation of a legal right, not on the presence or absence of loss**. Two questions follow: (i) Was a legal right of the plaintiff invaded? (ii) Did he suffer loss? Only (i) decides liability; (ii) decides the amount.

| | *Damnum sine injuria* | *Injuria sine damno* |
|---|---|---|
| Legal right violated? | No | Yes |
| Loss? | Yes | Not necessary |
| Actionable? | No | Yes |
| Typical examples | Competition; lawful use of own land | Trespass; libel; wrongful refusal of vote |

**4. Torts needing proof of damage.** In **negligence**, **nuisance**, **deceit** and **slander** (English rule) damage is part of the tort. The plaintiff must prove both the breach of duty and damage. Even here the law asks first whether the damage was caused by the violation of a legal right or duty.

**5. Limits and modern developments**
1. The maxims are not exact. In negligence, a violation of a duty with no damage is not actionable (carelessness alone is no tort), so the second maxim does not cover every case.
2. Nominal damages are awarded in *injuria sine damno*, but a successful plaintiff in such a case may be refused costs if his claim is trivial; the court may give **contemptuous damages**.
3. The rule on motive (*Allen v. Flood*) has exceptions where malice is itself an element (malicious prosecution, conspiracy, defamation with qualified privilege defeated by malice).
4. In the **public-law** area, Indian courts award compensation for the violation of fundamental rights (*Rudul Sah*; *Nilabati Behera*).

**Conclusion.** The statement correctly says that loss is neither necessary nor sufficient. A person who suffers loss through the lawful acts of another has no tort remedy (*Mogul*; *Bradford v. Pickles*; *Ushaben*). A person whose right is violated has a remedy even without loss (*Ashby v. White*; *Marzetti*; *Constantine*; trespass and libel). The test is whether a legal right has been violated.

## C-03 · Discuss the mental element in tort: fault, intention, negligence, malice and motive

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Syllabus line 1 ("fault; mental element (malice, negligence, motive)"). Related: A-19, A-20, A-21, B-09, C-13.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** general rule and fault 3 · intention 3 · negligence and recklessness 3 · malice 4 · motive 4 · strict liability as exception 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Tort liability depends on what the defendant did, and in many torts on his state of mind. The syllabus names the mental elements: fault, malice, motive and negligence. The general principle is that a person is liable only if he is at **fault**, but the law also imposes liability without fault.

**1. Fault.** "Fault" means that the defendant's conduct is blameworthy: he acted with **intention**, or **negligence**, or **recklessness**. The law of torts generally follows the principle *no liability without fault* in torts such as trespass, defamation, negligence, deceit and malicious prosecution. The main exceptions are strict liability (*Rylands v. Fletcher*), absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*), vicarious liability and some statutory liabilities.

**2. Intention.** A person intends a consequence if he desires it or knows that it is certain to follow. Intention is the mental element of the **intentional torts**: assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass, conversion, deceit, malicious prosecution and conspiracy. Some torts require **intention to do the act** but not to cause harm: a person who walks on another's land believing it to be his own still commits trespass (*Basely v. Clarkson*, 1681).

**3. Negligence and recklessness.** *Negligence* is carelessness: failure to take the care that a reasonable man would take, where there is a duty to take care (*Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks*, 1856). There are two meanings: a **state of mind** (inadvertence) and an **independent tort** (breach of a duty of care). *Recklessness* is knowing that there is a risk and going on regardless; in deceit, a false statement made "recklessly, careless whether it be true or false" is fraud (*Derry v. Peek*, 1889). In tort, negligence is judged by an **objective** standard. See C-13.

**4. Motive.** Motive is the reason why a person acts, such as spite, gain or revenge. It is **generally irrelevant**: a lawful act does not become unlawful because of a bad motive, and an unlawful act is not excused by a good motive.
- ***Bradford Corporation v. Pickles*** [1895] AC 587: a landowner may intercept underground water in his own land, even if he does so to compel the Corporation to buy him out.
- ***Allen v. Flood*** [1898] AC 1: shipwrights dismissed by an employer at the instance of a trade-union official; the official's motive was held irrelevant since he had only used a lawful right (he had not threatened a breach of contract).
- ***Mogul Steamship*** (1892): competition to ruin a rival is lawful.
**Exceptions** where motive matters: (i) where malice is an **element** of the tort, such as malicious prosecution, conspiracy, injurious falsehood; (ii) where it defeats a **defence**: qualified privilege and fair comment in defamation are lost by malice; (iii) in **nuisance** a spiteful use of land can turn a reasonable act into a nuisance (*Christie v. Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316; *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v. Emmett* [1936] 2 KB 468); (iv) in **exemplary damages**, a bad motive aggravates the award.

**5. Malice.** The word has two meanings.
- ***Malice in law*** (legal malice): a wrongful act done **intentionally without just cause or excuse** (*Bromage v. Prosser*, 1825). It is implied from the act; it is not about spite.
- ***Malice in fact*** (actual malice, express malice): an **improper or ill motive**, such as spite, ill-will or a wish to injure. It must be proved.
Malice in fact matters in: malicious prosecution (with no reasonable and probable cause: *Hicks v. Faulkner*, 1878); defamation (to defeat qualified privilege: *Horrocks v. Lowe*, 1975); conspiracy to injure (*Quinn v. Leathem*, 1901); nuisance (*Christie v. Davey*).

| | Malice in law | Malice in fact |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Wrongful act, intentionally, without justification | Spite, ill-will, improper motive |
| Proof | Implied from the act | Must be proved |
| Where relevant | Most intentional torts | Malicious prosecution; defeating privilege; conspiracy |

**6. Where no mental element is needed (strict liability).** Liability may arise without proof of intention or negligence: (i) *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868); (ii) absolute liability for hazardous enterprises (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987); (iii) vicarious liability of the master; (iv) liability for keeping dangerous animals; (v) statutory duties; (vi) no-fault compensation under the Motor Vehicles Act and product liability under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. The modern trend, especially in India, is towards **stricter liability** for activities that create risk.

**7. Table: torts and mental element**

| Tort | Mental element |
|---|---|
| Trespass, battery, false imprisonment | Intentional (or negligent) act |
| Negligence | Carelessness (objective) |
| Deceit | Knowledge, or recklessness |
| Malicious prosecution | Malice and absence of reasonable cause |
| Defamation | Intention not needed in civil law; malice defeats qualified privilege |
| Nuisance | Not needed, but malice may help |
| *Rylands v. Fletcher*; absolute liability | None |

**Conclusion.** The general rule is liability for fault, mostly for intention or negligence, and motive does not usually matter. Malice is important only where the tort requires it or where it defeats a defence. The law of torts also recognises liability without fault where the risk is created by the defendant's enterprise or where the law imposes a duty in the public interest.

## C-04 · Discuss the rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*. Are there any exceptions to the rule? Discuss

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13 ("Discuss the rule laid down in Rylands vs Fletcher. Are there any exceptions to this rule? Discuss."). Related: A-24 (Dec 2024 Q5, strict liability), A-25–A-30, B-12, B-13, B-14.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** facts and decision 4 · essentials 5 · exceptions 6 · development in England 2 · Indian law (absolute liability) 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) laid down a rule of **strict liability** for damage caused by the escape of a dangerous thing from land. It makes a person answerable for the harm without proof of negligence. In India the Supreme Court has gone further, in *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987), with the rule of **absolute liability**.

**1. Facts.** Rylands employed independent contractors to build a reservoir on his land to supply water to his mill. Under the land there were old, disused mine shafts and passages that connected, unknown to Rylands, with the working mines of the neighbouring Fletcher. The contractors failed to seal them. When the reservoir was filled, the water burst through, flooded Fletcher's mine and caused heavy damage. Rylands was not negligent; the contractors were. Fletcher sued.

**2. Decision.** In the Exchequer Chamber, **Blackburn J.** (1866) stated the rule: "the person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is *prima facie* answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape." The House of Lords (1868; **Lord Cairns**) affirmed, adding that the defendant must have made a **non-natural use** of the land.

**3. Essentials**
1. **Something dangerous** ("likely to do mischief if it escapes") that the defendant **brings, collects and keeps** on his land: water, gas, electricity, fire, explosives, noxious fumes, rusty wire, vibrations, flagpoles.
2. **Non-natural use of land.** Not an ordinary or usual use but a special use that increases the danger to others (*Rickards v. Lothian* [1913] AC 263). Water in domestic pipes is a natural use; storing large quantities of water or chemicals for industry is non-natural. The test is relative to time and place.
3. **Escape.** The thing must escape from the defendant's land (or from a place under his control) to a place outside his control (*Read v. J. Lyons & Co.* [1947] AC 156: a worker was injured by a shell explosion inside a munitions factory; no escape, no liability).
4. **Damage** caused by the escape. *Cambridge Water Co. v. Eastern Counties Leather* [1994] 2 AC 264 requires that damage of that type be **reasonably foreseeable**, and *Transco v. Stockport MBC* [2003] UKHL 61 treated the rule as an offshoot of nuisance and confined it to unusual and extraordinarily dangerous uses.
Liability is not absolute: the plaintiff can recover only for the natural consequence of the escape, and the defendant has the exceptions below. Personal injury has been doubted as a recoverable head in England (*Transco*), but Indian courts allow it.

**4. Exceptions (defences)**
1. **Act of God** (*Nichols v. Marsland* (1876) 2 Ex D 1): an extraordinary rainfall burst artificial lakes made years earlier by the defendant's predecessor; the jury found it an Act of God and the defendant was not liable.
2. **Consent of the plaintiff**, express or implied, as when the source of danger is maintained for the **common benefit** of plaintiff and defendant (*Carstairs v. Taylor* (1871) LR 6 Ex 217: a rat gnawed a gutter of a building in which the defendant let a floor to the plaintiff; the water damaged the plaintiff's goods; no liability).
3. **Default of the plaintiff** (*Ponting v. Noakes* [1894] 2 QB 281: the plaintiff's horse put its head over the defendant's fence and ate yew leaves; he could not recover).
4. **Act of a stranger**, over whom the defendant had no control, which could not have been foreseen or prevented (*Box v. Jubb* (1879) 4 Ex D 76).
5. **Statutory authority** (*Green v. Chelsea Waterworks* (1894): the company, authorised by statute to lay mains, was not liable for a burst pipe when it was not negligent).
6. **Natural use of land**: ordinary use for the benefit of the occupier is not covered (*Rickards v. Lothian*).
7. **Common benefit** of both plaintiff and defendant, as in 2 above.

**5. Development in England.** *Read v. Lyons* (1947) insisted on escape and doubted the rule for personal injury. *Cambridge Water* (1994) required foreseeability of the kind of damage. *Transco* (2003) held that the rule remains but is restricted to unusual hazards; ordinary pipes are not within it. The rule is now of limited use in England.

**6. The Indian position: absolute liability**
- India took the rule early (*Madras Railway Co. v. Zamindar of Carvetnagaram* (1874), where the Privy Council adapted it to tanks in India †).
- **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India** (1987) 1 SCC 395. In December 1985 oleum gas leaked from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilisers Industries in Delhi; one person died and others were affected. The Court (Bhagwati C.J.) said that the *Rylands* rule belongs to the 19th century and is not suited to modern industry. It laid down that an enterprise engaged in a **hazardous or inherently dangerous activity** owes an **absolute and non-delegable duty** to the community; if harm results it is **absolutely liable** to compensate; the exceptions of *Rylands v. Fletcher* **do not apply**; and compensation should be related to the **size and capacity** of the enterprise so as to have a **deterrent** effect.
- **Bhopal** (1984): after the Union Carbide gas leak the Union took over claims by statute (Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985) and the Court approved a settlement of US $470 million in 1989.
- **Later cases:** *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) (polluter pays; Bichhri) and *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) (precautionary principle). Statutes: **Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991** (no-fault relief) and **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** (s. 17, no-fault liability).

| | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
|---|---|---|
| Source | *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) | *M.C. Mehta* (1987) |
| Scope | Non-natural use; escape | Hazardous industry; escape not essential |
| Exceptions | Seven | None |
| Damages | Actual loss | Deterrent, linked to the size of the enterprise |

**7. Criticism.** *Rylands* is hard to apply to modern industry. The absolute-liability rule has been criticised as vague ("hazardous" or "inherently dangerous" are not defined) and for placing heavy burdens on enterprises; but it fills a gap and has been backed by statute.

**Conclusion.** *Rylands v. Fletcher* made the keeper of a dangerous thing liable for its escape, subject to exceptions such as Act of God, consent, plaintiff's default, third-party act and statutory authority. In India, *M.C. Mehta* has replaced those exceptions with a stricter rule of absolute liability for hazardous enterprises, which better suits the risks of modern industry.

## C-05 · Who may sue and who may not be sued for a tort? Discuss the capacity of parties

**Asked as:** not yet set in this form. The nearest question is Dec 2025 Q12 (C-10, who may sue and be sued for a tort to and by another). Syllabus line 1 ("who may sue and who may not be sued"). Related: A-22, A-23, B-11.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** general rule 2 · minors and persons of unsound mind 4 · corporations, firms and trade unions 3 · spouses 2 · State and foreign sovereigns 4 · judges and officials 2 · standing to sue for another 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** In principle, every person can sue and be sued in tort. The exceptions are limited and fall into two groups: persons who lack **capacity** to sue or be sued (infants, persons of unsound mind) and persons who enjoy an **immunity** (foreign sovereigns, ambassadors, judges). A third group are persons who can sue for a wrong done to someone else (dependants, estates, guardians).

**1. Who may sue and be sued: minors (infants).**
- A minor may sue through a **next friend** (CPC, O. XXXII). He may be sued for his own torts, if old enough to understand the nature of the act (the question is one of fact; a very young child cannot be negligent in the same way as an adult, and the standard is that of a child of that age).
- A minor cannot be sued in **contract** under a form of action that amounts to enforcing a void contract. Where the claim in tort is really a way of enforcing a void contract (for example, an action in deceit against a minor who lied about his age to get goods), the English cases refuse it (*Jennings v. Rundall* (1799); *Burnard v. Haggis* (1863) †); in India the remedy is restitution under s. 33 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 †.
- Parents are **not** vicariously liable for a child's torts merely because of the relationship, but may be liable for their own negligence, such as leaving a dangerous thing within the child's reach.

**2. Persons of unsound mind.** May sue through a guardian. They are liable for their torts, unless the state of mind is such that the particular tort cannot be committed, as in torts requiring a particular intent (malice, deceit) (a textbook rule †). The mental state may be taken into account in damages.

**3. Corporations.** A company is a legal person (*Salomon v. Salomon*, 1897). It sues and is sued **in its own name**. It is liable for torts committed by its servants in the course of employment (vicarious liability) and by the persons who direct it, in the course of its business. For torts that require a state of mind (malicious prosecution, deceit), the state of mind of the directing officers is attributed to the company. A company cannot be defamed in matters of personal character but can sue for injury to its trading reputation. Statutory corporations can be sued for the torts of their servants, as the State is.

**4. Firms, clubs, trade unions.** A registered trade union can sue and be sued in its own name (Trade Unions Act, 1926, s. 13). In a partnership, each partner is liable, jointly and severally, for a wrongful act done by a partner in the ordinary course of the firm's business (Partnership Act, 1932, ss. 25–26). An unregistered club or association is not a legal person; its members may be sued in a representative capacity (CPC, O. I r. 8).

**5. Husband and wife.** The old common-law unity of husband and wife is gone. Each spouse can sue and be sued, including each other, and each is separately liable for his or her torts. A husband is not vicariously liable for the wife's torts only because of the marriage.

**6. The State (Government).**
- The Union and the States can sue and be sued (Art. 300).
- The old rule gave **sovereign immunity** for torts in the exercise of sovereign powers (*P&O Steam Navigation Co.*, 1861; *Kasturilal*, 1965), but the courts now confine it (*Vidhyawati*, 1962; *Nagendra Rao*, 1994) and give public-law compensation for the violation of fundamental rights (*Nilabati Behera*, 1993). A suit needs **two months' notice** under CPC s. 80. See C-11.
- **Public officers** are protected for acts done in good faith in discharge of official duties by statute (e.g. CPC s. 80; BNSS s. 218 for prosecution †).

**7. Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and international bodies.** They cannot be sued in Indian courts without the consent of the Central Government (CPC s. 86), and diplomats enjoy immunity under the Diplomatic Relations (Vienna Convention) Act, 1972. An alien enemy cannot sue without a licence but can be sued.

**8. Judges and magistrates.** Not liable for judicial acts done in good faith, in the discharge of their duty, even if erroneous, under the Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850 (s. 1) and the common law (*Anowar Hussain v. Ajoy Kumar Mukherjee* AIR 1965 SC 1651). The protection is meant to preserve judicial independence.

**9. Convicts and insolvents.** A convict can sue for civil wrongs done to him. In insolvency, the right to sue for injury to property vests in the official assignee or receiver; purely personal claims stay with the debtor (†).

**10. Who may sue for a wrong done to another.**
- **Dependants** (spouse, parent, child) under the **Fatal Accidents Act, 1855** (s. 1A) for death caused by a wrongful act, neglect or default.
- **Legal representatives** under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, s. 166 and for the estate under the **Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306**, which says that causes of action survive except for defamation, assault and other personal injuries not causing death (*actio personalis moritur cum persona*).
- **Next friend or guardian** for a minor or a person of unsound mind.
- **Master** for loss of services of a servant (*per quod servitium amisit*); rarely used in India.
- **Consumer associations**, class complainants, and the Government under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, s. 35.
- **Public-interest petitioners** for a class of persons who cannot approach the court (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984).

**11. Summary table**

| Party | Can sue | Can be sued |
|---|---|---|
| Minor | Yes (next friend) | Yes, if able to appreciate the wrong |
| Person of unsound mind | Yes (guardian) | Yes, usually |
| Company | Yes | Yes |
| Registered trade union | Yes | Yes |
| Husband/wife | Yes, also against each other | Yes |
| State | Yes | Yes, with exceptions; notice under CPC s. 80 |
| Foreign sovereign / ambassador | Yes | No, without consent |
| Judge (judicial act) | Yes | No, in good faith |

**Conclusion.** The law of torts allows nearly everyone to sue and be sued. Exceptions rest on public policy (judges, diplomats, foreign States) or on the person's capacity (minors, persons of unsound mind). The State is liable, but subject to procedural protections. A person who is not liable himself may still have another made liable on his behalf (Unit 3).

## C-06 · Remedies in tort; how the right of action ends; and liability for a foreign tort

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 10 marks: Dec 2022 Q6 (B-07, "When a right to action for a tort comes to an end or discharged?") and Dec 2022 Q8 (B-08, "What are necessary conditions of liability for foreign tort?"); at 4 marks: Dec 2023 Q5 (A-14, unliquidated damages). Related: A-14–A-18, B-10.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** judicial remedies 6 · extra-judicial remedies 3 · discharge of liability 6 · foreign tort 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** A tort gives the injured person a remedy. Remedies are of two kinds: those given by a **court** (damages, injunction, restitution) and those the injured person takes **himself** (self-help). A right of action may later come to an end. A court in India may also be asked to try a tort that happened abroad.

**1. Judicial remedies**

*(a) Damages.* The usual remedy. They are **unliquidated**: not fixed in advance, so the court assesses them from the facts: the pain and suffering, loss of earnings, harm to reputation and so on (compare liquidated damages in contract, Contract Act, s. 74).
- **Nominal**: a small sum for violation of a right without loss (*Ashby v. White*).
- **Contemptuous**: a very small coin, where the plaintiff has a right but his claim is unworthy; costs may be refused.
- **General**: loss which the law presumes (reputation) or which is hard to measure.
- **Special**: actual quantifiable loss (medical bills, lost wages); must be pleaded and proved.
- **Aggravated**: where the defendant's conduct (malice, insolence) increased the injury to feelings; compensatory.
- **Exemplary (punitive)**: to punish and deter. *Rookes v. Barnard* [1964] AC 1129 allowed them only for (i) oppressive, arbitrary or unconstitutional acts by government servants, (ii) conduct calculated to make a profit exceeding the compensation, and (iii) where a statute authorises them. In India the Supreme Court awards exemplary compensation as a public-law remedy against the State (*Nilabati Behera*, 1993).
- **Principles of assessment** in personal injury and death cases: the multiplier method with heads such as loss of income, future prospects, medical expenses, loss of consortium and funeral expenses (*National Insurance Co. v. Pranay Sethi*, (2017) 16 SCC 680; Motor Vehicles Act, ss. 166 and 164 as amended).
- **Mitigation**: the plaintiff must take reasonable steps to reduce his loss.

*(b) Injunction.* An order to stop (prohibitory) or to undo (mandatory) a wrong. **Temporary** (interim) injunctions pending trial and **perpetual** injunctions after trial are granted under the Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 36–42, and CPC O. XXXIX. It is the main remedy for continuing wrongs: nuisance, trespass, and defamation (rarely before trial: *Bonnard v. Perryman*). The court may award damages in place of an injunction if it would be oppressive (*Shelfer* rule, *Coventry v. Lawrence*).

*(c) Specific restitution of property.* An order to restore specific property: recovery of possession of immovable property (Specific Relief Act, ss. 5–6; a person dispossessed may sue within six months under s. 6 without proving title) and of movable property (s. 7), the modern form of *detinue*.

**2. Extra-judicial remedies (self-help).** The injured person may, within limits:
1. **Defend** his person or property with reasonable force (*Bird v. Holbrook*: no spring-guns).
2. **Re-enter** land from which he has been wrongfully excluded, peacefully and with reasonable force only (in India the Specific Relief Act, s. 6 and the criminal law restrict force).
3. **Recapture** his goods, if he can do so peaceably.
4. **Abate** a nuisance by removing it, after notice, using no more force than is necessary (*Lemmon v. Webb* [1895] AC 1).
5. **Expel a trespasser** with reasonable force.
6. **Distrain damage feasant**: seize animals or chattels that are damaging his land, and hold them until compensation is paid.
Self-help is lawful only if it is reasonable, and the law prefers recourse to the courts.

**3. How a right of action in tort comes to an end (discharge of liability)**
1. **Death of either party: *actio personalis moritur cum persona*** ("a personal action dies with the person"). At common law a right to sue in tort ended when the plaintiff or the wrongdoer died. In India the **Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306** provides that all demands and rights to prosecute or defend any action survive to and against executors and administrators, **except** causes of action for **defamation, assault or other personal injuries not causing the death of the party**, and cases where the relief sought could not be enjoyed or would be nugatory after the death. The dependants' own claim for wrongful death is preserved by the **Fatal Accidents Act, 1855**, and legal representatives can claim under the Motor Vehicles Act, s. 166. In England the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1934 has the same effect.
2. **Waiver, acquiescence or election.** A person who with full knowledge elects to affirm the act, or who allows it to go on without protest, cannot later sue.
3. **Accord and satisfaction.** The injured party agrees to accept something in place of his right and the agreement is carried out; the right is discharged.
4. **Release.** A release for consideration, or under seal, discharges the claim. A release of one joint tortfeasor releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor*, 1614; Unit 3), though a covenant not to sue one does not.
5. **Judgment.** When judgment is given, the cause of action merges in the judgment. The same claim cannot be tried again (*res judicata*, CPC s. 11).
6. **Limitation.** The remedy is barred after the period fixed by the **Limitation Act, 1963**: one year for compensation for acts done under a statute, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, libel and slander (Arts. 72–76), with the time running from the date given in each Article ‡; the residuary Art. 113 gives three years †; for a continuing wrong, s. 22 gives a fresh period at every moment †; time does not run against a person under disability (s. 6) and the period may be extended for fraud or mistake (s. 17) †.
7. **Statutory bars and special rules**, such as the notice under CPC s. 80 for suits against the Government, and protection for acts done in good faith in discharge of official duties.

**4. Foreign torts**

A **foreign tort** is a wrong committed in another country but sued on in India. Which law applies? The English rule, stated by **Willes J. in *Phillips v. Eyre*** (1870) LR 6 QB 1, is that a tort committed abroad is actionable in the forum if:
1. **The wrong is one which would have been actionable as a tort if committed in the forum** (here, in India). This is *lex fori*. It is not enough that the act was wrong where it was done.
2. **The act must not have been justifiable by the law of the place where it was done** (*lex loci delicti*). It is enough that the act was *actionable civilly* there (not merely a crime or a breach of regulation) and that no defence applies there.
Thus there is **double actionability** (the "double test"). In ***Boys v. Chaplin*** [1971] AC 356 two English servicemen were in Malta, and one injured the other in a motor accident. Maltese law allowed only special damages, while English law allowed damages for pain and suffering. The House of Lords applied English law, because both parties were English and their only connection with Malta was temporary, so a "flexible exception" to the double test was recognised.
**Further conditions:** the court must have **jurisdiction** over the defendant (CPC ss. 19–20: place where the defendant resides or the cause of action arises); the claim must not be *penal* or against **public policy**; the foreign law must be **proved as a fact**.
**In England**, the double-actionability rule was abolished by Part III of the **Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1995**, replaced by the law of the place where the tort occurred, with some flexibility. India has no statute; the Indian courts have followed the English common-law rule, and I could not find a leading Supreme Court decision on it (†).

**Conclusion.** Damages are the central remedy for a tort, with injunction and restitution for continuing wrongs, and the law allows limited self-help. A right of action ends by death (in some cases), waiver, release, accord and satisfaction, judgment or limitation. A foreign tort is actionable in India if it would be a tort in India and was actionable civilly in the place where it was done.

## C-07 · "It is the *consent* which is the basis of the doctrine of *volenti non fit injuria*, and not knowledge of the risk." Elucidate with decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q9 (20 marks; the quoted words). Short forms: Dec 2025 Q6 (B-15, "Harm suffered voluntarily is not actionable but mere knowledge of the risk is not enough"). Related: A-31–A-33, B-16.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and rationale 3 · essentials 4 · consent v. knowledge with cases 6 · exceptions 4 · volenti v. contributory negligence and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** *Volenti non fit injuria* means "to a willing person no injury is done". A person who has voluntarily agreed to run the risk of harm cannot complain when it happens. The statement in the question is the settled meaning of the maxim: the defence applies only where the plaintiff has **consented**, expressly or by implication, to take the risk. **Knowledge** of the danger (*scienti*) is not enough. It is *volenti*, not *scienti*.

**1. Meaning and basis.** The defence rests on the ideas of **autonomy** and **fair play**: a person who freely chooses to take a risk should bear it. It has two forms: (i) consent to an act that would otherwise be a tort (a medical operation, a boxing match, a tackle in a game); (ii) consent to the risk of harm from another's negligence or from a dangerous situation. It is a **complete defence**, and the burden of proof is on the defendant.

**2. Essentials**
1. **Knowledge** of the risk, of its nature and extent.
2. **Free and voluntary assent** to run it: not mere acquiescence, and not agreement forced by duty, necessity or ignorance.
3. The harm must be **within the scope** of the consent.
4. The plaintiff must have **capacity** (a child or a person of unsound mind cannot consent), and the consent must not be **obtained by fraud** (*R v. Williams* [1923] 1 KB 340).

**3. Consent, not mere knowledge: the cases**

*(a) **Smith v. Baker & Sons*** [1891] AC 325. Smith, a workman, was drilling rock in a cutting. A crane swung heavy stones over his head. He knew of the danger and had complained. A stone fell and hurt him. The employer pleaded *volenti*. The House of Lords held that it failed. **Lord Herschell** and others explained that knowledge of a danger is not the same as agreement to take the risk: the question is whether the plaintiff freely and voluntarily, with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk, impliedly agreed to incur it. Smith continued working because he needed his wages, not because he agreed to take the employer's negligence on himself.

*(b) **Dann v. Hamilton*** [1939] 1 KB 509. The plaintiff accepted a lift from a driver whom she knew had been drinking. He crashed; she was injured. **Asquith J.** held that the defence failed: she knew the risk, but she did not agree to waive her right to claim if the driver was negligent. The law has since taken the same view in road cases; compulsory insurance (Motor Vehicles Act, s. 149 in India) supports it.

*(c) **Nettleship v. Weston*** [1971] 2 QB 691. A friend agreed to help the defendant learn to drive after checking her insurance. On the third lesson she crashed. The Court of Appeal held that **volenti** did not apply, because there was no agreement to waive the right to claim for negligence, and that the learner must reach the standard of a competent driver. The instructor's damages were reduced by 50% for contributory negligence ‡.

*(d) **I.C.I. Ltd. v. Shatwell*** [1965] AC 656. Two shot-firers deliberately disobeyed safety rules and tested a detonator without taking cover. One was injured and sued the employer, who was vicariously liable for the other. The House of Lords held that *volenti* applied: the plaintiff had himself taken part in the breach and had agreed to the risk.

*(e) **Hall v. Brooklands Auto-Racing Club*** [1933] 1 KB 205. A spectator at a motor race was injured when a car left the track. The Court of Appeal held that the spectator accepted the inherent risks of the sport and that the club was not negligent.

*(f) **Wooldridge v. Sumner*** [1963] 2 QB 43 and ***Condon v. Basi*** [1985] 1 WLR 866 (sport). A spectator takes the risk of an error of judgment in the heat of the game, but not of reckless disregard for safety; a player who commits a foul tackle is liable.

*(g) **Thomas v. Quartermaine*** (1887) 18 QBD 685 †. A brewery worker fell into a vat of boiling liquid while working in a place whose danger he knew; the older case usually set against *Smith v. Baker*. It is cited for the point that the maxim applies where the plaintiff has really accepted the risk; after *Smith v. Baker*, bare knowledge is not enough.

**4. Knowledge compared with consent**

| Knowledge (*scienti*) | Consent (*volenti*) |
|---|---|
| The plaintiff knows of the risk | The plaintiff agrees to run it |
| Passive awareness | Active, free and voluntary assent |
| May exist under pressure (a job, a duty) | Must be free of compulsion |
| Evidence of consent but not proof of it | The basis of the defence |

**5. Exceptions: where the defence fails**
1. **Rescue cases.** A rescuer who is hurt is not *volenti*, since he acts under a moral or social duty ("danger invites rescue": Cardozo J.). *Haynes v. Harwood* [1935] 1 KB 146 (a police constable stopped runaway horses in a crowded street and was injured; the owner's servant had left them unattended); *Baker v. T.E. Hopkins & Son* [1959] 1 WLR 966 (a doctor who went down a well to save two workmen overcome by fumes was killed; his dependants recovered). A rescuer who acts rashly may bear part of the blame.
2. **Employment.** A worker who continues in dangerous employment out of need is not *volenti* (*Smith v. Baker*; *Bowater v. Rowley Regis Corporation* [1944] KB 476: a carter ordered to drive a horse known to be vicious was not *volenti*).
3. **Statutory duties and unfair exclusions.** A notice or term excluding liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence is ineffective (England: Unfair Contract Terms Act, 1977, s. 2(1); in India the Contract Act, s. 23 points the same way †).
4. **Medical treatment without informed consent** is a battery (*Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda* (2008) 2 SCC 1 †).
5. **Consent by fraud or by a person without capacity** is no consent.
6. **Illegal acts**: consent cannot excuse an act that the law forbids for public protection.

**6. *Volenti* and contributory negligence**

| | *Volenti* | Contributory negligence |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Complete defence | Reduces damages |
| Basis | Agreement to run the risk | Failure to take care for own safety |
| Present use | Narrow, only where consent is clear | Usual route (*Dann*; *Nettleship*) |

**7. Indian law.** The Indian courts accept the maxim and apply it narrowly, mostly to sport, employment and medical procedures. In motor-accident claims they apportion blame instead. *Samira Kohli* requires informed consent in medical practice.

**Conclusion.** The statement correctly states the law. A plaintiff fails under *volenti* only if he agreed, freely and with knowledge, to take the risk. Mere knowledge, as in *Smith v. Baker*, *Dann v. Hamilton* and the rescue cases, is not enough. Courts prefer contributory negligence, which shares the loss, to the harsh all-or-nothing defence.

## C-08 · Discuss the defences of necessity, private defence and statutory authority in an action for tort

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Syllabus line 2 ("necessity; statutory authority"). Related: A-34, A-35, A-38, B-17, B-19.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and kinds of defence 2 · necessity 6 · private defence 4 · statutory authority 6 · Indian provisions and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** A defendant who is proved to have done a wrongful act may escape liability if the law regards the act as **justified**. The syllabus names necessity and statutory authority; private defence is closely related. Each makes the act lawful in the circumstances; none is an excuse for carelessness.

**1. Necessity**

*Meaning.* An act which would otherwise be a tort is justified if it was done to avoid a **greater harm** to the defendant, to another person or to the public, and the harm avoided was greater than the harm caused. It must be **reasonable** and **proportionate**.

*Kinds*
- **Private necessity**: to protect the defendant's own property or another's. ***Cope v. Sharpe (No. 2)*** [1912] 1 KB 496: the defendant entered the plaintiff's land and set fire to heather to stop the spread of a fire to the adjoining property that he was protecting; he was held not liable, because the act was necessary and reasonable. ***Mouse's Case*** (1608) 12 Co Rep 63: when a ferry was in danger in a storm, a casket was thrown overboard to save the passengers' lives; the thrower was held not liable to the owner of the casket.
- **Public necessity**: to protect the community: pulling down a house to stop the spread of fire, killing a rabid dog.
- **Medical necessity**: treating an unconscious patient without consent where treatment is urgent and in his best interests (*Re F* [1990] 2 AC 1).

*Limits*
- ***Southwark LBC v. Williams*** [1971] Ch 734. Homeless people occupied empty council houses. The Court of Appeal refused the plea of necessity: if it were allowed, "no man's house is safe".
- ***Esso Petroleum Co. v. Southport Corporation*** [1956] AC 218 †: a tanker ran aground and the captain discharged oil to save ship and crew; the oil fouled the foreshore. The owners were held not liable because the stranding was not due to negligence. The case is cited for the limit that necessity is no answer where the danger was caused by the defendant's own fault.
- The harm avoided must be **greater**. A person who destroys another's property to save his own goods of lesser value is liable.
- The plea cannot be used where the law provides another way.

*Indian law.* The IPC s. 81 (now **BNS s. 19** †) provides that an act likely to cause harm, done without criminal intention to prevent other harm, is not an offence if the harm avoided is greater. Courts use the principle in civil cases.

**2. Private defence (self-help)**
A person may use **reasonable force** to defend his own person or property, or that of another, against a wrongful attack.
- The force must be **proportionate**; deadly force to protect property is not allowed. ***Bird v. Holbrook*** (1828): a spring-gun set to protect a walled garden injured a trespasser; the owner was liable.
- A **mistaken but reasonable** belief in an attack may be enough (*Ashley v. Chief Constable of Sussex* [2008] UKHL 25 †).
- **Indian law**: the right of private defence of body and property is in IPC ss. 96–106, now **BNS ss. 34–44** †. The right extends to causing death in specified cases (e.g. a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt). Necessity differs from private defence: necessity is a response to danger from natural forces or a third party, which may injure an **innocent** person; private defence is a response to a **wrongful attack** by the person against whom force is used.

**3. Statutory authority**

*Rule.* An act **authorised by a statute** is not a tort even if it causes damage which would otherwise be actionable. The injured party has such a remedy as the statute provides, and none otherwise.

*Kinds*
| Kind | Meaning | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute (mandatory)** | The statute orders the act or makes harm inevitable | Complete, provided no negligence |
| **Conditional (permissive)** | The statute only permits the act, leaving the manner to the authority | Only if the authority used reasonable care and the way causing least harm |

*Cases*
- ***Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway*** (1860) 5 H & N 679: sparks from a locomotive set fire to the plaintiff's woods. The company was authorised to run engines and had taken every precaution; no liability.
- ***Hammersmith & City Railway v. Brand*** (1869) LR 4 HL 171: vibration from trains lowered the value of nearby houses. No action, since the statute authorised the railway and gave no right to compensation.
- ***Metropolitan Asylum District v. Hill*** (1881) 6 App Cas 193: managers built a smallpox hospital in a populated district under a general power. The Lords held the power was permissive, so the nuisance was not authorised; an injunction was granted.
- ***Geddis v. Proprietors of Bann Reservoir*** (1878) 3 App Cas 430: statutory authority is no defence for **negligence**.
- ***Allen v. Gulf Oil Refining*** [1981] AC 1001: statute authorised the building of a refinery; the nuisance was the inevitable result and not actionable.
- **Indian cases.** ***Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand*** AIR 1980 SC 1622: a statutory body cannot plead lack of funds to avoid its duty to remove a nuisance. ***Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti*** AIR 1966 SC 1750: the Corporation was liable when its clock tower collapsed, having failed to maintain it.

*Conditions.* (i) The act is **authorised** by the statute; (ii) it is done within the authority; (iii) the authority has used **due care** and chosen the least harmful method (for permissive powers); (iv) the statute may or may not give compensation.

*Constitutional limit.* A statute that violates a fundamental right is void (Art. 13), so statutory authority is no defence to a claim for violation of such a right.

**Conclusion.** Necessity, private defence and statutory authority are defences of justification. Necessity requires a greater harm avoided and a reasonable response; private defence permits proportionate force against a wrongful attack; statutory authority protects acts the legislature has authorised, but not negligence and not nuisance that could be avoided. Courts apply each strictly, and the burden is on the defendant.

## C-09 · Distinguish inevitable accident from Act of God. Discuss both as defences in an action for tort

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 10 marks: Dec 2024 Q6 (B-18, "Explain the distinction between inevitable accident and act of god") and at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q5 (A-36, "Define inevitable accident"). Related: A-36, A-37, B-18.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** inevitable accident: meaning, essentials, cases 7 · Act of God: meaning, essentials, cases 7 · distinction 4 · Indian note and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Both defences rest on the principle that a person is not liable for harm that he could not avoid by reasonable care. *Inevitable accident* is the wider idea; *Act of God* is a special kind of it.

**1. Inevitable accident**

*Meaning.* An accident which **could not have been avoided by the exercise of ordinary care, caution and skill** (Stephen's definition, adopted in the cases). "Inevitable" does not mean absolutely unavoidable; it means unavoidable by a reasonable person in the defendant's position. The defence shows that the defendant was **not at fault**. It is a complete defence to torts which require intention or negligence (trespass, negligence), and it is not a defence to strict liability.

*Essentials*
1. The accident was not caused by the defendant's intention or negligence.
2. It could not have been prevented by reasonable care.
3. It arose without his fault, from a cause which may be natural or human.

*Cases*
1. ***Stanley v. Powell*** [1891] 1 QB 86. A member of a shooting party fired at a pheasant. The bullet glanced off a tree and wounded a man employed to carry cartridges. It was held that the defendant was not liable: the accident was inevitable and there was no negligence. Trespass to the person requires intention or negligence.
2. ***Brown v. Kendall*** (1850) 60 Mass 292. Two dogs were fighting. The defendant tried to separate them by beating them with a stick and in raising it hit the plaintiff in the eye. The court held that he was not liable if he used ordinary care in a lawful act; the plaintiff must show a lack of due care.
3. ***Holmes v. Mather*** (1875) LR 10 Ex 261. The defendant's horses bolted after being frightened by a barking dog; the driver tried to stop them but they knocked down the plaintiff. No liability: the driver did what a reasonable man would do.
4. **Sudden illness**: *Mansfield v. Weetabix* [1998] 1 WLR 1263: a driver who was unaware that he was hypoglycaemic was not negligent. But a driver who knows that he is liable to blackouts and drives anyway is not within the defence.

**2. Act of God**

*Meaning.* An **operation of natural forces**, such as a storm, flood or earthquake, which is **so extraordinary that no human foresight or prudence could reasonably be expected to anticipate or guard against it**, and in which **human agency plays no part**.

*Essentials*
1. It is a **natural event** (not caused by man).
2. It is **extraordinary** and **unforeseeable**; ordinary storms or floods known in that area are not an Act of God.
3. It **could not have been prevented** by reasonable care.

*Cases*
1. ***Nichols v. Marsland*** (1876) 2 Ex D 1. The defendant had made artificial lakes on his land. An unprecedented storm broke the banks and the water carried away four bridges of the plaintiff. The jury found it an Act of God. The Court of Appeal upheld the finding: the defendant was not liable even under *Rylands v. Fletcher*.
2. ***Greenock Corporation v. Caledonian Railway*** [1917] AC 556 †. The Corporation, in making a paddling pool, altered the course of a stream; after an extraordinary rainfall the water flooded the railway's land. The Lords held that the damage was not due to an Act of God, since the Corporation had interfered with the natural watercourse and should have provided for even heavy rainfall.
3. The earthquake, cyclone and flood of exceptional severity may qualify; a wall that collapses in heavy rain (an ordinary monsoon) is a matter of negligence.

*Where Act of God is a defence*: negligence; nuisance (in a limited way); *Rylands v. Fletcher* (one of the exceptions); a common carrier's liability (common carrier is liable as an insurer except for Act of God and the enemies of the State; Carriers Act, 1865 †). It is **not** a defence under the Indian rule of **absolute liability** (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987).

**3. Distinction**

| Point | Inevitable accident | Act of God |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | Wider (genus) | Narrower (species) |
| Cause | Human or natural: the act of a person, a hidden defect, a sudden fit | Natural forces only; no human agency |
| Test | Not avoidable by ordinary care | Extraordinary natural event; not foreseeable by any human foresight |
| Standard | Reasonable man | Beyond human foresight |
| Examples | *Stanley v. Powell*; *Holmes v. Mather* | *Nichols v. Marsland*; a rare cyclone |
| Under *Rylands* | Not a recognised exception as such | A recognised exception |
| Under absolute liability | No defence | No defence |

**4. Related defences.** Inevitable accident is related to the defence of *no negligence*, and it differs from *volenti* (consent) and *necessity* (justification). It is an excuse, not a justification.

**5. Indian note.** The Indian courts apply the English principles. A claim that heavy rain collapsed a wall will fail unless the rainfall is shown to be extraordinary for that place. Carriers Act, 1865 (s. 9 relieves the owner from proving the carrier's negligence) and the Railways Act, 1989 deal with a carrier's liability and its exceptions †.

**Conclusion.** An inevitable accident is one that reasonable care could not have prevented; an Act of God is such an accident that is caused wholly by extraordinary natural forces. Both exclude liability for fault-based torts. Act of God is an exception to *Rylands v. Fletcher*, but neither excuses the owner of a hazardous enterprise in India, who is absolutely liable.
