## C-17 · Define nuisance. Distinguish public from private nuisance, and discuss the essentials of private nuisance, the defences and the remedies

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 10 marks: Dec 2025 Q8 (B-38, "What are the defences open in an action based on nuisance?"); at 4 marks: Dec 2024 Q1 (A-85, "What do you understand by Nuisance?"). Related: A-85–A-92, B-37–B-39.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** definition and kinds 4 · essentials and factors with cases 6 · Indian cases 2 · defences 5 · remedies and comparison 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Nuisance protects a person's **use and enjoyment of land** from unreasonable interference. **Winfield** defines it as an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over, or in connection with it. It is different from trespass, which protects *possession* and is a direct interference, and from negligence, which needs carelessness.

**1. Kinds: public and private nuisance**

| | Public nuisance | Private nuisance |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | An act or omission that materially affects the **reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of people** (BNS s. 270 †). *A-G v. PYA Quarries* [1957] 2 QB 169: so widespread that it is not reasonable to expect one person to sue | Unlawful interference with a person's **use or enjoyment of his land** |
| Nature | A **crime**, and a tort for a person who suffers **special damage** | A **tort** only |
| Who sues | The Advocate-General for the public; a private person only if he shows **special damage** different in kind from the rest of the public (*Rose v. Miles* (1815); *Benjamin v. Storr* (1874)). In India CPC **s. 91** allows a suit by the Advocate-General or by two or more persons with the court's leave | The occupier (or owner) of the land affected: *Hunter v. Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655 |
| Examples | Obstructing a highway; a dangerous quarry; selling unfit food; smoke affecting a neighbourhood | Smell, noise, smoke or vibration from a neighbour's land; overhanging branches |
| Remedy | Prosecution; conditional order for removal of the nuisance (CrPC s. 133, now BNSS s. 152 †); civil suit | Damages, injunction, abatement |

**2. Essentials of private nuisance**
1. **Unreasonable interference.** The test is what is reasonable between neighbours: "give and take, live and let live" (*Bamford v. Turnley* (1862)).
2. **With the plaintiff's use or enjoyment of land.** Interference may be (a) physical damage to property (fumes that kill trees, flooding); (b) substantial interference with comfort or convenience (noise, smell, vibration, smoke); (c) interference with an easement (such as a right to light).
3. **Damage**, actual and substantial: more than the ordinary person is expected to put up with.
4. **Standing:** only a person with a right in the land can sue (*Hunter v. Canary Wharf*).

**Factors the court weighs**

| Factor | Case |
|---|---|
| **Locality** | *Sturges v. Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852: a confectioner's noisy machinery disturbed a doctor's new consulting room; Thesiger L.J.: "what would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey". For **physical damage** locality does not matter: *St Helen's Smelting Co. v. Tipping* (1865) 11 HLC 642 |
| **Degree, duration, time** | *Halsey v. Esso Petroleum* [1961] 1 WLR 683 (night noise and smuts from an oil depot) |
| **Sensitivity of the plaintiff** | *Robinson v. Kilvert* (1889) 41 Ch D 88: heat from a cellar damaged a neighbour's unusually sensitive brown paper; no nuisance |
| **Malice** | *Christie v. Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316: a neighbour deliberately banged on the wall to annoy a music teacher; nuisance |
| **Utility** | Not a defence: *Adams v. Ursell* [1913] 1 Ch 269 (a fried-fish shop in a good residential area) |
| **Liability for others' nuisance** | *Sedleigh-Denfield v. O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880: an occupier who knows of a nuisance made by a trespasser and fails to stop it is liable |

**3. Indian cases**
- ***Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand*** AIR 1980 SC 1622. Residents suffered from open drains and the stench of an alcohol plant; the Magistrate ordered the municipality to build drains under CrPC s. 133. The Supreme Court (Krishna Iyer J.) upheld the order: a statutory body **cannot plead lack of funds** to avoid its duty.
- ***Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Association*** (2000) 7 SCC 282: noise from loudspeakers of a place of worship is a nuisance; no religion requires disturbing others by noise, and Art. 25 is subject to public order and health.
- ***In re: Noise Pollution (V)*** (2005) 5 SCC 733 †: the right to a quiet environment and to sleep is part of Art. 21; loudspeakers restricted at night; the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 †.
- Statutes: Water Act, 1974; Air Act, 1981; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.

**4. Defences (Dec 2025 B8)**

*Valid*
1. **Prescription.** A right to do the act can be acquired as an easement by 20 years' uninterrupted use as of right (Indian Easements Act, 1882, s. 15; *Sturges v. Bridgman*). The 20 years run from the time the plaintiff could first sue.
2. **Statutory authority** (*Allen v. Gulf Oil* [1981] AC 1001; see C-08).
3. **Consent** of the plaintiff.
4. **Act of a stranger or Act of God**, where the defendant could not have prevented it.
5. **Reasonable user** of land in the locality.
6. **Plaintiff's abnormal sensitivity** (*Robinson v. Kilvert*).

*Not defences*
1. **Coming to the nuisance:** *Bliss v. Hall* (1838); *Miller v. Jackson* [1977] QB 966 (a cricket club); *Coventry v. Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13.
2. **Public benefit** (*Adams v. Ursell*), though it may affect the remedy (damages in place of an injunction).
3. **Reasonable care**: nuisance looks at the result, not at the care taken.
4. **That others also contribute** (*Lambton v. Mellish* [1894] 3 Ch 163: two noisy roundabouts; each liable).
5. **Planning permission** by itself.

**5. Remedies.** *Damages*; *injunction* (prohibitory or mandatory; the court may give damages in place of an injunction if it would be oppressive: *Shelfer v. City of London Electric Lighting* [1895] 1 Ch 287, applied flexibly in *Coventry v. Lawrence*); *abatement*, a self-help remedy: the occupier may remove the nuisance, after notice (except in an emergency), using no more force than needed (*Lemmon v. Webb* [1895] AC 1).

**6. Distinctions**

| | Nuisance | Trespass to land | Negligence | *Rylands v. Fletcher* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interest | Use and enjoyment of land | Possession of land | Person, property | Land |
| Interference | Indirect | Direct | Breach of duty | Escape of a dangerous thing |
| Damage | Needed (private nuisance) | Not needed | Needed | Needed |
| Fault | Not essential | Not essential | Essential | Not essential |

**Conclusion.** Nuisance requires an unreasonable interference with the use or enjoyment of land that causes substantial damage. Public nuisance is both a crime and, with special damage, a tort. The defences are narrow: prescription, statutory authority, consent and reasonable user. Utility, and coming to the nuisance, do not excuse. The Indian courts increasingly use nuisance ideas, with Art. 21, to protect the environment.

## C-18 · What is trespass? Discuss trespass to the person, to land and to goods

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q2 (A-95, trespass *ab initio*) and Dec 2022 Q3 (A-98, ingredients of false imprisonment). Related: A-93–A-100, B-40–B-44.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and character of trespass 3 · person (assault, battery, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution) 7 · land (essentials, defences, *ab initio*) 6 · goods 2 · remedies and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** In the old law, **trespass** was a direct and forcible injury to a person, land or goods, for which the writ of *trespass* lay. It is distinguished from **trespass on the case** (the source of nuisance, negligence and deceit), which concerned indirect harm. Trespass is (i) a **direct** interference, (ii) actionable **without proof of damage** (*per se*), and (iii) done intentionally or negligently.

**A. TRESPASS TO THE PERSON**

**1. Assault.** An act by which the defendant **intentionally or recklessly** makes the plaintiff reasonably **apprehend immediate unlawful force**. No contact is needed. Words may suffice (*R v. Ireland* [1998] AC 147: silent telephone calls), though words can cancel an apparent threat (*Tuberville v. Savage* (1669): "If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you"). In *Stephens v. Myers* (1830) the defendant advanced with a clenched fist towards the chairman of a meeting and was stopped by a bystander; it was an assault, as he had the present means to carry out the threat.

**2. Battery.** The **direct and intentional application of force** to another without lawful justification. The slightest touch is enough if it is unwanted (*Cole v. Turner* (1704): "the least touching of another in anger is a battery"). *Wilson v. Pringle* [1987] QB 237 asked for a "hostile" touching; *F v. West Berkshire HA* [1990] 2 AC 1 preferred the test of contact beyond what is acceptable in everyday life. A continuing act is enough (*Fagan v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439: a driver stopped his car on a constable's foot and left it there). Treatment without consent is a battery (*Samira Kohli*).

*Defences to assault and battery:* consent, self-defence, lawful authority, necessity, parental discipline. Both are crimes: BNS ss. 128–131 (formerly IPC ss. 349–352) †.

**3. False imprisonment.** **Total restraint of a person's liberty without lawful justification.**
- **Total restraint:** a complete deprivation of the freedom to move. Partial obstruction is not enough if there is another way out (*Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742: part of Hammersmith Bridge was closed; the plaintiff could have gone back).
- **No lawful authority** (an arrest beyond the law, or detention after the lawful time).
- **Intentional act** of restraint.
- **Knowledge** of the plaintiff is not needed (*Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation* (1919) 122 LT 44).
- *Herd v. Weardale Steel* [1915] AC 67: a miner who refused to do work he thought unsafe, and was not at once taken up the shaft, was not falsely imprisoned, since the employer was not bound to carry him up at once. *Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry* [1910] AC 295: a condition that a fee be paid on leaving is reasonable.
- *Remedies:* damages, **habeas corpus** (Arts. 32, 226), prosecution for wrongful confinement (BNS ss. 126–127 †), and compensation as a public-law remedy against the State: *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 (14 years' detention after acquittal; ₹30,000); *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677; *D.K. Basu* (1997) 1 SCC 416. Limitation: one year (Art. 73) ‡.

**4. Malicious prosecution.** The defendant **prosecuted the plaintiff, without reasonable and probable cause, with malice; the proceedings ended in the plaintiff's favour; the plaintiff suffered damage** (*Hicks v. Faulkner* (1878) 8 QBD 167; *Glinski v. McIver* [1962] AC 726). It differs from false imprisonment because the arrest or process may be lawful, but the prosecution is malicious, and damage must be proved. Limitation: one year (Art. 74) ‡.

**B. TRESPASS TO LAND**

**5. Meaning and essentials.** The **direct, unjustified interference with the possession of land**: by entering, remaining on it, or placing an object on it.
1. **Possession** by the plaintiff (not ownership); a tenant may sue the landlord.
2. **Direct interference** (entering; *Holmes v. Wilson* (1839): supports built for a road on the plaintiff's land were a continuing trespass).
3. **Intentional or negligent**: a mistaken belief that the land is one's own is no defence (*Basely v. Clarkson* (1681): the defendant mowed the plaintiff's grass by mistake and was liable).
4. **Without consent or lawful justification.**
- ***Entick v. Carrington*** (1765): the King's Messengers entered the plaintiff's house on a general warrant; they were held liable. This is the source of the rule that an official needs lawful authority for any interference.
- Ownership extends to a reasonable height: *Bernstein v. Skyviews* [1978] QB 479 (an aircraft high above the land is no trespass).

*Defences:* licence (consent, including implied), justification by law (arrest, a search warrant, distress), necessity, defence of person or property, easement or right of way.

**6. Trespass *ab initio*.** A person who enters land **lawfully, by authority of the law**, but then commits a **positive wrongful act** is treated as a trespasser **from the very beginning**. ***The Six Carpenters' Case*** (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a: the carpenters entered an inn and ordered wine, which the law allowed; they then refused to pay. They were held **not** trespassers from the beginning, since refusing to pay was a mere omission (non-feasance); but if they had done a positive act (misfeasance), such as taking away the wine, the entry would have been unlawful from the start. *Limits:* it applies only to entry by authority of the law (e.g. a sheriff or distrainor), not to entry by the owner's licence, and only to positive abuse. It is criticised and little used today (*Elias v. Pasmore* [1934] 2 KB 164; *Cinnamond v. British Airports Authority* [1980] 1 WLR 582 †).

*Remedies:* damages; injunction; recovery of possession (Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 5–6: a dispossessed person may sue within six months under s. 6 without proving title); reasonable force to eject a trespasser (not excessive force: *Bird v. Holbrook*). Criminal trespass: BNS s. 329 †.

**C. TRESPASS TO GOODS**

**7.** *Trespass to goods* is a direct interference with goods in the plaintiff's **possession**: taking, moving or damaging them (*Kirk v. Gregory* (1876) 1 Ex D 55 †). *Conversion* is a dealing with another's goods **inconsistent with his rights**, so as to deny his title: *Hollins v. Fowler* (1875) LR 7 HL 757 (an innocent broker who sold cotton for a thief was liable); *Fouldes v. Willoughby* (1841) (taking a horse off a ferry did not deny the owner's title and was not conversion). *Detinue* is the wrongful refusal to give up goods on demand; in India the remedy is recovery of specific movables (Specific Relief Act, s. 7).

**Comparison**

| | Trespass | Nuisance | Negligence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interference | Direct | Indirect | Breach of a duty |
| Damage | Not needed | Needed | Needed |
| Interest | Person, land, goods | Use of land | Person, property |

**Conclusion.** Trespass protects bodily integrity, liberty, possession of land and possession of goods against direct interference and does so without proof of damage. In India it is reinforced by the Constitution (Arts. 21 and 22), under which the State pays compensation for unlawful detention.

## C-19 · Define defamation and discuss its essentials. What defences are available in an action for defamation?

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q11 ("Discuss the ingredients of the tort of defamation. What defence are available in an action for defamation?"), Dec 2023 Q13 ("Define Defamation and point out its essentials. What defences are available to a tort feasor in case of Defamation?") and Dec 2022 Q13 ("Explain in detail certain essential conditions of liability of a person for defamation"), all 20 marks. Short forms: Dec 2025 Q1 (A-103, libel and slander), Dec 2023 Q4 (A-104, innuendo). Related: A-102–A-110, B-46–B-50.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** definition and libel/slander 4 · essentials 7 · defences 7 · BNS s. 356 and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Defamation protects **reputation**. The law must balance a person's right to his good name (a facet of Art. 21: *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) 7 SCC 221) against freedom of speech (Art. 19(1)(a)), with "defamation" listed in Art. 19(2) as a ground of restriction. In India defamation is both a **tort** (uncodified; English common law) and a **crime** (BNS s. 356, replacing IPC ss. 499–502 from 1 July 2024).

**1. Definition**
- **Winfield & Jolowicz:** publication of a statement which tends to lower a person in the estimation of **right-thinking members of society generally**, or which tends to make them shun or avoid him.
- **Lord Atkin, *Sim v. Stretch*** [1936] 2 All ER 1237: would the words tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally?
- **BNS s. 356(1) (earlier IPC s. 499):** making or publishing any imputation concerning a person by words, signs or visible representations, **intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that it will harm, his reputation**.

**2. Libel and slander**

| | Libel | Slander |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Permanent (writing, printing, picture, film, broadcast) | Transient (spoken words, gestures) |
| English civil law | Actionable *per se* | Needs **special damage**, except: imputation of a crime punishable with imprisonment; of a loathsome contagious disease; of unchastity in a woman; words disparaging a person in office, trade or profession |
| India | Actionable; also an offence | Also an offence; the civil position is not settled in the sources I checked (many Indian writers say both are actionable *per se*) † |

**3. Essentials**
1. **The statement must be defamatory.** The test is the effect on the ordinary reasonable reader; intention is irrelevant in civil law. *Innuendo*: words that look harmless but carry a defamatory meaning because of **extrinsic facts** known to the audience. A *true* (legal) innuendo must be pleaded and proved with those facts: *Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons* [1931] AC 333 (a caricature of an amateur golfer in a chocolate advertisement suggested that he sold his amateur status); *Cassidy v. Daily Mirror* [1929] 2 KB 331 (a photograph of Mr C with "Miss X, whose engagement is announced" made friends think that his wife was not married). A *false* (popular) innuendo is a meaning that the ordinary reader would infer from the words: *Lewis v. Daily Telegraph* [1964] AC 234.
2. **The statement must refer to the plaintiff.** The test is whether sensible readers would understand it to refer to him, though not named (*Newstead v. London Express* [1940] 1 KB 377). Intention is not needed: *Hulton v. Jones* [1910] AC 20 (a newspaper wrote about "Artemus Jones", a church-warden at Peckham, whom the author thought fictitious; a real barrister of that name sued and won). A class is defamed only if it is small or the words point to the plaintiff (*Knupffer v. London Express* [1944] AC 116).
3. **Publication.** The statement must be communicated to at least **one person other than the plaintiff**. Dictation to a typist is publication (*Pullman v. Hill* [1891] 1 QB 524); every repetition is a fresh publication (*Duke of Brunswick v. Harmer* (1849)); online publication counts where it is read.
4. **Damage:** presumed in libel; special damage for slander, except in the cases above.

**4. Defences**

*(a) Justification (truth).* In civil law truth is a **complete defence** even if the motive is malicious; the defendant must prove the statement is **substantially true** (*Alexander v. North Eastern Railway* (1865)). In criminal law truth is a defence only if it was **for the public good** (BNS s. 356, Exception 1).

*(b) Fair comment.* Honest opinion on a **matter of public interest**, based on facts that are true or privileged, made without malice (*Campbell v. Spottiswoode* (1863); *Kemsley v. Foot* [1952] AC 345). It must be recognisable as comment, not a statement of fact. See the Exceptions to s. 356: public conduct of public servants (2), conduct of any person on a public question (3), merits of a public performance (6) (C-20).

*(c) Privilege*
- **Absolute privilege** (no liability even if false and malicious): statements by members in Parliament and the State legislatures (Arts. 105(2), 194(2)); judicial proceedings (judges, advocates, parties and witnesses); communications between State officers on State business (*Chatterton v. Secretary of State for India* [1895] 2 QB 189); fair reports of legislative proceedings (Art. 361A †).
- **Qualified privilege** (protected if made honestly and without malice: *Horrocks v. Lowe* [1975] AC 135): communications made in discharge of a legal, social or moral **duty**, or to protect an **interest**, to a person with a corresponding duty or interest (*Toogood v. Spyring* (1834); *Adam v. Ward* [1917] AC 309); fair and accurate reports of judicial proceedings; reports of public meetings. The *Reynolds* defence for responsible journalism on a matter of public concern (*Reynolds v. Times Newspapers* [2001] 2 AC 127) has been replaced in England by the statutory public-interest defence (Defamation Act, 2013, s. 4); Indian courts have not formally adopted it, but see *R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1994) 6 SCC 632.

*(d) Other defences.* Consent; apology (taken into account in reducing damages); innocent dissemination (a distributor without knowledge: *Emmens v. Pottle* (1885)); mere vulgar abuse is not defamatory. Online intermediaries are protected under s. 79 of the IT Act, 2000, subject to conditions †. Repeating what others said is **not** a defence.

**5. Remedies and limitation.** Damages (general, special, aggravated, exemplary); injunction, rarely before trial (*Bonnard v. Perryman* [1891] 2 Ch 269 †); apology. Limitation: one year (Arts. 75–76 of the Limitation Act, 1963) ‡.

**6. Criminal defamation.** BNS s. 356 provides simple imprisonment up to **two years**, or fine, or both, or **community service** ‡, with ten Exceptions (truth for the public good; conduct of public servants; public questions; court reports; merits of cases; public performances; censure by a person in authority; accusation to an authorised person; protection of interest; caution). *Subramanian Swamy* (2016) upheld the offence. On 22 September 2025, in a hearing, Justice M.M. Sundresh observed that the time had come to decriminalise it ‡; that was an oral remark and the law is unchanged.

**Conclusion.** A defamatory statement, referring to the plaintiff and published to a third person, is actionable. Truth, fair comment and privilege are the main defences, and the criminal law adds a "public good" requirement for truth. Libel and slander are distinguished mainly in English law.

## C-20 · (Problem) A says of a book published by Z: "Z's book is foolish; Z must be a weak man. Z's book is indecent; Z must be a man of impure mind." Can A be held liable for making a defamatory statement against Z?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q10 (20 marks, problem question). The facts match the Illustration to Exception 6 to IPC s. 499 (now BNS s. 356). Related: B-48, A-106.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** facts and issues 3 · rule (definition, defences, Exception 6) 6 · application 8 · conclusion and variant 3

### Answer

**Facts.** Z has published a book. A says: (1) "Z's book is foolish; Z must be a weak man", and (2) "Z's book is indecent; Z must be a man of impure mind." Z claims that A has defamed him.

**Issues**
1. Are A's statements defamatory of Z?
2. If so, can A rely on a defence: fair comment in tort, or Exception 6 to BNS s. 356 (merits of a public performance) in the criminal law?
3. What is the effect of A's statements about Z's *character*, as opposed to the book?

**Rule**
1. **Defamation** is the publication of a statement that tends to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society (*Sim v. Stretch*), and the criminal offence is the making or publishing of an imputation intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that it will harm, a person's reputation (BNS s. 356(1); earlier IPC s. 499). An imputation harms reputation if it lowers the person's moral or intellectual character in the estimation of others (Explanation 4).
2. **Fair comment** (tort): comment on a **matter of public interest**, which is an expression of **opinion**, based on **true facts**, made **honestly and without malice** (*Campbell v. Spottiswoode*; *Kemsley v. Foot*). A book put before the public is a matter of public interest, and criticism of it is allowed.
3. **Exception 6 to s. 356:** it is not defamation to express in **good faith** any opinion respecting the **merits of any performance which its author has submitted to the judgment of the public**, or respecting the **character of the author so far as his character appears in that performance, and no further**. "Good faith" means with due care and attention (IPC s. 52; the BNS definition is to the same effect †).
4. The Illustration to the Exception states the very facts of this problem: A is within the exception if he says these things in good faith, "in so far as the opinion which he expresses respects Z's character only so far as it appears in Z's book, and no further". It adds that if A says, "I am not surprised that Z's book is foolish and indecent, for he is a weak man and a libertine", A is **not** within the exception, because his opinion of Z's character is not founded on Z's book.

**Application**
1. *Are the statements defamatory?* "Foolish" and "indecent" are comments on the book. "Z must be a weak man" and "a man of impure mind" go to Z's intellectual and moral character, and they could lower Z in the estimation of right-thinking people. A statement referring to Z was published to others. The first elements of defamation are therefore met, and *prima facie* the statements are defamatory of Z. The burden is then on A to bring himself within a defence.
2. *Is Z's book a "performance submitted to the judgment of the public"?* Yes. Z published it, so the public, including A, may comment on its merits. The Exception covers any performance its author has put before the public, and the Illustration itself is about a book.
3. *Is A's opinion about the book's merits within the Exception?* Yes. "Foolish" and "indecent" are expressions of opinion on the work. They are not statements of fact, and the work is available for others to judge.
4. *Is the opinion on Z's character within the limit "so far as it appears in the performance and no further"?* A's inferences are drawn **from the book itself**: "Z's book is foolish; **Z must be** a weak man"; "the book is indecent; **Z must be** a man of impure mind". The word "must" shows the character is inferred from the book. A does not say that Z has a weak character or an impure mind independent of the book, or that he has led a bad life. The opinion on Z's character is therefore confined to what the book shows and goes no further.
5. *Good faith.* The Exception requires good faith (due care and attention). On the facts given, nothing shows malice or lack of care; A appears to have read the book and to state his honest view. If Z could prove that A had not read the book, or was moved by spite, the defence would fail, since the defence of fair comment is defeated by malice.
6. *Civil liability.* The same result follows in tort: A's comment is an honest opinion on a matter of public interest (a published book), based on facts that are stated truly (the content of the book is before the public), and made without malice. The defence of fair comment applies. A's statement that Z is "weak" or "of impure mind" is a fair inference from the book, as the critic is permitted to draw inferences about the author's character from his work. Truth (justification) is not needed.

**Conclusion.** A is **not liable**, civilly or criminally, if he made the statements in good faith, since they are opinions on the merits of a published work and on Z's character only as it appears in that work (Exception 6 to BNS s. 356; fair comment).

**Variant.** If A had said, "I am not surprised the book is foolish and indecent, for Z is a weak man and a libertine", the opinion about Z's character would **not** be founded on the book. A would be outside the Exception and would be liable unless he could prove justification (truth, and for the criminal law, public good) or another defence.

## C-21 · What do you mean by fraud or deceit? What are the essentials of the tort of deceit? Describe

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 (20 marks; this wording). Related: A-101, B-45, B-36.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · *Derry v. Peek* 5 · essentials with cases 8 · remedies, Indian law and distinctions 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** **Deceit** (fraud) is a tort in which a person who has been **deliberately misled** by a false statement, and who has acted on it to his loss, recovers damages. The action for deceit goes back to *Pasley v. Freeman* (1789) 3 TR 51, where a false statement of a person's creditworthiness was held actionable.

**1. Meaning.** Deceit is a **false representation, made knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly**, with the intention that the plaintiff should act on it, which the plaintiff does, and **suffers damage**.

**2. *Derry v. Peek*** (1889) 14 App Cas 337 — the leading case.
*Facts.* The directors of a tramway company issued a prospectus stating that they had the right to use **steam power** instead of horses. They believed that Parliament's special Act gave them the right, subject to the Board of Trade's consent, which they thought a formality. The Board later refused. The company failed. Peek, who had bought shares on the strength of the prospectus, sued the directors for deceit.
*Held (House of Lords; Lord Herschell).* Fraud is proved when it is shown that a false representation has been made (i) **knowingly**, or (ii) **without belief in its truth**, or (iii) **recklessly, careless whether it be true or false**. An **honest belief**, even if unreasonable, negates deceit. The directors honestly believed that the right existed, so there was no deceit. (Parliament responded with the Directors' Liability Act, 1890; see now the Companies Act, 2013, ss. 34–35 †.)

**3. Essentials**
1. **A false representation of fact.**
   - It must be a statement of **fact**, not opinion, promise or law (*Bisset v. Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177: a statement that land would carry 2,000 sheep was an opinion).
   - A statement of **present intention** is a statement of fact about one's state of mind (*Edgington v. Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459: directors said that a loan would be used to develop the company, but meant to pay debts).
   - It may be made by words or conduct. **Silence** is not misrepresentation unless there is a duty to speak, but a half-truth that misleads is (*Peek v. Gurney* (1873)).
2. **Knowledge of falsity, or recklessness** (*Derry v. Peek*). Negligence is not enough.
3. **Intention that the plaintiff should act on it.** The statement must be made to the plaintiff or to a class of which he is a member, with the intention that it be acted upon.
4. **The plaintiff acted on it** (reliance). It need not be the only inducement (*Edgington*).
5. **Damage.** Actual loss is essential; deceit without damage is not actionable.

**4. Remedies.** Damages for **all loss flowing directly from the fraud**, whether or not foreseeable (*Doyle v. Olby (Ironmongers)* [1969] 2 QB 158; *Smith New Court Securities v. Scrimgeour Vickers* [1997] AC 254); this is wider than the usual remoteness rule.

**5. Indian law.** The **Indian Contract Act, 1872**, s. 17 defines fraud for contracts: a suggestion as a fact of that which is not true by one who does not believe it to be true; active concealment of a fact; a promise made without any intention of performing it; any other act fitted to deceive; and any act or omission that the law declares fraudulent. Mere silence is not fraud unless there is a duty to speak, or the silence is equivalent to speech. Under s. 19, a contract induced by fraud is **voidable** at the option of the deceived party, who may also claim damages. **Cheating** is an offence (BNS s. 318, formerly IPC ss. 415–420 †); fraud in a company is punished by the Companies Act, 2013, s. 447 †.

**6. Distinctions**

| | Deceit | Negligent misstatement | Innocent misrepresentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mental state | Knowledge, no belief, or recklessness | Careless, with a duty of care (*Hedley Byrne*) | Honest and careful |
| Remedy | Damages for all direct loss; rescission | Damages | Rescission (Contract Act, s. 19); no damages in tort |
| Key case | *Derry v. Peek* | *Hedley Byrne v. Heller* | — |

**Conclusion.** Deceit needs a false statement of fact, made fraudulently, intended to be and in fact relied upon, and causing loss. *Derry v. Peek* fixes fraud at knowledge, absence of belief, or recklessness. An honest but careless misstatement gives a claim in negligence, not deceit.
