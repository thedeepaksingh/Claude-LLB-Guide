## B-37 · Distinguish between public nuisance and private nuisance

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q1 (A-85, 4 marks: "What do you understand by Nuisance?"). 20-mark form: C-17.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 2 · table of differences 6 · cases and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Nuisance** is an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right connected with it (Winfield).

| | Public nuisance | Private nuisance |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Act or omission materially affecting the comfort and convenience of a **class** of people (BNS s. 270 †) | Interference with one person's use or enjoyment of **his land** |
| Nature | A **crime**; a tort only for one who suffers **special damage** | A **tort** only |
| Who sues | Advocate-General; a private person only with special damage (*Rose v. Miles*, 1815); CPC s. 91 | The occupier of the land (*Hunter v. Canary Wharf*, 1997) |
| Examples | Obstructing a highway; a dangerous quarry; unfit food | Smell, noise, smoke, vibration |
| Remedy | Prosecution; order for removal (BNSS s. 152 †); damages | Damages, injunction, abatement |

*A-G v. PYA Quarries* [1957] 2 QB 169: a public nuisance is so widespread that it is unreasonable to expect one person to sue.

**Private nuisance requires** unreasonable interference, with use or enjoyment of land, causing substantial damage.

**Cases.** *Sturges v. Bridgman* (1879) (locality); *St Helen's v. Tipping* (1865) (damage to property); *Ratlam* AIR 1980 SC 1622 (a municipality cannot plead lack of funds).

## B-38 · What defences are open in an action based on nuisance?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q8 ("What are the defences open in an action based on nuisance?"). 20-mark form: C-17.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** valid defences 5 · non-defences 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Valid defences**
1. **Prescription.** A right to do the act is acquired as an easement by **20 years'** uninterrupted use as of right (Easements Act, 1882, s. 15; *Sturges v. Bridgman*).
2. **Statutory authority**: *Allen v. Gulf Oil* [1981] AC 1001.
3. **Consent** of the plaintiff.
4. **Act of a stranger or Act of God**, where the defendant could not prevent it.
5. **Reasonable user** in the locality.
6. **Plaintiff's abnormal sensitivity**: *Robinson v. Kilvert* (1889).

**Not defences**
1. **Coming to the nuisance**: *Bliss v. Hall* (1838); *Miller v. Jackson* [1977] QB 966; *Coventry v. Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13.
2. **Public benefit or utility**: *Adams v. Ursell* [1913] 1 Ch 269 (a fish shop in a residential street); it may affect the remedy (damages instead of an injunction).
3. **Reasonable care**: nuisance looks at the result.
4. **That others also contribute**: *Lambton v. Mellish* [1894] 3 Ch 163.
5. **Planning permission** alone.

**Conclusion.** The defences are narrow; the plaintiff need not have been there first, and public utility is not an excuse.

## B-39 · Distinguish nuisance from negligence, trespass and the rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-17, C-18. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** table 7 · explanation 3

### Answer

| | Nuisance | Trespass to land | Negligence | *Rylands v. Fletcher* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interest protected | Use and enjoyment of land | Possession of land | Person, property, economic interest | Land and property |
| Interference | **Indirect** | **Direct** | Breach of a duty of care | Escape of a dangerous thing |
| Damage | Needed (private nuisance) | **Not needed** (actionable *per se*) | Needed | Needed |
| Fault | Not essential | Intentional or negligent act | **Essential** | Not essential |
| Nature | Usually continuing | Entry, remaining | Single or continuing | Single escape |
| Example | Smoke, noise from a factory | Walking on another's land | Careless driving | Water from a reservoir |

**Explanation.** In **nuisance** the question is whether the interference is unreasonable between neighbours (*Bamford v. Turnley*, 1862). **Trespass** protects possession and is actionable without damage (*Entick v. Carrington*, 1765). **Negligence** needs duty, breach and damage (*Donoghue v. Stevenson*). **Rylands** imposes strict liability for the escape of a dangerous thing from non-natural use of land. One set of facts may fall under more than one head: a leaking factory chimney may be a nuisance and negligence.

## B-40 · Explain trespass to land and the doctrine of trespass *ab initio*

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q2 (A-95, 4 marks: "Define Trespass-ab-initio"). 20-mark form: C-18.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** trespass to land 4 · trespass *ab initio* with case 4 · defences and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Trespass to land** is the direct, unjustified interference with another's **possession** of land, by entering, remaining on it, or placing something on it. It is actionable *per se*.
1. Possession by the plaintiff.
2. Direct interference (*Holmes v. Wilson*, 1839).
3. Intentional or negligent; mistake is no defence (*Basely v. Clarkson*, 1681).
4. No consent or lawful justification. *Entick v. Carrington* (1765): officers entering on a general warrant were liable.

**Defences:** licence, justification by law, necessity, defence of person or property, easement.

**Trespass *ab initio*.** A person who enters land **lawfully, by authority of the law**, but then commits a **positive wrongful act** is treated as a trespasser from the very beginning. ***The Six Carpenters' Case*** (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a: the carpenters entered an inn and ordered wine, then refused to pay; they were not trespassers *ab initio*, because refusal to pay was a mere omission (non-feasance), but a positive act (misfeasance), such as taking away the wine, would have made them so.

**Limits.** Only entry by authority of the law (not by the owner's licence), and only positive abuse. It is little used today (*Elias v. Pasmore*, 1934; *Cinnamond*, 1980 †).

## B-41 · What are the ingredients of the tort of false imprisonment?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q3 (A-98, 4 marks: "Explain the ingredients of false imprisonment"). 20-mark form: C-18.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · ingredients with cases 6 · remedies 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** **Total restraint** of a person's liberty, without lawful justification.

**Ingredients**
1. **Total restraint.** Complete deprivation of the freedom to move. Partial obstruction is not enough if there is another way out: *Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742 (a closed part of Hammersmith Bridge; the plaintiff could have gone back).
2. **Without lawful authority**: an arrest outside the law, or detention beyond the lawful time.
3. **Intentional act** of restraint (not mere negligence).
4. **Knowledge** of the plaintiff is not needed: *Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation* (1919).
5. **Means:** physical barriers, force, or assertion of authority to which the plaintiff submits. A condition that a fee be paid on leaving is reasonable (*Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry*, 1910). *Herd v. Weardale Steel* [1915] AC 67: a miner not at once carried up the shaft was not falsely imprisoned.

**Remedies.** Damages (even nominal); **habeas corpus** (Arts. 32, 226); prosecution for wrongful confinement (BNS ss. 126–127 †); **compensation from the State**: *Rudul Sah* (1983) (14 years' detention after acquittal). Limitation: one year (Art. 73) ‡.

## B-42 · Distinguish between assault and battery

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-18. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** assault 3 · battery 3 · differences 2 · defences 2

### Answer

**Assault** is an act by which the defendant intentionally or recklessly makes the plaintiff reasonably **apprehend immediate unlawful force**. No contact is needed. Words can suffice (*R v. Ireland* [1998] AC 147: silent telephone calls), though words may negate a threat (*Tuberville v. Savage*, 1669: "If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you"). *Stephens v. Myers* (1830): a man advanced with a clenched fist towards the chairman of a meeting and was stopped by a bystander; assault.

**Battery** is the **direct and intentional application of force** to another without justification. The slightest unwanted touch is enough (*Cole v. Turner*, 1704). *Fagan v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439: a driver left his car on a constable's foot; a continuing act. Treatment without consent is a battery (*Samira Kohli*, 2008).

| | Assault | Battery |
|---|---|---|
| Essence | Apprehension of force | Actual application of force |
| Contact | Not needed | Needed |

Both are actionable *per se* and are crimes (BNS ss. 128–131 †). **Defences:** consent, self-defence, lawful authority, necessity.

## B-43 · Explain trespass to goods and conversion

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-18. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** trespass to goods 3 · conversion 4 · detinue and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Trespass to goods** is a **direct** interference with goods in the plaintiff's **possession**: taking, moving or damaging them. It is actionable *per se*. *Kirk v. Gregory* (1876) 1 Ex D 55 †: a dead person's jewellery was moved to another room, where it was stolen.

**Conversion** is a dealing with another's goods **inconsistent with his rights**, so as to deny his title: selling, destroying, refusing to return, or using them as one's own. Intention to deny the owner's title is needed; good faith is no defence. *Hollins v. Fowler* (1875) LR 7 HL 757: an innocent broker who sold cotton for a thief was liable. *Fouldes v. Willoughby* (1841): taking a horse off a ferry was not conversion, as it did not deny the owner's title.

**Detinue** is the wrongful refusal to give up goods on demand. In India the owner sues for **recovery of specific movables** (Specific Relief Act, 1963, s. 7), or damages.

| | Trespass to goods | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| Possession | Plaintiff's possession | Right to possess |
| Gist | Direct interference | Denial of title |

**Remedies.** Damages (value of goods), return, injunction.

## B-44 · Explain the tort of malicious prosecution. How does it differ from false imprisonment?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks; not named in the syllabus but a standard head. Related: C-18. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** essentials 6 · difference 3 · limitation 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** Prosecuting the plaintiff **maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause**, in proceedings that end in his favour, causing damage.

**Essentials**
1. The defendant **prosecuted** the plaintiff (set the law in motion).
2. The proceedings **ended in the plaintiff's favour**.
3. **No reasonable and probable cause**: no honest belief based on reasonable grounds (*Hicks v. Faulkner*, 1878).
4. **Malice**: an improper motive, not merely bringing a guilty person to justice (*Glinski v. McIver* [1962] AC 726).
5. **Damage** to reputation, person (liberty) or property.

| | False imprisonment | Malicious prosecution |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong | Unlawful total restraint | Malicious institution of proceedings |
| Nature | Trespass; direct | Action on the case; indirect |
| Damage | Not needed | **Needed** |
| Malice | Not needed | **Needed**, with absence of reasonable cause |
| Favourable termination | Not needed | **Needed** |

**Abuse of process** (*Grainger v. Hill*, 1838): misuse of legal process for an improper purpose, even if properly begun.

**Limitation.** One year from the termination of the proceedings (Limitation Act, 1963, Art. 74) ‡.

## B-45 · Explain the tort of deceit and the rule in *Derry v. Peek*

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 (C-21, 20 marks). 20-mark form: C-21.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · *Derry v. Peek* 4 · essentials 3 · remedy 1

### Answer

**Deceit** is a false statement made **knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly**, intending that the plaintiff act on it, which he does and suffers damage.

***Derry v. Peek*** (1889) 14 App Cas 337. Directors of a tramway company stated in a prospectus that they had the right to use **steam power**. They believed that the Board of Trade's consent was a formality; it was refused. A shareholder, Peek, sued for deceit. The House of Lords (Lord Herschell) held that fraud is proved when a false representation is made (i) knowingly, (ii) without belief in its truth, or (iii) recklessly, careless whether it be true or false. An **honest belief**, even if unreasonable, is no fraud; the directors were not liable.

**Essentials**
1. A **false representation of fact** (not opinion: *Bisset v. Wilkinson*, 1927). A statement of present intention is a statement of fact (*Edgington v. Fitzmaurice*, 1885).
2. **Knowledge of falsity** or recklessness.
3. **Intention** that the plaintiff act on it.
4. The plaintiff **acted** on it.
5. **Damage**.

**Remedy.** Damages for all direct loss (*Doyle v. Olby*, 1969). Contract Act, 1872, ss. 17 and 19: the contract is voidable. Careless statements: *Hedley Byrne*.

## B-46 · Distinguish between libel and slander

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q1 (A-103, 4 marks: "Difference between Libel and Slander"). 20-mark form: C-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 2 · table 5 · exceptions and India 3

### Answer

**Defamation** is the publication of a statement that tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society (*Sim v. Stretch*, 1936).

| | Libel | Slander |
|---|---|---|
| Form | **Permanent**: writing, printing, picture, film, broadcast | **Transient**: spoken words, gestures |
| English civil law | Actionable **without proof of damage** | Needs **special damage** |
| Crime | In India, an offence (BNS s. 356) | Also an offence in India |
| Reason | Permanence, wider spread | Transience |
| Example | A newspaper article, a social-media post | A speech |

**Slander actionable without special damage (English law):** imputation of (i) a crime punishable with imprisonment; (ii) a loathsome contagious disease; (iii) unchastity in a woman; (iv) words calculated to disparage a person in his office, profession, trade or business. England has since changed the law (Defamation Act, 2013, s. 14 †).

**India.** The courts follow the English distinction, but the position is not settled by the Supreme Court, and many Indian writers say that both libel and slander are actionable *per se* †. Limitation: one year for each (Arts. 75–76) ‡.

**Conclusion.** The distinction depends on the form of publication, which affects proof of damage.

## B-47 · What is "innuendo"? Explain with examples

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q4 (A-104, 4 marks: "What is meant by 'Innuendo'? Explain by giving examples."). 20-mark form: C-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · true innuendo 3 · false innuendo 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Innuendo** is a statement that looks harmless on its face but carries a defamatory meaning because of **extrinsic facts** known to those who read or hear it.

**True (legal) innuendo.** The hidden meaning arises from facts known to the recipients. The plaintiff must **plead and prove** those facts and show that the words were understood in that sense.
- *Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons* [1931] AC 333: an amateur golfer was shown in a chocolate advertisement; those who knew that amateurs may not be paid for their name took it that he had sold his amateur status.
- *Cassidy v. Daily Mirror* [1929] 2 KB 331: a photograph of Mr C and "Miss X, whose engagement is announced" led friends to think that his wife, Mrs C, was not married to him, and so she was defamed.

**False (popular) innuendo.** An inference that an ordinary reader would draw from the words themselves, without special knowledge: *Lewis v. Daily Telegraph* [1964] AC 234 (a report that the fraud squad was inquiring into a company's affairs).

**Conclusion.** The test is what the words mean to reasonable persons with the knowledge they have.

## B-48 · Explain justification and fair comment as defences in defamation

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2025 Q10 (C-20). 20-mark form: C-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** justification 4 · fair comment 5 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Justification (truth).** In **civil law** truth is a complete defence, even if the motive is malicious. The defendant must prove that the statement is **substantially true**; the "sting" must be true (*Alexander v. North Eastern Railway*, 1865). A failed plea of truth may increase damages. In **criminal law** truth is a defence only if the publication was **for the public good** (BNS s. 356, Exception 1).

**Fair comment (honest opinion).** The defendant must show that:
1. The comment is on a **matter of public interest**.
2. It is an **expression of opinion**, not a statement of fact.
3. It is based on **facts that are true** or privileged.
4. It is made **honestly**, without **malice**.
*Campbell v. Spottiswoode* (1863); *Kemsley v. Foot* [1952] AC 345. An opinion that would be defamatory in itself may still be fair if it is a fair inference from the facts.

**Indian criminal law.** Exceptions to s. 356: conduct of public servants (2); conduct of any person on a public question (3); **merits of a public performance** (6): see the book problem, Dec 2025 Q10 (C-20).

**Conclusion.** Truth protects the facts; fair comment protects opinions about them.

## B-49 · Explain absolute and qualified privilege in defamation

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-19. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** absolute 4 · qualified 5 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Absolute privilege.** No liability, even if the statement is false and malicious.
1. **Parliament and State legislatures**: statements by members in the House (Arts. 105(2), 194(2)).
2. **Judicial proceedings**: statements by judges, advocates, parties and witnesses.
3. **State communications**: between officers of State on State business (*Chatterton v. Secretary of State for India*, 1895).
4. **Fair reports** of proceedings of the legislatures, published in good faith (Art. 361A †).

**Qualified privilege.** Protected if made honestly and **without malice**; malice defeats it (*Horrocks v. Lowe* [1975] AC 135).
1. **Duty and interest**: a communication made in discharge of a legal, social or moral duty, or to protect one's own interest, to a person with a corresponding duty or interest (*Toogood v. Spyring*, 1834; *Adam v. Ward* [1917] AC 309). Indian Exceptions 8 (accusation to an authorised person), 9 (protection of interests) and 10 (caution).
2. **Fair and accurate reports** of judicial proceedings (Exception 4) and public meetings.
3. **Responsible journalism** on public concern (*Reynolds v. Times Newspapers* [2001] 2 AC 127); in England replaced by the Defamation Act, 2013, s. 4.

**Conclusion.** Absolute privilege protects the function; qualified privilege protects honest communications, and is lost by malice.

## B-50 · Explain criminal defamation under BNS s. 356. Was it upheld in *Subramanian Swamy*?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-19. 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** offence 3 · punishment and exceptions 3 · *Subramanian Swamy* 3 · current position 1

### Answer

**Offence.** BNS s. 356 (replacing IPC ss. 499–502 from 1 July 2024): whoever, by words, signs or visible representations, makes or publishes an imputation concerning a person **intending to harm, or knowing or having reason to believe that it will harm, his reputation**, defames him. Explanations cover the dead, companies and associations, ironical imputations, and what lowers a person's character or credit in others' estimation.

**Punishment.** Simple imprisonment up to **two years**, or fine, or both, or **community service** ‡.

**Exceptions** (ten): truth for the public good; conduct of public servants; public questions; court reports; merits of a decided case; merits of a public performance; censure by a person in authority; accusation to an authorised person; protection of interests; caution.

***Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India*** (2016) 7 SCC 221. The Supreme Court upheld IPC ss. 499–500 and CrPC s. 199 as reasonable restrictions on free speech under Art. 19(2), and held that **reputation is part of the right to life under Art. 21**.

**Current position.** On 22 September 2025, in a hearing, Justice M.M. Sundresh orally observed that the time had come to decriminalise defamation ‡. This was a remark only; *Subramanian Swamy* remains the law.
