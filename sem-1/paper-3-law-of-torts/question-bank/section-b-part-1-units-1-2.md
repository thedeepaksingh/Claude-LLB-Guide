## B-01 · Distinguish between tort and crime

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q2 (A-05, 4 marks: "What is the difference between tort and crime?"). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · five or six points of difference 6 · overlap with example 2

### Answer

A **tort** is a civil wrong redressed by an action for unliquidated damages; a **crime** is a wrong against the public punished by the State.

| Point | Tort | Crime |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong to | A private person | Society |
| Duty | Fixed by law, owed to people generally | Owed to the State |
| Parties | Injured person sues the wrongdoer | State prosecutes the accused |
| Aim | Compensation | Punishment and deterrence |
| Remedy | Damages, injunction | Imprisonment, fine, community service |
| Mental element | Not always needed | *Mens rea* usually needed |
| Compromise | Freely allowed | Only compoundable offences |
| Law | Mostly uncodified | Codified (BNS, 2023) |

**Overlap.** One act can be both: assault, defamation (BNS s. 356), and death by negligence (BNS s. 106). The two proceedings are separate; a conviction does not bar a civil claim, and the civil claim does not bar a prosecution.

**Conclusion.** Tort looks to the injured person's loss; crime looks to the harm to society.

## B-02 · Explain the difference between tort and contract

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q7 ("Explain the difference between tort and contract."). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · points of difference 6 · overlap and conclusion 2

### Answer

A **contract** is an agreement enforceable by law (Contract Act, 1872, s. 2(h)); a **tort** is a civil wrong for which the law gives damages, not being only a breach of contract or trust (Salmond).

| Point | Tort | Contract |
|---|---|---|
| Source of duty | Fixed by law | Created by the parties' agreement |
| Duty owed to | Persons generally (right *in rem*) | A party to the contract (right *in personam*) |
| Privity | Not required | Required, with exceptions |
| Damages | Unliquidated, fixed by the court | Liquidated or unliquidated (ss. 73–74) |
| Motive | May matter | Irrelevant |
| Capacity | A minor may be liable | A minor's agreement is void (*Mohori Bibee*, 1903) |
| Consent | Not needed to create liability | Essential |

**Overlap.** The same facts may give rise to both: a doctor's negligence towards a paying patient is a tort and a breach of contract (and "deficiency in service" under the Consumer Protection Act). The plaintiff may choose, but he cannot recover twice. *Donoghue v. Stevenson* (1932) shows liability in tort where there is no contract.

**Conclusion.** Tort duties come from the law, contract duties from agreement.

## B-03 · Define tort. What are its essential elements?

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q1 (A-02, 4 marks: "Define Tort and point out its main characteristics"). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definitions 3 · elements with example 5 · characteristics and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Definitions.** *Salmond:* a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common-law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively a breach of contract or of trust. *Winfield:* liability arising from breach of a duty primarily fixed by law, owed to persons generally and redressible by an action for unliquidated damages. *Limitation Act, 1963, s. 2(m):* a civil wrong which is not exclusively a breach of contract or of trust.

**Elements**
1. **A wrongful act or omission** by the defendant.
2. **A legal duty** owed to the plaintiff, fixed by law, and its breach.
3. **Legal damage** to the plaintiff: violation of a legal right. Some torts are actionable *per se* (trespass, libel); others only with proof of damage (negligence, nuisance).
4. **A legal remedy**, usually damages: *ubi jus ibi remedium*.

**Example.** A careless driver (act) who owes a duty to road users (duty) knocks down a pedestrian (damage), who sues for damages (remedy).

**Characteristics.** A civil wrong; the duty is owed to the public; damages are unliquidated; liability may be with or without fault; mostly uncodified in India.

## B-04 · Explain *damnum sine injuria* with decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q4 (A-10, 4 marks: "Explain 'Damnum Sine Injuria'"); Dec 2022 Q5 (A-11, the principle behind the maxim). 20-mark form: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · principle 2 · cases 4 · Indian case and conclusion 2

### Answer

***Damnum sine injuria*** means damage without legal injury: the plaintiff suffers loss, but no legal right of his is violated, so he has no action.

**Principle.** Liability depends on violation of a legal right, not on loss. The law does not make a person liable for lawful acts, even if they harm others: competition and the lawful use of one's property are the cost of living in society.

**Cases**
- ***Gloucester Grammar School Case*** (1410): a rival school opened nearby and the plaintiff's fees fell; no action.
- ***Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow*** (1889–92): shipowners cut freight rates to drive a rival out of the tea trade; lawful competition.
- ***Bradford Corporation v. Pickles*** [1895] AC 587: the defendant diverted underground water in his own land, hoping to be bought out; motive is immaterial for a lawful act.
- ***Ushaben v. Bhagyalaxmi Chitra Mandir*** AIR 1978 Guj 13: hurt to religious feelings by a film was not violation of a legal right.

**Contrast.** *Injuria sine damno* is a legal wrong without loss, and is actionable (*Ashby v. White*).

**Conclusion.** A moral or social wrong, or loss from lawful conduct, is not a tort.

## B-05 · Briefly explain the maxim *injuria sine damno* with the help of decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q7 (printed "Injuria sine Damnum"); Dec 2024 Q4 (A-10). 20-mark form: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · principle 2 · cases 4 · torts actionable per se and conclusion 2

### Answer

***Injuria sine damno*** means a legal wrong without actual loss. If a legal right of the plaintiff is violated, he can sue even though he has suffered no loss; he gets at least **nominal damages**.

**Principle.** *Ubi jus ibi remedium*: where there is a right there is a remedy. Rights must be protected whether or not harm follows. As Holt C.J. said, "every injury imports a damage".

**Cases**
- ***Ashby v. White*** (1703): the returning officer wrongly refused Ashby's vote. The candidate he wanted won anyway, so he lost nothing. The House of Lords upheld his right to sue, since the right to vote is a legal right.
- ***Marzetti v. Williams*** (1830): a banker wrongly dishonoured the customer's cheque; nominal damages were awarded.
- ***Constantine v. Imperial Hotels*** [1944] KB 693: a hotel refused to admit a cricketer because of his colour; he recovered nominal damages.
- **Torts actionable *per se*:** trespass to land, assault, battery, libel, false imprisonment.
- **India:** *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985): compensation for illegal detention of a legislator.

**Conclusion.** The test is violation of a right, not loss.

## B-06 · Explain the "pigeon-hole" theory of torts. Is the law of torts a list of torts or a general principle?

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q2 (A-07, 4 marks: "What do you understand by 'Pigeon Hole Theory'?"). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** the two theories 4 · arguments and cases 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Pigeon-hole theory.** Associated with *Salmond*, it says that there is no general liability for harm: the plaintiff must show that his case falls within one of the established torts (trespass, nuisance, defamation, negligence and so on), as papers fit into pigeon-holes. Hence "the law of **torts**". Harm that fits no pigeon-hole is not actionable.

**General-principle theory.** *Winfield* and *Pollock* said that any harm caused without lawful justification is actionable unless the law gives an excuse. Hence "the law of **tort**".

**Arguments.** For the first: certainty, and the courts' reluctance to create liability. For the second: *ubi jus ibi remedium*; new torts have been recognised: *Donoghue v. Stevenson* (1932) (general duty of care), *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868), *Wilkinson v. Downton* (1897), and in India absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987) and public-law compensation (*Nilabati Behera*, 1993).

**Conclusion.** The courts follow a middle path. The named torts are the starting point, but the list is not closed, and new torts are added by analogy.

## B-07 · When does a right of action for a tort come to an end or get discharged?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q6 ("When a right to action for a tort comes to an end or discharged?"). 20-mark form: C-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** death of a party 3 · other modes 5 · limitation and conclusion 2

### Answer

A right of action in tort may end in these ways:

1. **Death of a party.** *Actio personalis moritur cum persona*: a personal action dies with the person. In India the **Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306** lets causes of action survive to and against executors and administrators, **except** defamation, assault and other personal injuries not causing death. Dependants may sue under the **Fatal Accidents Act, 1855**.
2. **Waiver or acquiescence.** A person who, knowing the facts, elects to affirm the act or allows it, cannot later sue.
3. **Accord and satisfaction.** The injured party accepts something in place of his right, and the agreement is carried out.
4. **Release.** A release discharges the claim; release of one joint tortfeasor releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor*).
5. **Judgment.** The cause of action merges in the judgment; the matter cannot be tried again (*res judicata*, CPC s. 11).
6. **Limitation.** The Limitation Act, 1963 bars the remedy after the period: one year for false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, libel and slander (Arts. 73–76) ‡; the residuary Art. 113 gives three years †; for a continuing wrong a fresh period runs (s. 22).

## B-08 · What are the necessary conditions of liability for a foreign tort?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q8 ("What are necessary conditions of liability for foreign tort?"). 20-mark form: C-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 1 · rule in *Phillips v. Eyre* 4 · *Boys v. Chaplin* 3 · other conditions and conclusion 2

### Answer

A **foreign tort** is a wrong committed abroad but sued on in the forum (India). The rule is from ***Phillips v. Eyre*** (1870) LR 6 QB 1 (Willes J.). It is actionable in the forum if:

1. **It would have been actionable as a tort if committed in the forum** (*lex fori*). It is not enough that it was wrong where done.
2. **It was not justifiable by the law of the place where it was done** (*lex loci delicti*): it must be actionable civilly there, and no defence must apply.

This is **double actionability**. ***Boys v. Chaplin*** [1971] AC 356: two English servicemen were in Malta and one injured the other in a motor accident. Maltese law limited damages; English law did not. The House of Lords applied English law: both parties were English and Malta was only a temporary location (a flexible exception).

**Other conditions.** The court needs **jurisdiction** over the defendant (CPC ss. 19–20); the claim must not be penal or against public policy; foreign law must be **proved as a fact**.

**Position.** England abolished double actionability by the Private International Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1995. India has no statute and follows the common law †.

## B-09 · Distinguish between malice and motive in the law of torts

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Syllabus line 1 ("malice … motive"); Dec 2022–2025 none. 20-mark form: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** motive 3 · malice (law and fact) 3 · cases and exceptions 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Motive** is the reason why a person acts: spite, gain, revenge. It is **generally irrelevant**: a lawful act is not made unlawful by a bad motive. *Bradford Corporation v. Pickles* [1895] AC 587 (a landowner diverted water in his own land to force the Corporation to buy him out); *Allen v. Flood* [1898] AC 1; *Mogul* (1892) (competition to ruin a rival).

**Malice** has two senses.
- **In law:** a wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse (*Bromage v. Prosser*, 1825). It is implied from the act.
- **In fact:** ill-will or improper motive, such as spite. It must be proved.

| | Motive | Malice |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Reason for acting | Wrongful intention (law) or ill-will (fact) |
| General effect | Irrelevant | Relevant in some torts |

**Where malice matters:** malicious prosecution (*Hicks v. Faulkner*, 1878); defamation, where it defeats qualified privilege (*Horrocks v. Lowe*, 1975) and fair comment; conspiracy (*Quinn v. Leathem*, 1901); nuisance (*Christie v. Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316); and aggravated or exemplary damages.

**Conclusion.** Motive does not matter unless the tort or the defence depends on it.

## B-10 · Discuss the remedies available in an action for tort. What are the kinds of damages?

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q5 (A-14, 4 marks: "Give reasons for award of unliquidated damages in Torts"). 20-mark form: C-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** judicial remedies 5 · kinds of damages 3 · extra-judicial remedies and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Judicial remedies**
1. **Damages**: the usual remedy, **unliquidated** because they cannot be fixed in advance; the court assesses them on pain, loss of earnings, reputation and so on.
2. **Injunction**: prohibitory or mandatory, temporary or perpetual (Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 36–42; CPC O. XXXIX), for continuing wrongs such as nuisance and trespass.
3. **Restitution of property**: recovery of possession of immovable property (ss. 5–6) or movables (s. 7).

**Kinds of damages**
- **Nominal**: for a violation of a right without loss (*Ashby v. White*).
- **Contemptuous**: a trivial sum for a worthless claim.
- **General** (presumed) and **special** (proved) damages.
- **Aggravated**: for insult or malice that increased the injury.
- **Exemplary (punitive)**: to punish; allowed in India as public-law compensation (*Nilabati Behera*, 1993) and in English law in limited classes (*Rookes v. Barnard*, 1964).

The plaintiff must **mitigate** his loss. Motor and death claims are assessed by the multiplier method (*Pranay Sethi*, 2017).

**Extra-judicial remedies:** self-defence, re-entry on land, recapture of goods, abatement of nuisance (*Lemmon v. Webb*), expulsion of a trespasser, with reasonable force only.

## B-11 · Who may sue and be sued for a tort? Discuss the capacity of parties

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Syllabus line 1 ("who may sue and who may not be sued"). 20-mark form: C-05; related C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** general rule 1 · main classes 7 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**General rule.** Every person can sue and be sued in tort. The exceptions rest on incapacity or immunity.

- **Minors.** A minor sues through a **next friend** (CPC O. XXXII). He may be sued if old enough to understand the wrong, judged by a child's standard. Parents are not liable merely by the relationship.
- **Persons of unsound mind.** Sue through a guardian; generally liable, except for torts needing a particular intent †.
- **Corporations.** Sue and are sued in their own name (*Salomon v. Salomon*, 1897); liable for torts of servants in the course of employment.
- **Trade unions, firms.** A registered union can sue and be sued (Trade Unions Act, 1926, s. 13); partners are liable jointly and severally (Partnership Act, 1932, ss. 25–26).
- **Husband and wife.** Each sues and is liable separately.
- **State.** Liable under Art. 300; two months' notice under CPC s. 80.
- **Foreign sovereigns and diplomats.** Not suable without the Central Government's consent (CPC s. 86).
- **Judges.** Not liable for good-faith judicial acts (Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850).

**Conclusion.** Immunities rest on public policy.

## B-12 · State and explain the rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13 (C-04, 20 marks); Dec 2024 Q5 (A-24, "What do you mean by 'Strict Liability'?"). 20-mark form: C-04.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** facts 2 · rule 3 · essentials 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

***Rylands v. Fletcher*** (1866) LR 1 Ex 265; (1868) LR 3 HL 330. Rylands had a reservoir built on his land by independent contractors. Old mine shafts, unknown to him, connected it with Fletcher's mine. When it was filled, water burst through and flooded the mine. The contractors, not Rylands, were negligent. Fletcher won.

**Rule (Blackburn J.).** "The person who for his own purposes brings on his lands and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and, if he does not do so, is *prima facie* answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape." The House of Lords (Lord Cairns) added **non-natural use** of land.

**Essentials**
1. A dangerous thing brought, collected and kept by the defendant.
2. **Non-natural use** of the land (*Rickards v. Lothian*, 1913).
3. **Escape** to a place outside his control (*Read v. J. Lyons*, 1947).
4. **Damage**, of a reasonably foreseeable kind (*Cambridge Water*, 1994).

Liability is strict: negligence need not be proved. It is subject to exceptions (B-13). In India, hazardous industries are under **absolute liability** (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987: B-14).

## B-13 · What are the exceptions to the rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13 (C-04, 20 marks: "Are there any exceptions to this rule?"). 20-mark form: C-04.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each exception with case 1 mark × 7, introduction and conclusion 3

### Answer

The following are defences to an action under *Rylands v. Fletcher*:

1. **Act of God.** *Nichols v. Marsland* (1876): an extraordinary rainfall burst artificial lakes and washed away bridges; no liability.
2. **Consent of the plaintiff**, express or implied, or **common benefit**. *Carstairs v. Taylor* (1871): a rat gnawed a gutter and water damaged goods of a tenant in a building shared with the owner; no liability.
3. **Default of the plaintiff.** *Ponting v. Noakes* [1894] 2 QB 281: the plaintiff's horse reached over the fence and ate poisonous yew leaves; no recovery.
4. **Act of a stranger**, which the defendant could not foresee or prevent (*Box v. Jubb*, 1879).
5. **Statutory authority.** *Green v. Chelsea Waterworks* (1894): a company laying mains under statute was not liable for a burst pipe without negligence.
6. **Natural use of land**, such as ordinary domestic use (*Rickards v. Lothian*).
7. Damage caused by escape of something not "likely to do mischief" is outside the rule.

**India.** These exceptions do **not** apply to a hazardous enterprise, which is **absolutely liable** (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987).

## B-14 · Explain the rule of absolute liability. How does it differ from strict liability?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks; Dec 2024 Q5 (A-24) asked "strict liability" at 4 marks. 20-mark form: C-04.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** *M.C. Mehta* facts and rule 5 · difference 3 · later law 2

### Answer

***M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*** (1987) 1 SCC 395 (the **Oleum Gas Leak** case). Oleum gas escaped from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilisers in Delhi in December 1985; one person died and others were affected. The Court (Bhagwati C.J.) held that *Rylands v. Fletcher* belongs to the 19th century and does not suit modern industry. An enterprise engaged in a **hazardous or inherently dangerous activity** owes an **absolute and non-delegable duty** to the community; if harm results, it is **absolutely liable** to compensate. **None** of the *Rylands* exceptions applies. Compensation must be linked to the **size and capacity** of the enterprise, so as to deter.

| | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
|---|---|---|
| Source | *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) | *M.C. Mehta* (1987) |
| Scope | Non-natural use; escape from land | Hazardous industry; escape not essential |
| Exceptions | Seven | None |
| Damages | Actual loss | Deterrent, linked to size |

**Later law.** *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action* (1996) (polluter pays); Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991; National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, s. 17 (no-fault liability). After Bhopal (1984), the Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985 let the Union sue for victims.

<a id="b-15"></a>
## B-15 · "Harm suffered voluntarily is not actionable, but mere knowledge of the risk is not enough for the application of this rule." Discuss

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q6 (this wording); Dec 2023 Q9 (C-07, 20 marks). 20-mark form: C-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** rule and basis 2 · essentials 2 · knowledge v. consent with cases 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

The rule is ***volenti non fit injuria***: to a willing person no injury is done. A person who freely agrees to run a risk of harm cannot complain when it happens. It is a complete defence.

**Essentials.** (1) Knowledge of the risk; (2) **free and voluntary assent** to run it; (3) the harm is within the scope of consent; (4) capacity, and no fraud.

**Knowledge is not consent** (*volenti*, not *scienti*).
- ***Smith v. Baker*** [1891] AC 325: a workman drilled rock while a crane swung heavy stones overhead. He knew of the danger, and a stone fell on him. The House of Lords held that his knowledge and continuing to work did not mean that he agreed to bear the employer's negligence.
- ***Dann v. Hamilton*** [1939] 1 KB 509: a passenger who knew the driver had been drinking was not *volenti*; she had not agreed to waive her claim.
- ***Nettleship v. Weston*** [1971] 2 QB 691: a driving instructor was not *volenti*; contributory negligence only.
- Contrast ***I.C.I. v. Shatwell*** [1965] AC 656: a shot-firer who joined in a deliberate breach of safety rules was *volenti*.

**Conclusion.** The defence needs agreement; courts prefer contributory negligence.

## B-16 · What are the exceptions to *volenti non fit injuria*?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2025 Q6 (B-15), Dec 2023 Q9 (C-07). 20-mark form: C-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each exception with case 1.5 × 5, introduction and conclusion 2.5

### Answer

The defence fails in the following situations.

1. **Rescue cases.** A rescuer is not *volenti*, since he acts under a moral duty ("danger invites rescue"). *Haynes v. Harwood* [1935] 1 KB 146 (a constable stopped runaway horses); *Baker v. T.E. Hopkins & Son* [1959] 1 WLR 966 (a doctor who went down a well was killed; his dependants recovered). A rash rescuer may bear part of the blame.
2. **Employment.** A worker who continues in dangerous work from need is not *volenti*: *Smith v. Baker*; *Bowater v. Rowley Regis Corporation* [1944] KB 476.
3. **Exclusion of liability for negligence causing personal injury** is ineffective (in England, Unfair Contract Terms Act, 1977, s. 2(1)); and statutory duties cannot be waived.
4. **No informed consent** to medical treatment: treatment without it is a battery (*Samira Kohli*, 2008).
5. **Fraud or lack of capacity** makes consent ineffective (*R v. Williams* [1923] 1 KB 340); illegal acts cannot be consented to.
6. **Consent to the risk of negligence** is not implied from consent to the activity: road passengers (*Dann v. Hamilton*).

**Conclusion.** Because the defence is harsh, courts apply it narrowly and often apportion damages for contributory negligence.

## B-17 · Explain necessity and private defence as defences in tort

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Syllabus line 2 ("necessity"). 20-mark form: C-08.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** necessity with cases 5 · private defence 3 · difference and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Necessity.** An act that would otherwise be a tort is justified if done to avoid a **greater harm** to the defendant, to another or to the public, and if it was reasonable and proportionate.
- *Cope v. Sharpe (No. 2)* [1912] 1 KB 496: entry on land and burning heather to stop the spread of a fire; justified.
- *Mouse's Case* (1608): a casket was thrown overboard in a storm to save the ferry; no liability.
- *Re F* [1990] 2 AC 1: urgent medical treatment of a patient who cannot consent.
- It fails where the harm avoided is not greater, or the danger is of the defendant's making: *Southwark LBC v. Williams* [1971] Ch 734 (homeless squatters); *Esso v. Southport* † (limit).
- BNS s. 19 † (IPC s. 81).

**Private defence.** A person may use **reasonable force** to protect his person or property against a wrongful attack; deadly force to protect property is not allowed (*Bird v. Holbrook*, 1828: spring-gun). A reasonable mistake may suffice. BNS ss. 34–44 † (IPC ss. 96–106).

**Difference.** Necessity answers danger from natural forces or a third party and may harm an innocent person; private defence answers a wrongful attack by the person against whom force is used.

## B-18 · Explain the distinction between inevitable accident and Act of God

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q6 ("Explain the distinction between 'Inevitable accident and act of god.'"); Dec 2025 Q5 (A-36, inevitable accident). 20-mark form: C-09.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** inevitable accident 3 · Act of God 3 · table of differences 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Inevitable accident** is an accident that could not be avoided by ordinary care, caution and skill. *Stanley v. Powell* [1891] 1 QB 86: a bullet glanced off a tree and wounded a beater; no negligence, no liability. *Holmes v. Mather* (1875): horses bolted after being frightened by a dog and injured a pedestrian. *Mansfield v. Weetabix* [1998]: a driver unaware of his hypoglycaemia was not negligent.

**Act of God** is an operation of natural forces so extraordinary that no human foresight could be expected to guard against it, with no human agency. *Nichols v. Marsland* (1876): an unprecedented storm burst artificial lakes. An ordinary heavy rain is not an Act of God (*Greenock Corporation v. Caledonian Railway*, 1917 †).

| | Inevitable accident | Act of God |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | Wider (genus) | Narrower (species) |
| Cause | Human or natural | Natural forces only |
| Test | Not avoidable by reasonable care | Beyond human foresight |
| Example | *Stanley v. Powell* | Rare cyclone, earthquake |

Act of God is a recognised exception to *Rylands v. Fletcher*, but **not** to absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*).

## B-19 · Explain the defence of statutory authority

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Syllabus line 2 ("statutory authority"). 20-mark form: C-08.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** rule 2 · kinds 2 · cases 4 · conditions and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Rule.** An act authorised by a statute is not a tort even if it causes damage that would otherwise be actionable. The injured person has only the remedy, if any, that the statute gives.

**Kinds**
- **Absolute (mandatory):** the statute orders the act or makes harm inevitable; the defence is complete if there is no negligence.
- **Conditional (permissive):** the statute only permits the act; the authority must use reasonable care and the method causing least harm.

**Cases**
- *Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway* (1860): sparks from a locomotive burned the plaintiff's wood; the railway had taken every precaution; no liability.
- *Hammersmith Railway v. Brand* (1869): vibration from trains reduced the value of houses; no action.
- *Metropolitan Asylum District v. Hill* (1881): a smallpox hospital built under a general power was a nuisance; the power was permissive.
- *Geddis v. Bann Reservoir* (1878): no defence for negligence.
- *Allen v. Gulf Oil* [1981] AC 1001: nuisance inevitable from the authorised refinery was not actionable.
- *Municipal Corporation, Ratlam v. Vardhichand* (1980): a body cannot plead lack of funds to avoid its duty.

**Conclusion.** Statutory authority protects what the legislature authorised, not negligence or avoidable harm. A statute cannot violate a fundamental right (Art. 13).

## B-20 · Write short notes on other defences in tort: plaintiff's default, mistake, act of a stranger and judicial acts

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-04 (exceptions to *Rylands*), C-05 (judges). 
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each head 2 marks, introduction and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Plaintiff's default (contributory negligence).** If the plaintiff's own carelessness helped to cause the damage, his damages are reduced in proportion to his fault (*Froom v. Butcher* [1976] QB 286: 25% for not wearing a seat-belt). It was once a complete bar (*Butterfield v. Forrester*, 1809). Under *Rylands* the plaintiff's default is a full defence (*Ponting v. Noakes*).

**Mistake.** Generally **not** a defence in tort. A mistaken belief that land is one's own does not excuse trespass (*Basely v. Clarkson*, 1681); an innocent broker who sold stolen goods was liable for conversion (*Hollins v. Fowler*, 1875). Exceptions: reasonable mistake as to an attack in private defence; honest belief negates deceit (*Derry v. Peek*).

**Act of a stranger.** The defendant is not liable for the act of a third party whom he does not control and could not reasonably foresee or prevent (*Box v. Jubb*, 1879), unless he knowingly allowed the nuisance to continue (*Sedleigh-Denfield v. O'Callaghan*, 1940).

**Judicial acts.** Judges and magistrates are not liable for acts done in good faith in discharge of judicial duty (Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850, s. 1; *Anowar Hussain v. Ajoy Kumar Mukherjee*, 1965).
