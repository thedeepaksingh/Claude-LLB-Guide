## C-10 · Vicarious liability: the main incidents of a master's liability for his servant's wrongs; classes of cases in which one person may sue or be sued for a tort to or by another

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q9 ("What are the main incidents of master's liability for the wrong done by servants in the course of their employment?") and Dec 2025 Q12 ("In what classes of cases and on what grounds may one person sue and be sued for a tort committed to and by another?"), both 20 marks. Short forms: Dec 2023 Q8 (B-21, vicarious liability, master and servant), Dec 2023 Q6 (B-51, contract of service and for service), Dec 2025 Q3 (A-44). Related: A-41–A-50, B-21–B-23.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** general rule and reasons 3 · master and servant: servant, tort, course of employment (with cases) 8 · independent contractors 3 · other relationships 3 · suing for a wrong done to another 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The general rule is that a person answers only for his own wrongs. **Vicarious liability** is the exception: one person is made liable for a tort committed by another because of a **relationship** between them, though he is himself without fault. It rests on three ideas: *qui facit per alium facit per se* (he who acts through another acts himself); *respondeat superior* (let the superior answer), since the employer controls the work and takes its profit; and the employer's greater ability to pay and to insure, so that the victim is more likely to be compensated.

**1. The relationships in which one person is liable for another**

| Relationship | Rule |
|---|---|
| Master and servant | Master liable for torts of the servant in the course of employment (the main case) |
| Principal and agent | Principal liable for the agent's torts within his actual or apparent authority (*Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.* [1912] AC 716; *Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank* (1867)) |
| Partners | Each partner is liable for a wrongful act of another done in the ordinary course of the firm's business (Indian Partnership Act, 1932, ss. 25–26) |
| Company | Liable for the torts of its servants and agents; directors are liable only if they took part |
| Owner and driver | Owner liable if the driver was driving for the owner's purposes or as his agent (*Morgans v. Launchbury* [1973] AC 127) |
| Employer and independent contractor | Generally not liable, with exceptions (part 4) |
| Authorisation, ratification | A person who orders, authorises or ratifies a tort is liable as if he had done it |
| The State | Liable as a master (Art. 300; *Vidhyawati*): see C-11 |
| Parent and child; husband and wife; guardian and ward | **No** vicarious liability from the relationship alone |

**2. Master and servant: the main incidents.** The master is liable if three conditions are met.

*(1) There is a **tort by the servant**.* If the servant committed no tort, the master is not liable. The master and servant are **jointly and severally liable**: the victim may sue either or both, and may recover in full from the master. A master who pays may claim an **indemnity** from the servant, though this is rarely done.

*(2) The wrongdoer is a **servant**.* A servant works under a **contract of service**; an independent contractor works under a **contract for services**. The courts have used several tests:
- **Control test**: does the employer control not only *what* is done but *how* it is done? (*Yewens v. Noakes* (1880); *Dharangadhara Chemical Works v. State of Saurashtra* AIR 1957 SC 264).
- **Integration (organisation) test**: is the worker an integral part of the business? (Denning L.J. in *Stevenson Jordan & Harrison v. MacDonald & Evans* [1952]).
- **Multiple (mixed) test**: look at everything: control, ownership of tools, chance of profit and risk of loss, power to hire and dismiss, payment of wages (*Ready Mixed Concrete v. Minister of Pensions* [1968] 2 QB 497; *Silver Jubilee Tailoring House v. Chief Inspector of Shops* (1974) 3 SCC 498). In India the control test is the main test but not the only one.

| | Servant | Independent contractor |
|---|---|---|
| Contract | *Of* service | *For* services |
| Control | Over what and how | Over the result only |
| Pay | Wages or salary | Fee or price for the job |
| Tools and risk | Employer's | Own |
| Master's liability | **Yes** | **Generally no** |
| Examples | Driver, clerk, shop assistant | Builder, lawyer, doctor in private practice |

*(3) The tort was committed **in the course of employment**.* **Salmond's test**: a wrongful act is in the course of employment if it is (a) a wrongful act **authorised** by the master, or (b) a wrongful and **unauthorised mode of doing some act authorised** by the master. The master is liable even if he forbade the way in which the servant did the work, if the servant was doing what he was employed to do.

| Case | Facts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ***Limpus v. London General Omnibus Co.*** (1862) | A driver raced a rival bus against the company's instructions and caused a collision | Company **liable**: an authorised act (driving) done in a prohibited manner |
| ***Beard v. London General Omnibus Co.*** [1900] 2 QB 530 | A conductor drove the bus, which was not his job, and injured the plaintiff | **Not liable**: driving was outside the conductor's employment |
| ***Poland v. John Parr & Sons*** [1927] 1 KB 236 | A carter struck a boy he believed was stealing from the employer's wagon | **Liable**: done to protect the employer's goods |
| ***Century Insurance v. N.I. Road Transport Board*** [1942] AC 509 | A petrol-lorry driver lit a cigarette while delivering petrol and caused a fire | **Liable**: negligent performance of his duty |
| ***Twine v. Bean's Express*** (1946) | A driver gave a forbidden lift to a hitchhiker, who was hurt in an accident | **Not liable**: the lift was prohibited and not part of the job |
| ***Rose v. Plenty*** [1976] 1 WLR 141 | A milkman, against a ban, let a boy help with deliveries; the boy was injured | **Liable**: the boy was helping with the work; an unauthorised mode of doing it |
| ***Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.*** [1912] AC 716 | A managing clerk fraudulently transferred a client's property to himself | Firm **liable**: the fraud was committed in the course of the authority given to him |
| ***Warren v. Henlys*** [1948] | A petrol attendant, after a quarrel over payment, assaulted a customer | **Not liable**: an act of personal revenge |
| ***Mohamud v. WM Morrison*** [2016] UKSC 11 | A petrol-station employee abused and attacked a customer who asked about a printout | **Liable**: a "close connection" between the job and the wrong |
| ***WM Morrison v. Various Claimants*** [2020] UKSC 12 | An employee leaked colleagues' data to harm the employer | **Not liable**: he was pursuing his own purpose, even though the job gave him the opportunity |

*Rules drawn from the cases.* (i) A **prohibition** limits the work he is employed to do (not liable, as in *Beard* and *Twine*) but does not excuse a prohibited **mode** of doing it (liable, as in *Limpus*). (ii) A servant's **fraud and even crime** can be within the course of employment if it is closely connected with what he is employed to do (*Lloyd v. Grace, Smith*; *Mohamud*). (iii) A wrong done from **personal motive** is outside it (*Warren*; *Morrison* 2020). (iv) A **forbidden lift** to a passenger is outside the course of employment, so the employer is not liable to the passenger (*Twine*).

*Borrowed servant.* A servant lent to another remains the general employer's servant unless control of the *manner* of working passes; the burden of shifting liability is heavy (*Mersey Docks v. Coggins & Griffith* [1947] AC 1). In England the idea has extended to relationships **akin to employment** (*Various Claimants v. Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56).

**3. Indian position.** *State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati* AIR 1962 SC 933 applied the master's liability to the State: a Government jeep, driven rashly by a driver, killed a pedestrian, and the State was held liable as an ordinary employer. Under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, the owner's insurer must pay third-party claims (ss. 146–149), and compensation is claimed under s. 166. The Employees' Compensation Act, 1923 gives no-fault compensation for workplace injury.

**4. Independent contractors.** An employer is **not liable** for the torts of an independent contractor except where (1) he authorised or ratified the wrong; (2) he was negligent in choosing the contractor; (3) the work is **inherently dangerous**, giving a **non-delegable duty** (*Honeywill & Stein v. Larkin Bros.* [1934] 1 KB 191: a photographer's magnesium flash set the cinema on fire); (4) the work affects **support** or the **highway** (*Bower v. Peate* (1876); *Dalton v. Henry Angus* (1881)); (5) there is a non-delegable **statutory duty**; (6) the tort is one of **strict or absolute liability**; (7) the employer interferes and takes control.

**5. Who may sue and be sued for a tort to and by another (Dec 2025 Q12)**

*(A) Being sued for another's tort.* The master for his servant; the principal for his agent; partners for each other; a company for its officers and servants; the employer for his independent contractor (in the cases above); the owner for the driver; persons who authorise, abet or ratify; joint tortfeasors (C-12); and the State (C-11). Parents, guardians and husbands are **not** liable merely by relationship.

*(B) Suing for a tort to another.*
1. **Dependants** (spouse, parent, child) for the death of a person caused by a wrongful act: Fatal Accidents Act, 1855, s. 1A; and the **legal representatives** under the Motor Vehicles Act, s. 166.
2. The **estate**: under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306 the cause of action survives to and against executors, **except** defamation, assault and other personal injuries not causing death.
3. A **next friend or guardian** for a minor or a person of unsound mind (CPC O. XXXII).
4. A **master** for loss of his servant's services (*per quod servitium amisit*); a husband for loss of consortium: now limited †.
5. **Consumer** associations, or one consumer for many with the same interest (CPA 2019, s. 35), and **public-interest litigants** in the High Courts and the Supreme Court (Arts. 226 and 32).

**Conclusion.** A master is liable if his servant commits a tort in the course of employment, whether the act was authorised or an unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act. The courts now look at the close connection between the job and the wrong. An employer is generally not liable for an independent contractor, and the exceptions mostly concern hazardous work and non-delegable duties. The same ideas apply to the State; and the right to sue for a wrong done to another is given by statute to dependants, representatives and guardians.

## C-11 · "The old distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions is no longer invoked to determine the liability of the State." Do you agree? Comment with the help of decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q10 (20 marks; the quoted words). Short forms: B-24, B-25, A-51–A-57. Related: C-10.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** background and Art. 300 3 · P&O to Kasturilal 5 · modern cases and public-law compensation 6 · position now and remaining immunity 3 · procedure and reform 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** In English law "the King can do no wrong", and the Crown could not be sued in tort without its consent until the **Crown Proceedings Act, 1947**. In India the question is governed by **Art. 300(1)** of the Constitution: the Union and the States may sue and be sued "in the like cases as the Dominion of India and the corresponding Provinces" could have been, subject to any law made by Parliament or the legislature. No such law has been made, so the courts have worked out the rule through cases. I largely agree with the statement: the distinction is no longer the test for ordinary torts, but it has not entirely disappeared.

**1. Origin: *P. & O. Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India*** (1861) 5 Bom HCR App 1. A horse of the plaintiff was injured by the negligence of Government servants working in the Government dockyard at Kidderpore, Calcutta †. Peacock C.J. held the Secretary of State liable for acts done in the conduct of an **undertaking that a private person could carry on**, but not for acts done in the exercise of **sovereign powers** (waging war, taking prisoners, and so on). That is the source of the sovereign / non-sovereign distinction. The High Courts applied the distinction for the next century, and Art. 300(1) carried it into the Constitution.

**2. Early Supreme Court cases**
- ***State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati*** AIR 1962 SC 933. A Government jeep, being driven back from a workshop for the Collector's use, ran into and killed a pedestrian through the driver's rash driving. The State was held **liable** like any other employer. After the Constitution, the English doctrine of immunity did not apply; the driver's work was not an exercise of sovereign power.
- ***Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P.*** AIR 1965 SC 1039. Police seized gold and silver from the plaintiff, a dealer in Meerut. The goods were kept in the police store, and the head constable misappropriated them and fled to Pakistan. The Supreme Court held the State **not liable**: the police acted in exercise of **statutory powers of arrest, search and seizure**, which are **sovereign powers** that a private person cannot exercise. Gajendragadkar C.J. said that the law needed change and that the matter should be dealt with by legislation. The decision came to be criticised as harsh and as ignoring the principles of the Constitution.

**3. The shift: from sovereign immunity to constitutional accountability**
- ***Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*** (1983) 4 SCC 141. A man was kept in jail for 14 years after he was acquitted. The Supreme Court, in a petition under Art. 32, ordered **compensation** (₹30,000 as interim relief), treating it as a remedy in **public law** for violation of Art. 21.
- ***Bhim Singh v. State of J&K*** (1985) 4 SCC 677. A legislator was illegally detained to stop him from attending the Assembly; compensation of ₹50,000 was awarded.
- ***Saheli v. Commissioner of Police, Delhi*** (1990) 1 SCC 422. A child was beaten to death by a police officer. The State paid compensation; *Kasturilal* did not apply where the police acted unlawfully and beyond their powers.
- ***Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa*** (1993) 2 SCC 746. A young man died in police custody; his body was found on a railway track. The Court awarded **compensation under Art. 32** as a public-law remedy, and held that the defence of sovereign immunity **does not apply** to violation of fundamental rights. This is without prejudice to a civil suit or prosecution.
- ***N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v. State of A.P.*** (1994) 6 SCC 205. Foodgrains seized under a control order were left to rot through negligence, and the owner sued. The State was **liable**. *Kasturilal* was **confined to its own facts**; the Court said that the true area of sovereign powers is now limited to acts of State and a few high-level functions, and that negligent storage of seized goods is not one of them.
- ***D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal*** (1997) 1 SCC 416. Guidelines for arrest and detention; compensation for custodial death is available irrespective of the sovereign nature of the function.
- ***Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of W.B.*** (1996) 4 SCC 37. A man injured in a train accident was refused treatment by government hospitals. Failure to provide emergency treatment violated Art. 21; the State paid compensation (₹25,000).
- ***State of A.P. v. Challa Ramakrishna Reddy*** (2000) 5 SCC 712 †. A prisoner was killed by a gang in a sub-jail after the authorities failed to protect him; the State could not plead sovereign immunity for failing to protect the life of a person in custody.
- ***Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das*** (2000) 2 SCC 465. A Bangladeshi woman was raped by railway employees at the Railways' Yatri Niwas at Howrah. The Railways (Union) was liable to pay compensation (₹10 lakh); the right to life under Art. 21 extends to non-citizens.

**4. The position today.** Taking the cases together:
1. For ordinary torts (negligent driving, hospitals, storage, public works, railways, municipal bodies), the State is liable like any employer, **without asking** whether the function was "sovereign" (*Vidhyawati*, *Nagendra Rao*).
2. For violations of **fundamental rights**, particularly custodial violence, there is **no sovereign immunity** and compensation is a public-law remedy under Arts. 32 and 226 (*Nilabati*; *Rudul Sah*). It is in addition to a private-law remedy.
3. **Some immunity remains.** *Kasturilal* has not been overruled. Core sovereign acts, such as acts of State, defence and war, and legislative and judicial functions, are still outside tort liability; there are special protections for **judges** (Judges (Protection) Act, 1985), for **judicial officers' acts done in good faith**, and for the President and Governors (Art. 361). Many statutes protect acts done in good faith under them.
4. The Supreme Court has said that the old distinction is unsatisfactory, but it has not formally abolished it.

**5. Procedure.** A suit against the Government needs **two months' prior notice** under **CPC s. 80** (a suit may be filed with leave in an urgent case, s. 80(2)); it is brought against the Union of India or the State (Art. 300; CPC s. 79).

**6. Reform.** The Law Commission's First Report (1956) recommended that the State be liable like a private employer; a Government Liability Bill (1965) lapsed †. The law is judge-made.

| Sovereign (immunity possible) | Non-sovereign (liable) |
|---|---|
| Waging war, defence, acts of State | Running railways, hospitals, transport, public works |
| Legislation and administration of justice | Trading and commercial undertakings |
| Statutory police powers of arrest and seizure (*Kasturilal*; now doubted) | Negligent driving of a Government vehicle (*Vidhyawati*); negligent storage (*Nagendra Rao*) |

**Conclusion.** The statement is substantially correct. From *P. & O.* (1861) to *Kasturilal* (1965) the sovereign/non-sovereign test decided the matter. Since *Nilabati* (1993) and *Nagendra Rao* (1994) it has been confined to a small core of acts, and fundamental-rights violations attract compensation whatever the nature of the function. A clear statute, as the Supreme Court has urged since 1965, is still needed.

## C-12 · What do you mean by joint tortfeasors? Discuss the nature and extent of the liability of joint tortfeasors, citing relevant case laws

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q12 and Dec 2023 Q10 (both 20 marks, same wording). Short form: Dec 2025 Q4 (A-58, "What is meant by 'Joint Tortfeasors'?"). Related: A-58–A-62, B-26.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · when persons are joint tortfeasors (The Koursk categories) 5 · nature of liability: joint and several 3 · judgment, release, contribution, indemnity 6 · composite negligence and India 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Where one wrongful act injures a plaintiff, it is easy to say who must pay. Where several persons are concerned in the wrong, the law has to say how far each is liable and how they stand to each other. This is the law of **joint tortfeasors**.

**1. Meaning.** Joint tortfeasors are **two or more persons who are responsible for the same tort**, so that the cause of action against each is the same. In Winfield's words, persons are joint tortfeasors when the cause of action against each is the same, namely the same tort. They are distinct from **concurrent (several, independent) tortfeasors**, whose separate wrongs cause the same damage.

**2. When are persons joint tortfeasors?** Scrutton L.J. in ***The Koursk*** [1924] P 140 identified the cases:
1. **Agent and principal**, and **master and servant**, where the master is vicariously liable for the servant's tort (see C-10).
2. **Breach of a joint duty**: two persons under a joint duty of care or a joint statutory duty.
3. **Concerted action or common design**: persons who act together in furtherance of a common plan, as in a joint trespass, a conspiracy, or a joint assault or defamation. In ***Brooke v. Bool*** [1928] 2 KB 578 two persons looked for a gas leak with a naked flame and caused an explosion; both were held liable because they were acting together.
Also included: persons who **authorise, instigate or ratify** a wrong.
Persons who act **independently** and happen to cause the same damage are **not** joint tortfeasors but concurrent tortfeasors (***Thompson v. London County Council*** [1899] 1 QB 840; ***Drinkwater v. Kimber*** [1952] 2 QB 281: two negligent drivers whose separate negligence combined to injure a pedestrian).

**3. Nature of the liability**
1. **Joint and several.** Each is liable for the **whole** damage. The plaintiff may sue all together or any one, and recover the whole from any, but only once. He cannot collect more than his loss.
2. **Joinder.** All may be joined as defendants where the right to relief arises out of the same act or transaction (CPC O. I r. 3), but the plaintiff is not bound to sue all.
3. **Master and servant** are liable jointly and severally, so the plaintiff may recover from either.

**4. Incidents of the liability**

*(a) Judgment.* At common law, a **judgment against one** joint tortfeasor barred an action against the others, even if it was not satisfied (*Brinsmead v. Harrison* (1872) LR 7 CP 547), because there was a single cause of action. In England this was reversed by the Law Reform (Married Women and Tortfeasors) Act, 1935 and the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act, 1978, s. 3, so that judgments against one do not bar proceedings against others (the damages recovered in the later suit are limited by the earlier). India has no statute on the point, and I could not confirm how the Indian courts have settled it †.

*(b) Release.* A **release** of one joint tortfeasor releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor* (1614)), because the single cause of action is discharged. A **covenant not to sue** one does not release the others. A settlement accepted in full satisfaction of the whole claim ends the claim against the others (*Jameson v. CEGB* [2000] 1 AC 455).

*(c) Contribution.* The old rule was that there was **no contribution** between wrongdoers (*Merryweather v. Nixan* (1799) 8 TR 186: a tortfeasor who had paid all the damages could not recover a share from the other). In England the 1935 and 1978 Acts allow a tortfeasor who has paid more than his share to recover contribution from others liable for the same damage, in the amount the court finds "just and equitable" having regard to the responsibility of each. India has no general statute; the courts apply the English principle of equitable contribution, except where the claimant was a knowing wrongdoer †.

*(d) Indemnity.* A master who has been made liable for the servant's tort may claim an **indemnity** from him. An agent who commits a tort at the principal's request is entitled to indemnity, unless he knew it was unlawful. Compare the Contract Act, ss. 222–223 † (the agent's right to indemnity).

**5. Composite negligence in motor-accident claims (India).** Where two vehicles collide and both drivers are negligent, the injured third party may sue **either or both** and recover the **whole** amount from any; the tribunal need not apportion between them (*T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan* (2008) 3 SCC 748; *Khenyei v. New India Assurance* (2015) 9 SCC 273 †). This differs from **contributory negligence**, where the plaintiff's own fault reduces his damages.

| | Joint tortfeasors | Concurrent tortfeasors |
|---|---|---|
| Cause of action | One tort | Separate wrongs, one damage |
| Examples | Master and servant; common design; joint duty | Two drivers colliding with a pedestrian |
| Liability | Joint and several | Several, each for the whole damage (or for his share if it can be separated) |
| Release of one | Releases all | Does not release others, unless full satisfaction |
| Judgment against one | Bar at common law; changed by statute in England | No bar |

**Conclusion.** Joint tortfeasors are persons answerable for the same wrong: as master and servant, as partners in a joint duty, or as parties to a common design. Each is liable for all the damage, and the plaintiff may choose whom to sue. English statutes have removed the harsh rules on judgment and contribution; India relies on the common law and on motor-claims cases for composite negligence.
