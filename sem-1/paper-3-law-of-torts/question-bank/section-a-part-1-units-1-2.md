<a id="a-01"></a>
**A-01 · Meaning of "tort"** · *Related: B-03, C-01*

"Tort" comes from the Latin *tortum*, meaning twisted or wrong, through Norman French. It is a civil wrong, other than a mere breach of contract or trust, for which the law gives a remedy by an action for unliquidated damages. The duty is fixed by law and owed to persons generally.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-02"></a>
**A-02 · Define tort and state its main characteristics** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q1*

Salmond: a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common-law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively a breach of contract or trust. Characteristics: (1) civil wrong; (2) duty fixed by law, owed to persons generally; (3) remedy is unliquidated damages; (4) liability may be with or without fault; (5) mostly uncodified in India.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-03"></a>
**A-03 · Winfield's definition** · *Related: B-03, C-01*

Winfield: tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; such duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressible by an action for unliquidated damages. It stresses the source of the duty (the law, not agreement) and its generality (owed to everyone), which separates tort from contract.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-04"></a>
**A-04 · Salmond's definition** · *Related: B-03, C-01*

Salmond defines tort as a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common-law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation. It defines by the remedy and excludes contract and trust. The Limitation Act, 1963, s. 2(m) is similar.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-05"></a>
**A-05 · Difference between tort and crime** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q2*

A tort is a wrong to a private person, redressed by an action for damages to compensate him. A crime is a wrong to society, prosecuted by the State and punished by imprisonment, fine or community service. The duty in tort is fixed by law and owed to individuals; a crime needs *mens rea* usually. One act (assault, defamation) may be both.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-06"></a>
**A-06 · Difference between tort and contract** · *Related: B-02, C-01*

In tort the duty is fixed by law and owed to persons generally; in contract it is created by the parties' agreement and owed to a party. Tort needs no privity. Damages in tort are unliquidated; motive may matter. A minor may be liable in tort but his contract is void (*Mohori Bibee*). The same facts may give both claims.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-07"></a>
**A-07 · Pigeon-hole theory** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q2*

Associated with Salmond, it holds that there is liability only if the harm falls within a recognised tort (trespass, nuisance, defamation and so on), as papers fit into pigeon-holes: "the law of torts". Winfield and Pollock preferred a general principle that unjustified harm is actionable. The courts take a middle course and add new torts by analogy (*Donoghue v. Stevenson*).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-08"></a>
**A-08 · Elements of a tort** · *Related: B-03*

(1) A wrongful act or omission by the defendant; (2) a legal duty owed to the plaintiff and its breach; (3) legal damage to the plaintiff, that is, violation of a legal right; (4) a legal remedy, usually damages. Some torts (trespass, libel) are actionable without proof of loss; others (negligence, nuisance) need damage.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-09"></a>
**A-09 · Legal damage; torts actionable *per se*** · *Related: B-05, C-02*

Legal damage means the violation of a legal right, whether or not loss follows. Torts actionable *per se*, without proof of actual loss, include trespass to land, assault, battery, false imprisonment and libel. A trespasser who harms nothing is still liable for at least nominal damages. In negligence, nuisance, deceit and slander, damage is part of the tort.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-10"></a>
**A-10 · *Damnum sine injuria*** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q4*

It means damage without legal injury: the plaintiff suffers loss but no legal right is violated, so he cannot sue. Examples: *Gloucester Grammar School Case* (1410), where a rival school was opened; *Mogul Steamship* (1889), lawful competition; *Bradford Corporation v. Pickles* (1895), use of one's own land; *Ushaben* (1978), hurt religious feelings.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-11"></a>
**A-11 · Principle underlying *damnum sine injuria*** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q5*

The principle is that liability depends on the violation of a legal right, not on loss alone. A person who uses his own rights lawfully, such as a trader competing or a landowner drawing water from his own land, is not liable if others suffer. Such losses are the price of living in society. Moral or social wrongs without legal injury are not actionable.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-12"></a>
**A-12 · *Injuria sine damno*** · *Related: B-05, C-02*

It means violation of a legal right without actual loss. The plaintiff may sue and gets at least nominal damages. *Ashby v. White* (1703): a voter wrongly refused could sue though his candidate won. *Marzetti v. Williams* (1830): a banker wrongly dishonoured a cheque. *Constantine v. Imperial Hotels* (1944): a hotel refused a guest. Trespass and libel are actionable *per se*.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-13"></a>
**A-13 · *Ubi jus ibi remedium*** · *Related: C-02*

"Where there is a right, there is a remedy." If the law recognises a right, it will provide a remedy for its violation. The maxim explains *injuria sine damno*: the right is protected without proof of loss. It also supports the growth of new torts. The converse is that where no legal right is violated, there is no remedy (*damnum sine injuria*).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-14"></a>
**A-14 · Reasons for unliquidated damages in tort** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q5*

Damages in tort are unliquidated because the amount is not fixed in advance by the parties or by law. The harm (pain, loss of reputation, loss of earnings, damage to property) varies from case to case, so the court assesses compensation on the facts. In contract, damages may be liquidated by agreement (Contract Act, s. 74). This flexibility lets the court do justice in each case.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-15"></a>
**A-15 · Kinds of damages** · *Related: B-10, C-06*

Nominal (right violated, no loss); contemptuous (a trivial sum for a worthless claim); general (presumed loss) and special (proved loss); aggravated (increased by insult or malice); exemplary or punitive (to punish and deter: *Rookes v. Barnard*; in India as public-law compensation, *Nilabati Behera*). The plaintiff must mitigate his loss.

*Word count: 49*

<a id="a-16"></a>
**A-16 · Injunction** · *Related: B-10*

An injunction is a court order to refrain from, or to undo, a wrong. It may be temporary (pending trial) or perpetual, prohibitory or mandatory (Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 36–42; CPC O. XXXIX). It is the main remedy for continuing wrongs such as nuisance and trespass. The court may award damages instead if an injunction would be oppressive (*Shelfer*).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-17"></a>
**A-17 · *Actio personalis moritur cum persona*** · *Related: B-07, C-06*

"A personal action dies with the person." At common law a right to sue in tort ended on the death of either party. In India the Indian Succession Act, 1925, s. 306 preserves causes of action, except for defamation, assault and other personal injuries not causing death. Dependants may sue under the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-18"></a>
**A-18 · Foreign tort** · *Related: B-08, C-06*

A foreign tort is a wrong committed abroad and sued on in the forum. Under *Phillips v. Eyre* (1870), the act must be (1) actionable as a tort in the forum, and (2) not justifiable by the law of the place where it was done. This is double actionability; *Boys v. Chaplin* (1971) allowed a flexible exception. India follows the common law †.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-19"></a>
**A-19 · Malice in law and malice in fact** · *Related: B-09, C-03*

Malice in law: a wrongful act done intentionally without just cause or excuse; it is implied from the act. Malice in fact: ill-will or improper motive, such as spite; it must be proved. Malice in fact matters in malicious prosecution, conspiracy, and to defeat qualified privilege or fair comment in defamation.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-20"></a>
**A-20 · Motive in tort** · *Related: B-09, C-03*

Motive is the reason for acting, such as spite or gain. Generally it is irrelevant: a lawful act does not become wrongful because of a bad motive (*Bradford Corporation v. Pickles*; *Allen v. Flood*). Exceptions: where malice is an element of the tort (malicious prosecution, conspiracy), where it defeats a defence (privilege), in nuisance (*Christie v. Davey*) and in assessing damages.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-21"></a>
**A-21 · Fault, intention, negligence and recklessness** · *Related: C-03*

Fault is blameworthy conduct. Intention is desiring a consequence or knowing it is certain. Negligence is failure to take reasonable care where there is a duty. Recklessness is knowing the risk and going on regardless; in deceit, a statement made careless whether true or false is fraud (*Derry v. Peek*). Liability generally needs fault, but strict liability does not.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-22"></a>
**A-22 · Minors and persons of unsound mind** · *Related: B-11, C-05*

A minor sues through a next friend (CPC O. XXXII) and may be sued if old enough to understand the wrong; the standard is that of a child of his age. A person of unsound mind sues through a guardian and is generally liable, except for torts needing a particular intent †. A tort cannot be used to enforce a void contract.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-23"></a>
**A-23 · Corporations, the State and foreign sovereigns** · *Related: B-11, C-05*

A company sues and is sued in its own name and is liable for torts of its servants in the course of employment. The Union and States can be sued (Art. 300), after two months' notice under CPC s. 80. A foreign sovereign or ambassador cannot be sued without the Central Government's consent (CPC s. 86) or in the face of diplomatic immunity.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-24"></a>
**A-24 · Strict liability** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q5*

Strict liability is liability without proof of fault. Under *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868), a person who for his own purposes brings and keeps on his land something likely to do mischief if it escapes is answerable for all damage that naturally results, however careful he was. It requires non-natural use and escape, and is subject to exceptions. In India, absolute liability applies to hazardous industries.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-25"></a>
**A-25 · The rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*** · *Related: B-12, C-04*

Rylands's contractors built a reservoir over disused mine shafts; water flooded Fletcher's mine. Blackburn J. held that a person who brings on his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril and is *prima facie* liable for all damage that naturally follows. The House of Lords (1868) added non-natural use of land.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-26"></a>
**A-26 · Non-natural use of land** · *Related: B-12, C-04*

Non-natural use is a special use that brings increased danger to others, not the ordinary use of land (*Rickards v. Lothian*, 1913). Water in domestic pipes is a natural use; storing large quantities of water or chemicals for industry is non-natural. The test varies with time and place. *Transco v. Stockport* (2003) confined the rule to extraordinary and unusual uses.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-27"></a>
**A-27 · Exceptions to *Rylands v. Fletcher*** · *Related: B-13, C-04*

(1) Act of God (*Nichols v. Marsland*); (2) consent of the plaintiff or common benefit (*Carstairs v. Taylor*); (3) default of the plaintiff (*Ponting v. Noakes*); (4) act of a stranger (*Box v. Jubb*); (5) statutory authority; (6) natural use of land. These exceptions do not apply to the Indian rule of absolute liability.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-28"></a>
**A-28 · Absolute liability** · *Related: B-14, C-04*

In *M.C. Mehta* (1987) the Supreme Court held that an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is absolutely liable to compensate for harm resulting from it. It owes a non-delegable duty to the community, none of the *Rylands* exceptions applies, escape is not essential, and compensation is related to the size and capacity of the enterprise to deter.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-29"></a>
**A-29 · *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987)** · *Related: B-14, C-04*

Oleum gas leaked from a Shriram Foods and Fertilisers unit in Delhi in December 1985; one person died and others suffered. The Supreme Court (Bhagwati C.J.) said that *Rylands v. Fletcher* is unsuited to modern industry and laid down absolute liability for hazardous enterprises, with exemplary compensation proportionate to the enterprise's size and resources.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-30"></a>
**A-30 · Bhopal gas leak (1984)** · *Related: B-14*

In December 1984 methyl isocyanate leaked from the Union Carbide plant in Bhopal, killing thousands. The Bhopal Gas Leak Disaster (Processing of Claims) Act, 1985 let the Union sue for the victims. The Supreme Court approved a US $470 million settlement in 1989. The disaster prompted the doctrine of absolute liability and the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-31"></a>
**A-31 · *Volenti non fit injuria*** · *Related: B-15, C-07*

"To a willing person no injury is done." A person who freely agrees to run a risk of harm cannot complain of it. Essentials: knowledge of the risk, voluntary assent, harm within the scope of consent, capacity. It is a complete defence. Examples: sport (*Hall v. Brooklands*), *I.C.I. v. Shatwell* (1965). It is narrowly applied; contributory negligence is preferred.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-32"></a>
**A-32 · Knowledge is not consent** · *Related: B-15, C-07*

*Volenti* requires consent, not mere knowledge of a risk (*volenti*, not *scienti*). In *Smith v. Baker* (1891), a workman knew that stones swung over him, but did not thereby agree to bear his employer's negligence. In *Dann v. Hamilton* (1939), a passenger who knew that the driver had been drinking did not waive her claim.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-33"></a>
**A-33 · Rescue cases** · *Related: B-16, C-07*

A rescuer who is hurt in saving another is not *volenti*, since he acts under a moral duty ("danger invites rescue"). *Haynes v. Harwood* (1935): a constable stopped runaway horses. *Baker v. T.E. Hopkins* (1959): a doctor who went down a well to save workmen was killed; his dependants recovered. A rash rescuer may bear part of the blame.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-34"></a>
**A-34 · Necessity** · *Related: B-17, C-08*

An act that would otherwise be a tort is justified if done to avoid a greater harm, and reasonably. *Cope v. Sharpe (No. 2)* (1912): entry to stop a fire. *Mouse's Case* (1608): a casket thrown overboard in a storm. *Re F* (1990): urgent medical treatment. It fails where the harm avoided is not greater (*Southwark v. Williams*, 1971). See BNS s. 19 †.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-35"></a>
**A-35 · Private defence** · *Related: B-17, C-08*

A person may use reasonable force to protect his body or property, or another's, against a wrongful attack. Force must be proportionate; deadly force to protect property is not allowed (*Bird v. Holbrook*, spring-gun case). A reasonable but mistaken belief in an attack may suffice. The criminal law is in BNS ss. 34–44 (IPC ss. 96–106) †.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-36"></a>
**A-36 · Inevitable accident** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q5*

An accident that could not have been avoided by ordinary care, caution and skill. It is a defence to torts needing fault. *Stanley v. Powell* (1891): a bullet glanced off a tree and wounded a beater; no liability. *Holmes v. Mather* (1875): horses bolted, frightened by a dog. It is a wider category than Act of God, and is no defence to absolute liability.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-37"></a>
**A-37 · Act of God** · *Related: B-18, C-09*

An operation of natural forces so extraordinary that no human foresight could be expected to guard against it, with no human agency. *Nichols v. Marsland* (1876): an unprecedented storm burst artificial lakes and carried away bridges; no liability. Ordinary heavy rain is not an Act of God. It is an exception to *Rylands v. Fletcher*, but not to absolute liability.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-38"></a>
**A-38 · Statutory authority** · *Related: B-19, C-08*

An act authorised by statute is not a tort, even if it causes damage that would otherwise be actionable (*Vaughan v. Taff Vale*, 1860; *Hammersmith Railway v. Brand*, 1869). The defence is complete where the statute orders the act; where it only permits it, the authority must use reasonable care. It is not a defence to negligence (*Geddis*). The injured party gets only the statutory remedy.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-39"></a>
**A-39 · Plaintiff's default; mistake** · *Related: B-20*

If the plaintiff's own act causes the harm, he cannot recover (*Ponting v. Noakes*); where his carelessness only contributes, damages are reduced (contributory negligence). Mistake is generally no defence in tort: a trespasser who believes the land is his own is liable (*Basely v. Clarkson*), and an innocent seller of stolen goods is liable for conversion (*Hollins v. Fowler*).

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-40"></a>
**A-40 · Judicial acts** · *Related: B-20, C-05*

A judge or magistrate is not liable for acts done in good faith in the discharge of judicial duty, even if wrong, so that judicial independence is protected. In India the Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850, s. 1 gives this protection, and the Judges (Protection) Act, 1985 extends it (*Anowar Hussain v. Ajoy Kumar Mukherjee*, 1965). Malice or lack of jurisdiction in bad faith removes it.

*Word count: 66*
