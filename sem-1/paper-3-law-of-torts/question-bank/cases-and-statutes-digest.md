## 2A · Unit 1 · Nature of tort, damage and malice, strict and absolute liability

### 1. *Ashby v. White* (1703) 2 Ld Raym 938 · King's Bench, reversed by the House of Lords (1704)
- **Facts.** A returning officer wrongfully refused to register Ashby's vote at a parliamentary election at Aylesbury. The candidate Ashby supported won anyway, so Ashby lost nothing material.
- **Issue.** Is there an action when a legal right is infringed but no loss follows?
- **Held.** Yes. The King's Bench majority refused a remedy, but the House of Lords agreed with Holt C.J.'s dissent: denying a man his vote is an injury for which damages lie.
- **Principle.** *Injuria sine damno* is actionable. Where there is a right there is a remedy (*ubi jus ibi remedium*).
- **After.** *Marzetti v. Williams* (1830) (a bank wrongly dishonoured a cheque; nominal damages) and *Constantine v. Imperial Hotels* [1944] KB 693 (a hotel refused a room to the cricketer Learie Constantine; five pounds †). In India the idea reappears when courts award compensation for violation of fundamental rights (*Bhim Singh*, 2C).
- **Used in:** A-12, B-04, B-05, B-10, C-02, C-06.

### 2. *Bradford Corporation v. Pickles* [1895] AC 587 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Pickles sank a shaft on his own land and drew off underground water that percolated towards Bradford Corporation's land. The Corporation said he did it to force it to buy him out at a high price.
- **Issue.** Can a lawful act be made wrongful by a bad motive?
- **Held.** No. The House of Lords refused an injunction: a landowner may take the water percolating in his own land, whatever his motive (*Chasemore v. Richards* (1859); *Acton v. Blundell* (1843)).
- **Principle.** *Damnum sine injuria.* Motive is irrelevant to an act that is the exercise of a legal right.
- **After.** *Allen v. Flood* [1898] AC 1 applied the same view to trade disputes. But malice does matter where it turns the act into a nuisance (*Christie v. Davey*) or an unlawful conspiracy (*Quinn v. Leathem*).
- **Used in:** A-10, A-20, B-04, B-09, C-02, C-03.

### 3. *Allen v. Flood* [1898] AC 1 · House of Lords (6:3)
- **Facts.** Two shipwrights, Flood and Taylor, were employed by an iron company on a day-to-day basis. Allen, a trade-union delegate, told the employer the union men would stop work if the two stayed. The employer discharged them.
- **Issue.** Is a person liable for maliciously inducing an employer to end an employment that he was free to end?
- **Held.** No. The employer was entitled to end the employment, and an act lawful in itself is not made unlawful by a bad motive.
- **Principle.** There is no tort of maliciously inducing a person to do what he is lawfully free to do.
- **After.** *Quinn v. Leathem* [1901] AC 495 (a combination of union officials to injure a butcher was an actionable conspiracy) was distinguished; *Crofter Hand Woven Harris Tweed Co. v. Veitch* [1942] AC 435 (a combination to protect legitimate trade interests is not actionable).
- **Used in:** A-20, B-09, C-02, C-03.

### 4. *Mogul Steamship Co. v. McGregor, Gow & Co.* [1892] AC 25 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A group of shipping firms (a "conference") offered low rates and rebates to shippers who dealt only with them, in order to drive the Mogul Steamship Co. out of the China tea trade.
- **Issue.** Is a combination to ruin a rival in trade an actionable wrong?
- **Held.** No. Competition pursued by lawful means for the defendants' own trade interests is not actionable, even if it ruins a rival.
- **Principle.** Trade competition by lawful means, without any unlawful act, is justified; damage alone does not make a tort.
- **After.** In India the Competition Act, 2002 now regulates anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance (ss. 3–4).
- **Used in:** A-10, B-04, C-02, C-03.

### 5. *Christie v. Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316 · Chancery Division (North J.)
- **Facts.** A music teacher gave lessons at home. The neighbour, annoyed, banged on the party wall, shrieked, and beat trays whenever lessons were given.
- **Issue.** Is deliberate noise made out of spite a nuisance even if the same noise might be lawful?
- **Held.** Yes. An injunction was granted against the neighbour, because the noises were made maliciously to annoy.
- **Principle.** Malice may turn an otherwise lawful act into a nuisance.
- **After.** *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v. Emmett* [1936] 2 KB 468 (a neighbour had guns fired near the boundary to frighten breeding silver foxes; injunction and damages) is the usual companion case.
- **Used in:** A-20, B-09, C-03, C-17.

### 6. *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) LR 3 HL 330 · House of Lords, affirming (1866) LR 1 Ex 265 (Blackburn J.)
- **Facts.** Fletcher employed independent contractors to build a reservoir on his land. They found old coal shafts beneath it, connected to Rylands' mine workings, and did not block them. When the reservoir filled, water burst through into the neighbouring mine and flooded it.
- **Issue.** Is a landowner liable, without proof of negligence, for damage caused by something dangerous that escapes from his land?
- **Held.** Yes. Blackburn J.: a person who brings on his land for his own purposes, and keeps there, anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it at his peril, and is answerable for all the damage that is the natural consequence of its escape. Lord Cairns added that the use of the land must be **non-natural**.
- **Principle.** Strict liability for the escape of a dangerous thing from land used non-naturally. Defences: consent, common benefit, act of a stranger, Act of God, statutory authority, default of the plaintiff.
- **After.** Narrowed by *Read v. Lyons* (escape needed), *Cambridge Water* (foreseeability of damage) and *Transco* (extraordinary and unusual use; no claim for personal injury). In India, replaced for hazardous industry by **absolute liability** in *M.C. Mehta* (1987).
- **Used in:** A-24, A-25, A-27, A-28, A-29, A-37, B-06, B-12, B-13, B-14, B-18, B-20, B-23, B-39, C-03, C-04, C-09, C-17.

### 7. *Nichols v. Marsland* (1876) 2 Ex D 1 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** The defendant made ornamental lakes by damming a natural stream. An unprecedented rainstorm burst the dams and washed away four bridges belonging to the plaintiff.
- **Issue.** Is the defendant liable under *Rylands v. Fletcher* when the escape is caused by an extraordinary natural event?
- **Held.** No. The rain was an Act of God: an extraordinary natural event that no human foresight could have guarded against.
- **Principle.** Act of God is a defence to strict liability.
- **After.** *Greenock Corporation v. Caledonian Railway* [1917] AC 556 (heavy rain after the defendant diverted a stream was not Act of God †) shows how strictly the defence is applied.
- **Used in:** A-27, A-37, B-13, B-18, C-04, C-09.

### 8. *Read v. J. Lyons & Co.* [1947] AC 156 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Miss Read, a Ministry inspector, was injured by an explosion in the defendants' munitions factory during the war. There was no proof of negligence.
- **Issue.** Does *Rylands v. Fletcher* apply to personal injury within the defendant's premises, where nothing escapes?
- **Held.** No. There must be an **escape** from a place where the defendant has occupation or control to a place outside it. The explosion occurred inside the factory.
- **Principle.** Escape is an essential element of the rule in *Rylands v. Fletcher*.
- **After.** *Transco* (2003) accepted that the rule does not give damages for personal injury. India's absolute liability rule in *M.C. Mehta* does not need an escape from the premises, though the harm must arise from the hazardous activity.
- **Used in:** B-12, C-04.

### 9. *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987) 1 SCC 395 · Supreme Court, 5 judges (Bhagwati C.J.) — the Oleum gas leak case
- **Facts.** In December 1985 oleum gas leaked from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilizer Industries in a crowded part of Delhi. A lawyer died and several people were affected. The petition was brought during a challenge to the plant's reopening.
- **Issue.** What is the liability of an enterprise carrying on a hazardous activity for harm to those affected, and does the rule of *Rylands v. Fletcher* apply in India?
- **Held.** The Court refused to follow the English exceptions. An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity owes an **absolute and non-delegable duty** to the community to ensure that no harm results. It is **absolutely liable**, with none of the *Rylands* exceptions, and compensation should be proportionate to the **size and capacity** of the enterprise (the "deep pocket" idea).
- **Principle.** **Absolute liability** for hazardous industry: no escape needed, no exceptions, compensation correlated to capacity to pay.
- **After.** Applied and extended in *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action* (1996) and *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) (polluter pays and the precautionary principle). Statutes: Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991; National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. After Bhopal: *Union Carbide v. Union of India* (1989) 1 SCC 674 (settlement of US $470 million); curative petition dismissed on 14 March 2023 †.
- **Used in:** A-24, A-27, A-28, A-29, A-30, A-36, A-37, B-06, B-12, B-13, B-14, B-18, B-23, C-01, C-03, C-04, C-09, C-10.

### 10. *Cambridge Water Co. v. Eastern Counties Leather plc* [1994] 2 AC 264 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A tannery used a chlorinated solvent and over many years small spillages seeped into the ground. The solvent contaminated the water at Cambridge Water's borehole more than a mile away.
- **Issue.** Is the defendant liable under *Rylands v. Fletcher* for damage of a kind that he could not reasonably foresee?
- **Held.** No. Foreseeability of damage of the relevant type is a prerequisite of damages under *Rylands v. Fletcher*, which Lord Goff treated as an extension of nuisance. The pollution was not foreseeable.
- **Principle.** *Rylands* is strict as to the escape but needs reasonable foresight of the kind of harm.
- **After.** Lord Goff added that liability for such risks was better dealt with by legislation. India chose the stricter rule of absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*).
- **Used in:** B-12, C-04.

### 11. *Transco plc v. Stockport MBC* [2003] UKHL 61, [2004] 2 AC 1 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A leak from a water pipe supplying a block of flats washed away an embankment and exposed Transco's gas main. Transco claimed the cost of protecting it.
- **Issue.** Was the supply of water to a block of flats a "non-natural user" under *Rylands v. Fletcher*?
- **Held.** No. The rule applies only to an **extraordinary and unusual** use of land. An ordinary domestic water supply is not. Lord Bingham doubted whether the rule should survive, and stated that it does not give damages for personal injury.
- **Principle.** Non-natural use means extraordinary and unusual, judged at the time and place.
- **After.** In the United Kingdom the rule is now very limited. Indian courts rely on absolute liability for hazardous industries.
- **Used in:** A-26, C-04.

### 12. *Ponting v. Noakes* [1894] 2 QB 281 · Queen's Bench Division
- **Facts.** The plaintiff's horse reached over the boundary fence and ate leaves from the defendant's yew tree, and died.
- **Issue.** Is the owner of a poisonous tree liable under *Rylands v. Fletcher* when a trespassing animal eats it?
- **Held.** No. Nothing had escaped from the defendant's land; the horse had intruded onto it.
- **Principle.** The plaintiff must show an escape. Harm caused by the plaintiff's own intrusion is not within the rule.
- **Used in:** A-27, A-39, B-13, B-20, C-04.

### 13. *Box v. Jubb* (1879) 4 Ex D 76 and *Rickards v. Lothian* [1913] AC 263 (PC) · defence: act of a stranger
- **Facts.** *Box*: the overflow of the defendant's reservoir was caused by a third party emptying water into it. *Rickards*: an unknown person deliberately blocked a basin's waste pipe and left the tap running, flooding the premises below.
- **Issue.** Is the defendant liable under *Rylands* for escape caused by an act that he could not foresee or control?
- **Held.** No in each case. The escape was caused by the unforeseeable act of a stranger. In *Rickards* the Privy Council added that a water supply for ordinary domestic use is a natural use.
- **Principle.** Act of a stranger is a defence if it could not reasonably have been foreseen or prevented.
- **After.** The defence survives in nuisance if the occupier fails to abate (*Sedleigh-Denfield*, 2E).
- **Used in:** A-26, A-27, B-12, B-13, B-20, C-04.

### 14. *Carstairs v. Taylor* (1871) LR 6 Ex 217 · Exchequer Chamber
- **Facts.** Water was stored on the upper floor of a building for the benefit of all occupiers. A rat gnawed through the box and the water damaged the ground-floor tenant's goods.
- **Issue.** Is the owner liable under *Rylands* when the escape arises from an event for which he is not at fault and the arrangement benefits both parties?
- **Held.** No. The water was there for the common benefit of both, and the escape was not due to the defendant's default.
- **Principle.** Common benefit (consent) is a defence.
- **Used in:** A-27, B-13, C-04.

### 15. *Madras Railway Co. v. Zamindar of Carvetnagaram* (1874) 1 IA 364 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** A tank (reservoir) belonging to the zamindar in the Madras Presidency burst and damaged the railway's line †.
- **Issue.** Does the rule of *Rylands v. Fletcher* apply to ancient irrigation tanks in India?
- **Held.** The Privy Council did not apply the English rule to such tanks †, which were part of the country's irrigation and had been kept as the custom of the land.
- **Principle.** The English rule was adapted to Indian conditions, especially irrigation.
- **After.** Overtaken in hazardous industry by *M.C. Mehta*. Check the report before relying on details.
- **Used in:** C-04.

### 16. *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) 3 SCC 212 · Supreme Court (the Bichhri case)
- **Facts.** Chemical plants at Bichhri village in Rajasthan manufactured acid and discharged highly toxic sludge, which polluted the soil and groundwater of the area.
- **Issue.** Who must bear the cost of restoring the damage?
- **Held.** The industries are absolutely liable for the harm and must pay the cost of remedial measures: the **polluter pays** principle follows from absolute liability.
- **Principle.** Absolute liability plus polluter pays; the Court may direct restoration at the polluter's cost.
- **After.** Followed by *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) 5 SCC 647 (tanneries; precautionary principle and polluter pays are part of Indian environmental law).
- **Used in:** B-14, C-04.

### 17. *Salomon v. A. Salomon & Co. Ltd* [1897] AC 22 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Salomon sold his boot business to a company he had formed, taking shares and debentures. On the company's insolvency, the unsecured creditors claimed that the company was only his agent.
- **Issue.** Is a registered company a legal person distinct from its members?
- **Held.** Yes. The company was a separate legal person, so Salomon, as a secured creditor, ranked before the unsecured creditors.
- **Principle.** A corporation is a separate person: it can sue and be sued in its own name, and is liable in tort for its agents' wrongs.
- **After.** Used in the topic "who may sue and be sued" (corporations, State, minors, insane persons, foreign sovereigns).
- **Used in:** B-11, C-05.

## 2B · Unit 2 · General defences: consent, necessity, accident, statutory authority

### 18. *Smith v. Baker & Sons* [1891] AC 325 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Smith drilled rock in a cutting. A crane swung heavy stones over his head. He knew of the danger and had complained. A stone fell and injured him; the employer had been negligent.
- **Issue.** Does knowledge of a risk amount to consent to it (*volenti non fit injuria*)?
- **Held.** No. Knowledge is not agreement. The maxim is *volenti*, not *scienti*. Smith carried on working because he had to earn a living, not because he agreed to run the risk of his employer's negligence.
- **Principle.** *Volenti* needs a free and voluntary agreement, express or implied, to run the risk, with full knowledge of its nature and extent.
- **After.** Followed in *Bowater v. Rowley Regis Corporation* [1944] KB 476 (a carter ordered to drive a vicious horse was not volenti) and *Thomas v. Quartermaine* (1887) 18 QBD 685 †, the older case that is usually set against it.
- **Used in:** A-32, B-15, B-16, C-07, C-14.

### 19. *Dann v. Hamilton* [1939] 1 KB 509 · King's Bench (Asquith J.)
- **Facts.** Mrs Dann accepted a lift in a car driven by a man she knew had been drinking heavily. The car crashed and she was injured; the driver was killed.
- **Issue.** Does a passenger who knowingly rides with a drunk driver consent to the risk of injury?
- **Held.** No. Knowing the driver was drunk did not mean she agreed to waive her claim for his negligence. The defence failed.
- **Principle.** *Volenti* needs consent to the legal risk of injury without compensation, not just awareness of a danger.
- **After.** The modern approach is to consider contributory negligence instead. In India, the MV Act requires insurance for third-party risks, so the insurer pays (ss. 146–149).
- **Used in:** A-32, B-15, B-16, C-07.

### 20. *I.C.I. Ltd v. Shatwell* [1965] AC 656 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Two brothers, shot-firers in a quarry, tested detonators without taking cover, breaking statutory rules and the employer's orders. George was injured and sued the employer, who was vicariously liable for James's breach.
- **Issue.** Can *volenti* defeat a claim where the plaintiff and fellow-servant both took the risk?
- **Held.** Yes. George had himself joined in the unsafe act, fully understood the risk and agreed to it. The employer was not liable.
- **Principle.** *Volenti* may succeed in an employment case where the employee's own deliberate, informed act caused the harm.
- **After.** Contrast *Smith v. Baker*: there the employee did not join in the unsafe system.
- **Used in:** A-31, B-15, C-07.

### 21. *Nettleship v. Weston* [1971] 2 QB 691 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** A friend agreed to teach the defendant to drive, having confirmed that she was insured. On the third lesson she crashed into a lamp post and injured his knee.
- **Issue.** Is a learner driver judged by a lower standard, and did the instructor consent to the risk?
- **Held.** The learner is judged by the standard of the **competent driver**; the instructor was not *volenti* since he had asked about insurance. Damages were reduced by **50%** for contributory negligence, because he had not kept watch or taken control (‡).
- **Principle.** The standard of care is objective; *volenti* needs agreement to waive the right to sue.
- **After.** Followed in later cases on learner drivers. Compare *Mullin v. Richards* [1998] 1 WLR 1304 (children are judged by the standard of an ordinary child of the same age).
- **Used in:** A-70, B-15, B-29, C-07, C-13.

### 22. *Haynes v. Harwood* [1935] 1 KB 146 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** The defendant's servant left a horse-drawn van unattended in a busy street. A boy threw a stone, the horses bolted, and a police constable, Haynes, was injured stopping them.
- **Issue.** Is a rescuer *volenti*?
- **Held.** No. A rescuer is not volenti if he acts in response to danger created by the defendant's wrong; rescue was a natural and foreseeable result of the negligence.
- **Principle.** "Danger invites rescue" (Cardozo J. in *Wagner v. International Railway*, 1921). The wrongdoer owes a duty to the rescuer.
- **After.** *Baker v. T.E. Hopkins & Son* [1959] 1 WLR 966 (a doctor who went down a well to rescue workmen overcome by fumes, and died, was not volenti; the employer was liable).
- **Used in:** A-33, B-16, C-07.

### 23. *Hall v. Brooklands Auto-Racing Club* [1933] 1 KB 205 · Court of Appeal; *Wooldridge v. Sumner* [1963] 2 QB 43; *Condon v. Basi* [1985] 1 WLR 866 · sports cases
- **Facts.** *Hall*: a spectator was injured when a racing car left the track. *Wooldridge*: a photographer was knocked down by a horse ridden too fast in a show. *Condon*: a footballer broke a leg in a foul tackle.
- **Issue.** Which risks of sport does a participant or spectator accept?
- **Held.** A spectator accepts the ordinary risks (*Hall*), and a competitor is not liable for an error of judgment in the "agony of the moment", unless he shows reckless disregard for spectators' safety (*Wooldridge*). A player who plays below the standard of a reasonable player in that league is liable (*Condon*).
- **Principle.** Consent covers the inherent risks of a lawful sport, not reckless or flagrant breaches of its rules.
- **Used in:** A-31, C-07.

### 24. *Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda* (2008) 2 SCC 1 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The patient consented to a diagnostic laparoscopy under general anaesthesia. While she was unconscious, the doctor removed her uterus and ovaries without consulting her.
- **Issue.** Is consent to a diagnostic procedure consent to a further surgical procedure?
- **Held.** No. Consent must be real, informed and for the specific treatment; an unauthorised extension is a **battery** unless there is an emergency that threatens life. The Court awarded compensation.
- **Principle.** Treatment without consent is a trespass to the person; doctors must disclose the nature of the procedure and the material risks.
- **After.** In England, *Montgomery v. Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11 replaced the *Bolam* test for disclosure with the test of what a reasonable patient would want to know.
- **Used in:** A-82, A-97, B-16, B-35, B-42, C-07, C-16, C-18.

### 25. *Cope v. Sharpe (No. 2)* [1912] 1 KB 496 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** A fire was spreading on the plaintiff's land towards a neighbouring property. The defendant, a gamekeeper, entered the plaintiff's land and burnt a strip of heather to stop it reaching the land that he was protecting.
- **Issue.** Is entry to prevent the spread of fire a trespass?
- **Held.** No. The danger was real and imminent, the steps were reasonable, and the act was necessary to protect the property.
- **Principle.** Private necessity can justify an act that would otherwise be a trespass, if the danger is imminent and the response proportionate.
- **After.** *Southwark LBC v. Williams* [1971] Ch 734 (homeless squatters could not plead necessity: "no man's house will be safe").
- **Used in:** A-34, B-17, C-08.

### 26. *Esso Petroleum Co. v. Southport Corporation* [1956] AC 218 · House of Lords †
- **Facts.** A tanker ran aground in an estuary. The captain discharged oil to save the ship and crew, and the oil fouled the foreshore †.
- **Issue.** Is the discharge to save life and ship justified as public necessity?
- **Held.** The House of Lords held the owners not liable, since the stranding was not due to negligence †. Necessity is not a defence where the danger was caused by the defendant's own fault.
- **Principle.** Necessity is a defence only where the defendant did not cause the danger by his own fault.
- **After.** Check the report for the exact reasoning.
- **Used in:** B-17, C-08, C-17.

### 27. *Holmes v. Mather* (1875) LR 10 Ex 261; *Stanley v. Powell* [1891] 1 QB 86; *Brown v. Kendall* (1850) 60 Mass 292 · inevitable accident
- **Facts.** *Holmes*: the defendant's horses bolted after a dog barked; his groom tried to stop them but the plaintiff was hurt. *Stanley*: a shot glanced off a tree and wounded a beater. *Brown*: the defendant raised a stick to part fighting dogs and hit the plaintiff in the eye.
- **Issue.** Is there liability for harm that was inevitable despite reasonable care?
- **Held.** No liability: trespass and negligence need intention or fault. The plaintiff must prove want of ordinary care (*Brown*).
- **Principle.** Inevitable accident means harm that could not have been avoided by the exercise of ordinary care and skill.
- **After.** *Mansfield v. Weetabix* [1998] 1 WLR 1263 (a driver unaware that he was hypoglycaemic was not negligent).
- **Used in:** A-36, B-18, C-09.

### 28. *Hammersmith & City Railway v. Brand* (1869) LR 4 HL 171; *Vaughan v. Taff Vale Railway* (1860) 5 H&N 679; *Geddis v. Proprietors of Bann Reservoir* (1878) 3 App Cas 430; *Metropolitan Asylum District v. Hill* (1881) 6 App Cas 193; *Allen v. Gulf Oil Refining* [1981] AC 1001 · statutory authority
- **Facts.** *Hammersmith*: vibration from trains reduced the value of the plaintiff's house. *Vaughan*: sparks from a lawful locomotive set fire to woods. *Geddis*: a statutory body's negligent work flooded land. *Metropolitan Asylum*: a smallpox hospital was built in a residential area under a general power. *Allen*: an Act authorised a refinery.
- **Issue.** How far does statutory authority excuse harm?
- **Held.** No action lies for what the statute authorises and what is inevitable (*Hammersmith*, *Vaughan*, *Allen*). But statutory power does not excuse **negligence** (*Geddis*), and a **permissive** power does not authorise a nuisance (*Metropolitan Asylum*).
- **Principle.** Statutory authority protects against harm that is the inevitable result of what the statute authorises, done without negligence. A duty to pay compensation may be provided by the Act.
- **After.** *Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand* (1980) 4 SCC 162: a statutory body cannot plead lack of funds as an excuse for failing to remove a public nuisance. *M.C. Mehta* removed the exceptions for hazardous industry.
- **Used in:** A-38, B-19, B-38, B-41, C-08, C-17, C-18.

## 2C · Unit 3 · Vicarious liability, State liability and joint tortfeasors

### 29. *Century Insurance Co. v. Northern Ireland Road Transport Board* [1942] AC 509 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A driver delivering petrol struck a match to light a cigarette and caused a fire and explosion.
- **Issue.** Was the employee acting in the course of employment?
- **Held.** Yes. Lighting a cigarette was an unauthorised and negligent way of doing his job of delivering petrol; the employer was liable.
- **Principle.** A master is liable for a servant's wrongful act if it is a wrongful mode of doing something authorised, even if prohibited by the master.
- **Used in:** B-22, C-10.

### 30. *Limpus v. London General Omnibus Co.* (1862) 1 H&C 526; *Beard v. London General Omnibus Co.* [1900] 2 QB 530 · course of employment
- **Facts.** *Limpus*: a bus driver, against instructions, tried to obstruct a rival bus and caused a collision. *Beard*: a conductor, without authority, turned the bus round and injured a person.
- **Issue.** When is a prohibited act within the course of employment?
- **Held.** *Limpus*: the employer was liable. The driver was doing his work (driving) in a prohibited and improper manner. *Beard*: not liable; turning the bus was not part of the conductor's work at all.
- **Principle.** A prohibition limiting the **mode** of doing the work does not take the act outside the employment; a prohibition limiting the **scope** does.
- **After.** *Twine v. Bean's Express* [1946] 1 All ER 202 (a driver gave a lift to a stranger against a notice; the employer was not liable) and *Rose v. Plenty* [1976] 1 WLR 141 (a milkman allowed a boy to help on his round, against orders; the employer was liable since the help served the employer's business).
- **Used in:** B-22, C-10.

### 31. *Poland v. John Parr & Sons* [1927] 1 KB 236; *Warren v. Henlys* [1948] 2 All ER 935 · force used by servants
- **Facts.** *Poland*: a carter struck a boy he thought was stealing sugar from the employer's waggon; the boy was injured. *Warren*: a petrol-pump attendant assaulted a customer after a quarrel over a payment.
- **Issue.** When is an assault by a servant in the course of employment?
- **Held.** *Poland*: the employer was liable, because the servant acted to protect the employer's property. *Warren*: not liable; the attack was a personal act of revenge after the transaction had ended.
- **Principle.** Use of force is in the course of employment only if it is connected with the employer's business, not if it is personal.
- **Used in:** B-22, C-10.

### 32. *Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co.* [1912] AC 716; *Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank* (1867) LR 2 Ex 259 · fraud by servants
- **Facts.** *Lloyd*: a solicitor's managing clerk, entrusted with the conveyancing, induced a client to sign over property and kept the proceeds. *Barwick*: a bank manager made a fraudulent guarantee on the bank's behalf.
- **Issue.** Is a master liable for a servant's fraud for his own benefit?
- **Held.** Yes. The wrong was committed in the course of the very business the master had entrusted to the servant, within his apparent authority.
- **Principle.** A master is liable for frauds committed by a servant in the course of the business entrusted to him, even if the servant alone benefited.
- **After.** *Mohamud v. WM Morrison Supermarkets* [2016] UKSC 11 and *Various Claimants v. Catholic Child Welfare Society* [2012] UKSC 56 restate the test as a **close connection** with the employment (a petrol-station attendant assaulted a customer and the employer was liable). *WM Morrison v. Various Claimants* [2020] UKSC 12 (an employee leaked data to harm the employer; not liable) shows its limits.
- **Used in:** A-45, A-48, B-21, C-10.

### 33. *Dharangadhara Chemical Works v. State of Saurashtra* AIR 1957 SC 264; *Silver Jubilee Tailoring House v. Chief Inspector of Shops* (1974) 3 SCC 498 · who is a servant
- **Facts.** *Dharangadhara*: salt-makers (*agarias*) were paid by the quantity of salt made. *Silver Jubilee*: tailors in a shop were paid by the piece.
- **Issue.** Is the worker an employee (contract of service) or an independent contractor (contract for services)?
- **Held.** The **control test** (the employer controls not only what is done but how) is the main test, but the Court looks at the whole relationship. In *Dharangadhara* the workers were servants; in *Silver Jubilee* the right of control was decisive.
- **Principle.** Tests: control (*Yewens v. Noakes*), integration (*Stevenson Jordan v. MacDonald & Evans*), multiple (*Ready Mixed Concrete v. Minister of Pensions* [1968] 2 QB 497) and "in business on his own account" (*Market Investigations* [1969] 2 QB 173).
- **Used in:** A-43, B-21, B-51, C-10, C-22.

### 34. *Honeywill & Stein Ltd v. Larkin Bros* [1934] 1 KB 191; *Bower v. Peate* (1876) 1 QB 321; *Dalton v. Henry Angus & Co.* (1881) 6 App Cas 740 · independent contractors
- **Facts.** *Honeywill*: a photographer hired by the defendants used magnesium flash powder, and set fire to a cinema. *Bower*: the contractor dug so as to remove support from a neighbour's house. *Dalton*: a contractor's negligent work removed support for neighbouring land.
- **Issue.** When is an employer liable for the wrongs of an independent contractor?
- **Held.** Where the work is **ultra-hazardous** (*Honeywill*), or involves an **absolute or non-delegable duty** (*Bower*, *Dalton*: the duty of support), the employer remains liable.
- **Principle.** General rule: no liability for an independent contractor's torts. Exceptions: non-delegable duties, hazardous work, nuisance, strict liability, the employer's own negligence in choosing.
- **After.** *Wilsons & Clyde Coal Co. v. English* [1938] AC 57 (an employer's duty to provide competent staff, safe plant, a safe place and a safe system is non-delegable). In India see also *M.C. Mehta*.
- **Used in:** A-47, B-23, C-10.

### 35. *Mersey Docks & Harbour Board v. Coggins & Griffith* [1947] AC 1 · House of Lords; *Morgans v. Launchbury* [1973] AC 127
- **Facts.** *Mersey Docks*: a crane with its driver was hired out to stevedores; the driver negligently injured a person. *Morgans*: a husband, who was drunk, asked a friend to drive the family car; the friend crashed.
- **Issue.** Who is liable: the general employer, the hirer, or the owner?
- **Held.** *Mersey Docks*: the general employer (the Board) stayed liable, because the hirer had not taken over control of **how** the driver worked. *Morgans*: the owner was not liable, as the driver was not acting on the owner's business.
- **Principle.** Liability follows control of the manner of the work; vicarious liability of a car owner requires that the driver drove for the owner's purposes.
- **Used in:** A-50, C-10.

### 36. *Peninsular & Oriental Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State for India* (1861) 5 Bom HCR App 1 · Supreme Court of Calcutta (Peacock C.J.)
- **Facts.** A horse of the plaintiff was injured by the negligence of Government servants working in the Government dockyard at Kidderpore (Calcutta) †.
- **Issue.** Is the Government liable for a servant's tort?
- **Held.** Yes, where the act was done in carrying on an undertaking that a private person could carry on. It would not be liable for acts done in the exercise of **sovereign** powers (waging war, taking prisoners, etc.).
- **Principle.** The origin of the sovereign / non-sovereign distinction in India.
- **After.** Art. 300 of the Constitution continues the position of the Dominion and the Provinces. See *Kasturilal* and *Vidhyawati*.
- **Used in:** A-51, A-52, B-24, C-11.

### 37. *State of Rajasthan v. Vidhyawati* AIR 1962 SC 933 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A driver employed by the State was driving a Government jeep, which had been repaired in a workshop, and ran over and killed a pedestrian (Vidhyawati's husband).
- **Issue.** Is the State liable for the negligence of its driver?
- **Held.** Yes. The State is liable as any master is. The driving of a Government vehicle was not a sovereign function.
- **Principle.** The State, like a private employer, is vicariously liable for torts of its servants committed in non-sovereign work; Art. 300 refers back to the liability of the East India Company.
- **After.** *Kasturilal* (1965) restricted the principle again for sovereign powers; *Nagendra Rao* (1994) narrowed *Kasturilal*.
- **Used in:** A-55, B-21, B-24, C-05, C-10, C-11.

### 38. *Kasturilal Ralia Ram Jain v. State of U.P.* AIR 1965 SC 1039 · Supreme Court (Gajendragadkar C.J.)
- **Facts.** Police officers arrested Kasturilal, a bullion dealer, and seized a quantity of gold. The gold was kept in the police malkhana. A head constable misappropriated it and fled to Pakistan. The owner sued the State for return or value.
- **Issue.** Is the State liable for a loss caused by a police servant's negligence in custody of seized property?
- **Held.** No. The power of arrest, search and seizure is a **sovereign power** delegated by statute. The tort occurred in the exercise of that power, so the State was not liable. The Court said the law needed changing and left it to the legislature.
- **Principle.** The State has immunity when its servants commit torts in the exercise of sovereign powers.
- **After.** Narrowed in *Nagendra Rao*; replaced for violation of fundamental rights by public-law compensation (*Rudul Sah*, *Nilabati Behera*).
- **Used in:** A-54, B-24, C-05, C-11.

### 39. *N. Nagendra Rao & Co. v. State of A.P.* (1994) 6 SCC 205 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Foodgrains seized by officials under a control order were left to rot through want of care. The owner sued the State.
- **Issue.** Is a negligent failure to look after seized goods a sovereign act?
- **Held.** No. The State was liable. *Kasturilal* is confined to its facts; in the changed context, sovereign powers are only the acts of State and a few high-level functions.
- **Principle.** The immunity is now narrow: only those acts truly in the nature of sovereign powers.
- **Used in:** A-54, B-24, C-05, C-11.

### 40. *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141; *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677; *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) 2 SCC 746 · Supreme Court, public-law compensation
- **Facts.** *Rudul Sah*: kept in jail for 14 years after his acquittal. *Bhim Singh*: an MLA arrested illegally and not produced before a magistrate in time, so that he could not attend the Assembly. *Nilabati Behera*: a young man was taken by the police and found dead on a railway track.
- **Issue.** Can the Supreme Court award monetary compensation for violation of Art. 21 under Art. 32?
- **Held.** Yes. *Rudul Sah*: ₹30,000 as interim compensation. *Bhim Singh*: ₹50,000. *Nilabati Behera*: ₹1.5 lakh to the mother; the State's liability for custodial death is strict and sovereign immunity does not apply to this public-law remedy.
- **Principle.** Compensation under Arts. 32 and 226 is a public-law remedy for violation of fundamental rights. It is **in addition** to private-law damages, and the State cannot plead sovereign immunity.
- **After.** *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416 (guidelines on arrest and detention). *Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das* (2000) 2 SCC 465 (₹10 lakh to a Bangladeshi woman raped at a railway guest house; Art. 21 protects non-citizens). *Saheli v. Commissioner of Police* (1990) 1 SCC 422 (child beaten to death by police). *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of W.B.* (1996) 4 SCC 37 (denial of emergency treatment, ₹25,000). *State of A.P. v. Challa Ramakrishna Reddy* (2000) 5 SCC 712 † (prisoner killed after the authorities failed to protect him).
- **Used in:** A-15, A-51, A-56, A-98, B-05, B-06, B-10, B-24, B-25, B-41, C-01, C-02, C-05, C-06, C-11, C-18.

### 41. *Brinsmead v. Harrison* (1872) LR 7 CP 547; *Cocke v. Jennor* (1614); *Merryweather v. Nixan* (1799) 8 TR 186; *Thompson v. London County Council* [1899] 1 QB 840; *Drinkwater v. Kimber* [1952] 2 QB 281 · joint and concurrent tortfeasors
- **Facts.** *Merryweather*: one of two defendants who had paid the whole damages sought a share from the other. *Thompson* and *Drinkwater*: two persons whose separate acts caused the same damage. *Brooke v. Bool* [1928] 2 KB 578: two persons looked for a gas leak with a naked flame and caused an explosion. *The Koursk* [1924] P 140: the negligent navigation of two ships, one after the other, led to a collision with a third †.
- **Issue.** Who are joint tortfeasors, and what follows from joint liability?
- **Held.** Joint tortfeasors are those who act in concert, or where one is vicariously liable for the other, or who are in breach of a joint duty. Independent wrongdoers whose acts happen to combine are **concurrent** tortfeasors (*Thompson*, *Drinkwater*; in *The Koursk* the test was whether there was a common design or the same act causing the same damage †). At common law, judgment against one barred an action against another (*Brinsmead*), release of one released all (*Cocke v. Jennor*), and there was no contribution (*Merryweather*).
- **Principle.** Each joint tortfeasor is liable for the whole damage (joint and several liability). The plaintiff may sue any or all.
- **After.** In England these rules were changed by the Law Reform (Married Women and Tortfeasors) Act, 1935 and the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act, 1978. India has no general contribution statute; courts follow the modern English approach †. In motor claims, *T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan* (2008) 3 SCC 748 and *Khenyei v. New India Assurance* (2015) 9 SCC 273 † allow the injured party to recover the whole from either negligent driver.
- **Used in:** A-58, A-59, A-60, A-61, B-07, B-26, C-06, C-12.

## 2D · Unit 4A · Negligence

### 42. *Donoghue v. Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 · House of Lords (3:2)
- **Facts.** Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle that a friend bought for her at a café in Paisley. She said that it contained the remains of a snail. She sued the manufacturer, Stevenson, since she had no contract with the café.
- **Issue.** Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of his goods?
- **Held.** Yes. A manufacturer who sells goods in a form that shows he intends them to reach the consumer without reasonable chance of intermediate examination owes the consumer a duty to take reasonable care. Lord Atkin's **neighbour principle**: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour — persons so closely and directly affected that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.
- **Principle.** Duty of care is based on reasonable foresight and proximity; liability in negligence is independent of contract.
- **After.** *Anns v. Merton LBC* [1978] AC 728 (two-stage test) was overruled by *Murphy v. Brentwood DC* [1991] 1 AC 398. *Caparo Industries v. Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605 requires foreseeability, proximity and that it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. India follows *Donoghue*: *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* AIR 1966 SC 1750; *M.S. Grewal v. Deep Chand Sood* (2001) 8 SCC 151 †.
- **Used in:** A-07, A-64, A-67, A-68, B-02, B-06, B-27, B-28, B-39, B-56, C-01, C-13, C-25.

### 43. *Caparo Industries plc v. Dickman* [1990] 2 AC 605 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Caparo bought shares in a company after reading its audited accounts, which proved to be wrong, and then took over the company. It sued the auditors.
- **Issue.** Do auditors owe a duty to an individual investor who relies on the statutory accounts?
- **Held.** No. The statutory audit exists for the shareholders as a body, not for those who buy shares; there was no sufficient proximity.
- **Principle.** Three-stage test: damage reasonably foreseeable, a relationship of proximity, and it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. The courts proceed incrementally by analogy.
- **After.** Followed in India for liability for statements and for new categories.
- **Used in:** A-69, B-27, B-28, C-13.

### 44. *Hedley Byrne & Co. v. Heller & Partners* [1964] AC 465 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Hedley Byrne, an advertising agency, asked its bank to check the creditworthiness of a customer, Easipower. Heller (the customer's bank) gave favourable references "without responsibility". The customer failed and the agency lost money.
- **Issue.** Is there liability for a careless statement that causes pure economic loss?
- **Held.** In principle yes, where there is a **special relationship** and reliance and an assumption of responsibility. On the facts there was no liability because Heller's disclaimer excluded it.
- **Principle.** Negligent misstatement causing economic loss is actionable where the speaker assumes responsibility to a person who relies on him.
- **After.** *Henderson v. Merrett Syndicates* [1995] 2 AC 145 (assumption of responsibility). *Derry v. Peek* stays the test for fraud; see 2E.
- **Used in:** A-81, B-28, B-36, B-45, C-13, C-21.

### 45. *Spartan Steel & Alloys Ltd v. Martin & Co.* [1973] QB 27 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** Contractors negligently cut an electricity cable. The power cut spoilt a melt of metal in the plaintiff's furnace and stopped production.
- **Issue.** Can the plaintiff recover pure economic loss caused by a negligent act?
- **Held.** Only the loss from the physical damage (the spoilt melt) and the profit on it. The loss of profit on other work during the power cut was **pure economic loss** and not recoverable (Lord Denning relied on policy).
- **Principle.** Pure economic loss is generally not recoverable in negligence unless there is a special relationship (*Hedley Byrne*).
- **After.** *Murphy v. Brentwood* (defective buildings). Indian courts have not adopted a settled rule †.
- **Used in:** B-28, B-36, C-13.

### 46. *Bolam v. Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582 · Queen's Bench (McNair J.); *Bolitho v. City & Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232
- **Facts.** *Bolam*: a patient given electro-convulsive therapy without muscle relaxant or restraint suffered fractures. *Bolitho*: a child with croup was not seen by the doctor, suffered respiratory failure and brain damage, and died.
- **Issue.** What is the standard of care in professional negligence?
- **Held.** A doctor is not negligent if he acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion skilled in that art (*Bolam*). The court must also be satisfied that the body of opinion has a logical basis (*Bolitho*).
- **Principle.** Professional standard of care: the ordinary skill of an ordinary competent member of the profession.
- **After.** *Montgomery v. Lanarkshire HB* [2015] UKSC 11 applies a different test for consent. In India the Supreme Court follows *Bolam*: *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1; *Kusum Sharma v. Batra Hospital* (2010) 3 SCC 480.
- **Used in:** A-71, B-29, B-35, C-13, C-16.

### 47. *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1 · Supreme Court, 3 judges
- **Facts.** A patient with breathing difficulty was admitted to a hospital in Ludhiana. It was alleged that the doctors came late and that the oxygen cylinder was empty †. The patient died and the doctors were prosecuted for causing death by negligence (IPC s. 304A).
- **Issue.** What degree of negligence makes a doctor criminally liable?
- **Held.** Criminal liability needs **gross** negligence or recklessness, a degree much higher than civil negligence. Prosecution of a doctor should not proceed without a credible opinion from another competent doctor, and the doctor should not be arrested in a routine way.
- **Principle.** Civil negligence is a failure to take reasonable care; criminal negligence needs gross negligence or recklessness. The same *Bolam* standard measures a doctor's skill.
- **After.** BNS s. 106(1) (up to five years; up to two years for a registered medical practitioner doing a medical procedure) ‡. *Dr. Suresh Gupta v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi* (2004) 6 SCC 422 is the companion case.
- **Used in:** A-83, B-35, C-16.

### 48. *Dr. Laxman Balkrishna Joshi v. Dr. Trimbak Bapu Godbole* AIR 1969 SC 128; *Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel* (1996) 4 SCC 332; *Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia* (1998) 4 SCC 39 · doctors and hospitals
- **Facts.** *Laxman Joshi*: a patient with a fracture was treated under anaesthesia without adequate care and died of shock †. *Poonam Verma*: a homoeopath practised allopathic medicine. *Spring Meadows*: a child suffered brain damage after a wrong injection by a hospital nurse.
- **Issue.** What duties does a doctor owe and when is the hospital liable?
- **Held.** A doctor owes a duty (a) to decide whether to take the case, (b) to decide what treatment to give, and (c) to administer it with reasonable care (*Laxman Joshi*). One who practises a system of medicine in which he is not qualified is negligent *per se* † (*Poonam Verma*). A hospital is liable for its staff, and the parents may claim (*Spring Meadows*).
- **Principle.** A professional is liable if he lacks the skill he claims or fails to use it with reasonable care.
- **After.** *Martin F. D'Souza v. Mohd. Ishfaq* (2009) 3 SCC 1 (prior expert opinion) was refined in *V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital* (2010) 5 SCC 513 (*res ipsa loquitur* may apply and expert opinion is not needed in every case).
- **Used in:** A-82, B-35, B-53, B-54, C-16, C-22.

### 49. *Vaughan v. Menlove* (1837) 3 Bing NC 468; *Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co.* (1856) 11 Ex 781; *Glasgow Corporation v. Muir* [1943] AC 448 · the reasonable man
- **Facts.** *Vaughan*: the defendant built a haystack near the plaintiff's cottages, in spite of warnings, and said he would "chance it"; it caught fire by spontaneous combustion. *Blyth*: a fire-plug failed in an unusually severe frost.
- **Issue.** By what standard is conduct judged?
- **Held.** *Vaughan*: the standard is objective, that of a person of ordinary prudence, not the defendant's own best judgment. *Blyth* (Alderson B.): negligence is the omission to do something that a reasonable man would do, or doing something that a prudent and reasonable man would not do; the frost was beyond the precautions of a reasonable man.
- **Principle.** The reasonable man is an objective standard, neither perfect nor careless, and not omniscient (*Glasgow Corporation v. Muir*).
- **After.** *Nettleship v. Weston* (learner driver); *Mullin v. Richards* [1998] 1 WLR 1304 (a child is judged by an ordinary child of the same age).
- **Used in:** A-63, A-66, B-27, B-29, C-03, C-13.

### 50. *Bolton v. Stone* [1951] AC 850; *Paris v. Stepney BC* [1951] AC 367; *Latimer v. A.E.C. Ltd* [1953] AC 643; *Watt v. Hertfordshire CC* [1954] 1 WLR 835 · factors in breach
- **Facts.** *Bolton*: a cricket ball hit out of the ground over a high fence struck a passer-by; this had happened only a few times in many years. *Paris*: a one-eyed workman was blinded in his good eye; the employer gave no goggles. *Latimer*: the factory floor was slippery after a flood; the employer spread sawdust. *Watt*: a fireman was hurt by a heavy jack carried in an unfitted lorry in an emergency.
- **Issue.** What decides whether the defendant has fallen below the standard?
- **Held.** *Bolton*: not liable, the risk was very small. *Paris*: liable, as the gravity of the harm to this workman was high. *Latimer*: not liable, he took all reasonable steps. *Watt*: not liable, as the risk was justified by the importance of saving life.
- **Principle.** Factors: probability of harm, seriousness of the harm, the cost and practicality of precautions, and the utility of the defendant's activity.
- **After.** *Miller v. Jackson* [1977] QB 966 (cricket again; see 2E).
- **Used in:** B-27, B-29, C-13.

### 51. *Byrne v. Boadle* (1863) 2 H&C 722; *Scott v. London & St Katherine Docks* (1865) 3 H&C 596; *Henderson v. Henry E. Jenkins & Sons* [1970] AC 282 · res ipsa loquitur
- **Facts.** *Byrne*: a barrel of flour fell from a window of the defendant's warehouse and struck a passer-by. *Scott*: bags of sugar fell from a crane on a dock-worker. *Henderson*: a lorry's brakes failed on a hill and a pedestrian was killed; the defendants said that hidden corrosion in a brake pipe caused it †.
- **Issue.** When may negligence be inferred from the accident itself?
- **Held.** When (a) the thing was under the defendant's control, (b) the accident would not ordinarily happen without negligence, and (c) the cause is unknown to the plaintiff, the court may infer negligence, and the defendant must explain (*Scott*). In *Henderson* the House of Lords held that the doctrine shifts only the **evidential** burden: the defendant must explain how the accident could have happened without negligence, but the legal burden stays on the plaintiff †.
- **Principle.** *Res ipsa loquitur* is a rule of evidence, not of law: it raises an inference of negligence.
- **After.** India: *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* AIR 1966 SC 1750 (the clock tower in Chandni Chowk fell); *Shyam Sunder v. State of Rajasthan* AIR 1974 SC 890 †; *Syad Akbar v. State of Karnataka* AIR 1979 SC 1848 † (the limits of the doctrine); *V. Kishan Rao* (2010) 5 SCC 513 (medical negligence).
- **Used in:** A-75, A-76, B-32, C-14.

### 52. *Butterfield v. Forrester* (1809) 11 East 60; *Davies v. Mann* (1842) 10 M&W 546; *Swadling v. Cooper* [1931] AC 1 · contributory negligence
- **Facts.** *Butterfield*: the plaintiff rode fast at dusk into a pole that the defendant had left across the road. *Davies*: the plaintiff left a donkey with its forefeet tied on the highway; a wagon driven too fast ran it down. *Swadling*: a cyclist and a lorry collided at a blind junction.
- **Issue.** How does the plaintiff's own fault affect the claim?
- **Held.** *Butterfield*: the plaintiff could not recover, because with ordinary care he could have seen the pole (the old all-or-nothing rule). *Davies*: the defendant was liable because he had the last opportunity to avoid the collision. *Swadling*: the last-opportunity rule was confined; the House of Lords looked for the real cause.
- **Principle.** Old rule: contributory negligence barred the claim. Modern rule: damages are reduced to the extent that the plaintiff's own negligence contributed to the damage.
- **After.** UK: Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945, s. 1. India follows apportionment. *Froom v. Butcher* [1976] QB 286 (25% for no seat belt where it would have prevented the injuries; 15% where it would have reduced them), *Capps v. Miller* [1989] 1 WLR 839 (10% for an unfastened helmet), *Jones v. Livox Quarries* [1952] 2 QB 608 (riding on a towbar against orders; Denning L.J.: failure to take reasonable care for his own safety), *Gough v. Thorne* [1966] 1 WLR 1387 (a 13-year-old was not contributorily negligent).
- **Used in:** A-77, A-78, B-20, B-31, B-33, C-14.

### 53. *Re Polemis* [1921] 3 KB 560 and *Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v. Morts Dock & Engineering Co. (The Wagon Mound No. 1)* [1961] AC 388 · remoteness
- **Facts.** *Polemis*: stevedores dropped a plank into a ship's hold; a spark ignited petrol vapour and destroyed the ship. *Wagon Mound*: oil was carelessly spilled in Sydney harbour; welding on a wharf set fire to cotton waste floating on the oil and the fire damaged the wharf.
- **Issue.** For which consequences is a negligent defendant liable?
- **Held.** *Polemis*: for all the direct consequences, however unforeseeable. *Wagon Mound (No. 1)*: only for damage of a kind that was **reasonably foreseeable**; the fire was not.
- **Principle.** Remoteness test: reasonable foreseeability of the kind of damage.
- **After.** *Wagon Mound (No. 2)* [1967] 1 AC 617 (liability where the fire was a foreseeable risk). *Hughes v. Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837 (the kind of harm, burns, was foreseeable although the manner, an explosion, was not). *Smith v. Leech Brain* [1962] 2 QB 405 (thin-skull rule: a defendant takes his victim as he finds him).
- **Used in:** A-64, A-73, B-27, B-30, C-13.

### 54. *Barnett v. Chelsea & Kensington Hospital Management Committee* [1969] 1 QB 428; *Bonnington Castings v. Wardlaw* [1956] AC 613; *McKew v. Holland & Hannen & Cubitts* [1969] 3 All ER 1621; *Scott v. Shepherd* (1773) 2 Wm Bl 892 · causation
- **Facts.** *Barnett*: three night-watchmen who drank tea containing arsenic went to casualty; the doctor sent them home without examining them; one died. *Bonnington*: a worker contracted silicosis from dust, only part of which was from the employer's fault. *McKew*: after a negligent injury the plaintiff's leg gave way, and he jumped down stairs and was hurt again. *Scott*: a lighted squib thrown into a market was tossed on by two others and burst in the plaintiff's face.
- **Issue.** What must the plaintiff prove about the connection between breach and harm?
- **Held.** *Barnett*: the doctor's breach was not the cause, because the man would have died anyway (the "but for" test). *Bonnington*: where the employer's dust made a **material contribution**, he was liable. *McKew*: the plaintiff's own unreasonable act broke the chain (*novus actus*). *Scott*: the intervening acts of self-preservation did not break the chain; the first thrower was liable.
- **Principle.** Causation: "but for" test, material contribution, and *novus actus interveniens*.
- **After.** *Fairchild v. Glenhaven Funeral Services* [2002] UKHL 22 (mesothelioma and multiple employers: a relaxed test).
- **Used in:** A-72, A-74, B-27, B-30, C-13.

### 55. *Wilkinson v. Downton* [1897] 2 QB 57 and *Rhodes v. OPO* [2015] UKSC 32 · intentional infliction of harm †
- **Facts.** Downton, as a practical joke, told Mrs Wilkinson that her husband had been smashed up in an accident and was lying at Leytonstone with both legs broken. She suffered a severe shock and illness.
- **Issue.** Is there a remedy for words intended to cause physical harm?
- **Held.** Yes. Wright J.: the defendant wilfully did an act calculated to cause physical harm, and thereby caused it, without justification.
- **Principle.** A wilful act calculated to cause physical harm to the plaintiff, which does cause it, is actionable.
- **After.** *Rhodes v. OPO* (2015) † confirms the tort requires an intention to cause severe distress and does not extend to publication of a book where the intent was other.
- **Used in:** B-06, C-01, C-15.

### 56. *Victorian Railways Commissioners v. Coultas* (1888) 13 App Cas 222; *Dulieu v. White* [1901] 2 KB 669; *Bourhill v. Young* [1943] AC 92; *Hambrook v. Stokes Bros* [1925] 1 KB 141; *McLoughlin v. O'Brian* [1983] 1 AC 410; *Alcock v. Chief Constable of South Yorkshire* [1992] 1 AC 310 · nervous shock
- **Facts.** *Coultas*: a woman suffered shock when a train nearly hit her carriage at a crossing. *Dulieu*: a pregnant barmaid, frightened when a van was driven into the pub, became seriously ill. *Bourhill*: a woman, out of sight of the collision, heard it and later saw blood on the road; she claimed shock. *Hambrook*: a mother was shocked by fear for her children. *McLoughlin*: a wife was shocked when she saw her family in hospital about two hours after a road crash. *Alcock*: relatives of Hillsborough victims.
- **Issue.** When is damages for psychiatric injury (nervous shock) available?
- **Held.** *Coultas* (no recovery without impact) was rejected in *Dulieu*: shock from a reasonable fear for one's own safety is actionable. *Bourhill*: no duty to a person outside the area of foreseeable physical harm. *McLoughlin* extended the aftermath. *Alcock* set control mechanisms for secondary victims: a close tie of love and affection, proximity in time and space to the event or its immediate aftermath, perception with one's own unaided senses, and a sudden shock.
- **Principle.** Primary victims (in the zone of danger) recover for psychiatric injury if physical injury was foreseeable (*Page v. Smith* [1996] AC 155); secondary victims must satisfy the *Alcock* tests.
- **After.** *White v. Chief Constable of South Yorkshire* [1999] 2 AC 455 (rescuer police officers not within the secondary victim class). *Walker v. Northumberland CC* [1995] 1 All ER 737 (an employer's liability for psychiatric injury from overwork). *Paul v. Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust* [2024] UKSC 1 † (no secondary victim claim where relatives witnessed a patient's medical collapse caused by negligence). I did not find a leading Supreme Court of India decision on nervous shock †.
- **Used in:** A-79, A-80, B-34, C-15.

## 2E · Unit 4B · Nuisance, trespass, false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, conversion, deceit

### 57. *Sturges v. Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** A confectioner had used a mortar and pestle in his premises for more than 20 years. Next door, a doctor built a consulting room against the party wall, and the noise and vibration then disturbed his work.
- **Issue.** Is the noise a nuisance, and had the confectioner acquired a right to make it by prescription?
- **Held.** It was a nuisance and could be stopped by injunction. The right of 20 years' use runs only from the time the nuisance could be complained of, which was when the consulting room was built. It was no defence that the doctor "came to the nuisance". Thesiger L.J.: "What would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey."
- **Principle.** Whether an interference is a nuisance depends on the locality (for comfort); a nuisance can be acquired by 20 years' prescription; coming to the nuisance is no defence.
- **After.** Indian Easements Act, 1882, s. 15 (20 years).
- **Used in:** A-85, A-90, A-91, B-37, B-38, C-17.

### 58. *St Helen's Smelting Co. v. Tipping* (1865) 11 HLC 642 and *Bamford v. Turnley* (1862) 3 B&S 62
- **Facts.** *St Helen's*: fumes from a copper smelter damaged the plaintiff's trees and crops. *Bamford*: the defendant burned bricks on his land near the plaintiff's house.
- **Issue.** How does locality affect liability?
- **Held.** *St Helen's*: where the harm is **material injury to property**, locality is irrelevant; the defendant is liable even in a manufacturing area. For mere **sensible personal discomfort**, locality matters. *Bamford*: public utility is not a defence; the test is reasonable give and take between neighbours.
- **Principle.** Private nuisance: unreasonable user judged by locality (for comfort), duration, malice, utility and sensitivity.
- **Used in:** A-87, B-37, B-39, C-17.

### 59. *Hunter v. Canary Wharf Ltd* [1997] AC 655 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Residents near the Canary Wharf tower in London claimed that it interfered with their television reception.
- **Issue.** Who can sue in private nuisance, and is interference with television reception a nuisance?
- **Held.** Only a person with a right in the land (an owner or tenant, or exclusive possession) can sue, not mere licensees or family members. Interference with television reception by the mere presence of a building is not an actionable nuisance.
- **Principle.** Private nuisance protects land interests; the plaintiff needs a proprietary interest.
- **After.** *Fearn v. Tate Gallery* [2023] UKSC 4 † (visual intrusion from a viewing gallery can be a nuisance in principle, but the claim failed).
- **Used in:** A-87, B-37, C-17.

### 60. *Rose v. Miles* (1815) 4 M&S 101 and *Attorney-General v. P.Y.A. Quarries Ltd* [1957] 2 QB 169
- **Facts.** *Rose*: the defendant's barge obstructed a navigable creek and the plaintiff had to carry his goods overland at extra cost. *PYA*: a quarry caused dust and vibration affecting people in the neighbourhood.
- **Issue.** What is a public nuisance and who can sue in tort?
- **Held.** *PYA*: it is an act that materially affects the reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of persons, though not every individual in it need be affected. *Rose*: a private individual can sue only if he suffers **special damage** different in kind from the public.
- **Principle.** Public nuisance is a crime; a civil action needs special damage, or the Advocate-General's suit (CPC s. 91).
- **After.** BNS s. 270 †.
- **Used in:** A-86, B-37, C-17.

### 61. *Robinson v. Kilvert* (1889) 41 Ch D 88; *Bliss v. Hall* (1838) 4 Bing NC 183; *Adams v. Ursell* [1913] 1 Ch 269; *Lambton v. Mellish* [1894] 3 Ch 163; *Miller v. Jackson* [1977] QB 966; *Coventry v. Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13 · defences and remedies in nuisance
- **Facts.** *Robinson*: heat from the defendant's lower-floor premises damaged the plaintiff's paper, which was unusually sensitive. *Bliss*: a candle-maker's neighbour moved close to an existing factory. *Adams*: a fried-fish shop in a poor area caused smells. *Lambton*: two neighbouring merry-go-rounds made noise. *Miller*: new houses were built near a cricket ground. *Coventry*: a motor-racing track.
- **Issue.** What is no defence, and when is damages preferred to an injunction?
- **Held.** Abnormal sensitivity is no ground for complaint (*Robinson*); coming to the nuisance (*Bliss*), public benefit (*Adams*) and the plea that others also contribute (*Lambton*) are no defence. The court may award damages instead of an injunction (*Miller*; *Coventry*, which relaxed the strict rules of *Shelfer v. City of London Electric Lighting Co.* [1895] 1 Ch 287).
- **Principle.** Remedies: damages, injunction (prohibitory or mandatory), abatement (*Lemmon v. Webb* [1895] AC 1).
- **After.** *Halsey v. Esso Petroleum* [1961] 1 WLR 683 (smell, noise and acid smuts from an oil depot: damages and an injunction). *Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Assn.* (2000) 7 SCC 282 (noise from loudspeakers at a place of worship is a nuisance; no religion requires it) and *Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand* (1980) 4 SCC 162.
- **Used in:** A-16, A-89, A-91, A-92, B-10, B-38, C-06, C-17.

### 62. *Sedleigh-Denfield v. O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A trespasser laid a pipe in a ditch on the defendant's land. The defendants' servants knew of it and used the ditch; a blocked grating later caused a flood on the plaintiff's land.
- **Issue.** Is an occupier liable for a nuisance created by a stranger?
- **Held.** Yes. An occupier who knows of the hazard, or ought to, and fails to abate it within a reasonable time, **adopts** or **continues** it.
- **Principle.** Liability for a nuisance created by another: knowledge, adoption or continuation.
- **After.** *Leakey v. National Trust* [1980] QB 485 (natural hazards).
- **Used in:** B-20, C-17.

### 63. *Entick v. Carrington* (1765) 19 St Tr 1029; *Basely v. Clarkson* (1681) 3 Lev 37; *Holmes v. Wilson* (1839) 10 A&E 503; *Bernstein v. Skyviews & General Ltd* [1978] QB 479 · trespass to land
- **Facts.** *Entick*: the King's messengers broke into a house and seized papers under a general warrant of a Secretary of State. *Basely*: the defendant mowed the plaintiff's grass by mistake. *Holmes*: highway authorities supported a road with buttresses on the plaintiff's land. *Bernstein*: an aeroplane flew over a house to take photographs.
- **Issue.** What is trespass to land and what are the limits?
- **Held.** *Entick*: officials need lawful authority for entry. *Basely*: mistake is no defence. *Holmes*: a continuing trespass; damages do not give a licence. *Bernstein*: the owner's rights in the airspace are limited to the height needed for the use and enjoyment of the land; an ordinary overflight is not a trespass.
- **Principle.** Trespass to land is a direct, unjustified interference with possession and is actionable *per se*.
- **After.** *Elias v. Pasmore* [1934] 2 KB 164 (entry to arrest lawful, but seizure of papers not). *Six Carpenters' Case* (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a (trespass *ab initio*).
- **Used in:** A-39, A-94, B-20, B-39, B-40, C-03, C-18.

### 64. *The Six Carpenters' Case* (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a · trespass ab initio
- **Facts.** Six carpenters entered an inn under the authority of law, ordered wine and bread, drank and refused to pay.
- **Issue.** Does a later abuse of lawful entry make the entry a trespass from the start?
- **Held.** No for them: refusing to pay was an omission (nonfeasance). A positive wrongful act (misfeasance) after lawful entry makes the entrant a trespasser *ab initio*; an omission does not.
- **Principle.** Trespass *ab initio* applies only to entry under legal authority (not licence) followed by a positive abuse.
- **After.** The doctrine has been criticised and was limited in modern law †.
- **Used in:** A-95, B-40, C-18.

### 65. *Tuberville v. Savage* (1669) 1 Mod Rep 3; *Stephens v. Myers* (1830) 4 C&P 349; *R v. Ireland* [1998] AC 147 · assault
- **Facts.** *Tuberville*: the defendant put his hand on his sword and said, "If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you." *Stephens*: a man threatened with a clenched fist to strike the chairman at a meeting but was stopped by a bystander. *Ireland*: silent telephone calls made to women.
- **Issue.** What is an assault?
- **Held.** *Tuberville*: no assault, because the words showed that there was no present intention to strike. *Stephens*: assault; the defendant had the present ability and apparent intention. *Ireland*: silent calls can be an assault, since words or silence can create apprehension of immediate violence.
- **Principle.** Assault is an act that causes reasonable apprehension of immediate unlawful force; no contact is needed.
- **After.** BNS ss. 130–131 †.
- **Used in:** A-96, B-42, C-18.

### 66. *Cole v. Turner* (1704) 6 Mod 149; *Fagan v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439; *Wilson v. Pringle* [1987] QB 237 · battery
- **Facts.** *Fagan*: a driver accidentally stopped his car on a policeman's foot and then refused to move it.
- **Issue.** What is a battery and can an omission or a continuing act amount to one?
- **Held.** Holt C.J. (*Cole*): the least touching of another in anger is a battery. *Fagan*: the act of driving on to the foot was a continuing act, and the intention arose while the wheel was still on the foot.
- **Principle.** Battery is the direct application of force without consent or lawful justification; ordinary conduct of daily life is excepted (*Wilson v. Pringle* required hostility; later cases doubted that).
- **After.** *Samira Kohli* (2008): treatment without consent is a battery.
- **Used in:** A-97, B-42, C-18.

### 67. *Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742; *Herd v. Weardale Steel* [1915] AC 67; *Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry* [1910] AC 295; *Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation* (1919) 122 LT 44; *Murray v. Ministry of Defence* [1988] 1 WLR 692 · false imprisonment
- **Facts.** *Bird*: part of Hammersmith Bridge was closed off for a regatta and the plaintiff was stopped from going that way, though he could go back. *Herd*: a miner who had refused to carry on work in an unsafe place was not at once raised up the shaft. *Robinson*: the plaintiff had to pay a penny to leave a ferry wharf. *Meering*: an employee was held in an office, not knowing that he was under suspicion.
- **Issue.** What is total restraint and does the plaintiff have to know of it?
- **Held.** *Bird*: a **partial** obstruction is not imprisonment. *Herd*: no duty to provide a means of leaving the mine at once. *Robinson*: a reasonable condition for leaving is not imprisonment. *Meering*: knowledge of the restraint is not essential; *Murray* confirms this but reduces damages if the plaintiff did not know.
- **Principle.** False imprisonment: total, unlawful restraint of liberty. Remedy: damages, *habeas corpus*, and public-law compensation (*Rudul Sah*).
- **After.** *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416; BNS ss. 126–127 †; BNSS ss. 35–36 †.
- **Used in:** A-98, B-41, C-18.

### 68. *Hicks v. Faulkner* (1878) 8 QBD 167; *Glinski v. McIver* [1962] AC 726; *Grainger v. Hill* (1838) 4 Bing NC 212 · malicious prosecution and abuse of process
- **Facts.** *Grainger*: the defendant had the plaintiff arrested on a debt claim in order to force him to hand over a ship's register †.
- **Issue.** What must the plaintiff prove in malicious prosecution?
- **Held.** Hawkins J. (*Hicks*): (1) prosecution by the defendant, (2) it ended in the plaintiff's favour, (3) no reasonable and probable cause, (4) malice, (5) damage. *Grainger*: abuse of legal process for an ulterior purpose is a separate tort.
- **Principle.** Malicious prosecution needs malice and absence of reasonable and probable cause; limitation is one year (Art. 74) ‡.
- **Used in:** A-99, B-09, B-44, C-03, C-18.

### 69. *Kirk v. Gregory* (1876) 1 Ex D 55; *Fouldes v. Willoughby* (1841) 8 M&W 540; *Hollins v. Fowler* (1875) LR 7 HL 757 · trespass to goods and conversion
- **Facts.** *Fouldes*: a ferry-master put the plaintiff's horses ashore when the plaintiff would not leave the ship. *Hollins*: a broker bought cotton from a fraudster and sold it to a mill; the true owner sued the broker.
- **Issue.** What is conversion?
- **Held.** *Hollins*: even an innocent broker who sells the goods of another is liable in conversion. *Fouldes*: a mere removal that does not deny the owner's title is not conversion. *Kirk*: moving a dead woman's rings from one room to another, to protect them, was a trespass to goods.
- **Principle.** Conversion: a dealing with goods inconsistent with the owner's rights and denying his title. Good faith is no defence.
- **Used in:** A-39, A-100, B-20, B-43, C-18.

### 70. *Pasley v. Freeman* (1789) 3 TR 51; *Derry v. Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337; *Edgington v. Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459; *Bisset v. Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177 · deceit
- **Facts.** *Derry*: the directors of a tramway company said that it was authorised to use steam power, on the basis of a statute, and honestly believed it. The Board of Trade later refused consent, and the shares fell.
- **Issue.** When is a false statement fraudulent?
- **Held.** *Derry*: fraud is a false statement made knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly careless whether it is true or false. An honest belief, however unreasonable, is not fraud. *Edgington*: a misstatement of one's intention is a misstatement of fact. *Bisset*: an honest opinion is not a statement of fact.
- **Principle.** Deceit: false representation of fact, knowledge of falsity, intent that the plaintiff act on it, reliance, damage.
- **After.** *Doyle v. Olby* [1969] 2 QB 158 and *Smith New Court v. Scrimgeour Vickers* [1997] AC 254 (damages for all direct loss). Directors' Liability Act, 1890 (UK). India: Contract Act, ss. 17 and 19 (fraud and misrepresentation make a contract voidable).
- **Used in:** A-21, A-101, B-20, B-36, B-45, C-03, C-21.

## 2F · Unit 4C · Defamation

### 71. *Sim v. Stretch* [1936] 2 All ER 1237 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** The defendant, in whose household the plaintiff's former maid now worked, sent the plaintiff a telegram asking him to send the maid's belongings and "the money you borrowed" and her wages †. The plaintiff claimed that it implied he was in debt to a servant.
- **Issue.** What makes a statement defamatory?
- **Held.** Lord Atkin: would the words tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally? On the facts, the words were not defamatory.
- **Principle.** The test is objective: the reaction of right-thinking members of society.
- **After.** Also: a statement that makes people shun or avoid the plaintiff, or exposes him to hatred, contempt or ridicule.
- **Used in:** A-102, B-46, C-19, C-20.

### 72. *Tolley v. J.S. Fry & Sons* [1931] AC 333; *Cassidy v. Daily Mirror* [1929] 2 KB 331; *Lewis v. Daily Telegraph* [1964] AC 234 · innuendo
- **Facts.** *Tolley*: a famous amateur golfer's caricature appeared in a chocolate advertisement without his consent. *Cassidy*: a newspaper photograph of Mr C, captioned as "engaged to Miss X", offended Mrs C, who was known as his wife.
- **Issue.** When do apparently innocent words become defamatory?
- **Held.** *Tolley*: the advertisement could imply that he had sold his amateur status, which was defamatory to those who knew golf's rules. *Cassidy*: to friends who knew that Mr C was married, the words suggested that Mrs C was not his wife. A **legal innuendo** must be pleaded and proved with the extrinsic facts; a **false innuendo** is only an inference from the words (*Lewis*).
- **Principle.** Innuendo: a secondary meaning arising from facts known to the readers.
- **Used in:** A-104, B-47, C-19.

### 73. *Hulton & Co. v. Jones* [1910] AC 20; *Newstead v. London Express* [1940] 1 KB 377; *Knupffer v. London Express* [1944] AC 116 · reference to the plaintiff
- **Facts.** *Hulton*: a newspaper wrote a fictitious account of a churchwarden "Artemus Jones" at Dieppe; a real barrister of that name sued. *Newstead*: a report of the bigamy of "Harold Newstead, a 30-year-old Camberwell man" fitted a real man of that name. *Knupffer*: a statement about a Russian émigré organisation with many members.
- **Issue.** Does the defendant's intention matter, and can a class be defamed?
- **Held.** *Hulton*: the defendant's intention is irrelevant; the question is whether reasonable people would understand the words to refer to the plaintiff. *Newstead*: same. *Knupffer*: a statement about a large group does not refer to any individual member.
- **Principle.** Defamatory words must refer to the plaintiff; liability does not depend on intention.
- **Used in:** C-19.

### 74. *Duke of Brunswick v. Harmer* (1849) 14 QB 185; *Pullman v. Hill* [1891] 1 QB 524; *Emmens v. Pottle* (1885) 16 QBD 354 · publication
- **Facts.** *Brunswick*: the Duke's agent bought an old copy of a newspaper from the publisher's office, 17 years after the original issue. *Pullman*: a letter was dictated to a typist and read by clerks.
- **Issue.** What is publication?
- **Held.** *Brunswick*: every fresh sale is a new publication, so the time for suing runs from each. *Pullman*: dictation to a typist and its reading by clerks are publications. *Emmens*: a newsvendor is not liable if he did not know, and was not negligent in not knowing, that the paper was libellous (innocent dissemination).
- **Principle.** Publication is communication to at least one person other than the plaintiff.
- **After.** *Dow Jones v. Gutnick* (2002) 210 CLR 575 (internet publication occurs where it is downloaded). In England, Defamation Act, 2013, s. 1 requires "serious harm"; *Lachaux v. Independent Print* [2019] UKSC 27.
- **Used in:** A-110, C-19.

### 75. *Alexander v. North Eastern Railway Co.* (1865) 6 B&S 340 · justification
- **Facts.** The railway published that the plaintiff had been convicted of a ticket offence and sentenced to a fine of one pound or three weeks' imprisonment. The real alternative was two weeks.
- **Issue.** Must every word of a defamatory statement be proved true?
- **Held.** No. It is enough that the statement is substantially true, so that the "sting" is justified; the difference was immaterial.
- **Principle.** Truth is a complete defence to civil defamation, even if malice is shown; the defendant must prove the substance.
- **After.** In criminal defamation truth is a defence only if for the public good (BNS s. 356, Exception 1).
- **Used in:** A-105, B-48, C-19.

### 76. *Campbell v. Spottiswoode* (1863) 3 B&S 769; *Kemsley v. Foot* [1952] AC 345 · fair comment
- **Facts.** *Campbell*: a newspaper criticised the plaintiff's religious periodical and his motives †. *Kemsley*: an article under the headline "Lower than Kemsley" criticised the press group of Lord Kemsley.
- **Issue.** What is the defence of fair comment?
- **Held.** A comment on a matter of public interest, based on facts that are true or privileged and honestly held, is protected. *Kemsley*: a comment on facts stated in the article is protected even in a headline; the facts must be stated or indicated.
- **Principle.** Fair comment (honest opinion): public interest, facts true, comment recognisable as opinion, without malice.
- **After.** England: Defamation Act, 2013, s. 3 (honest opinion). India: Exceptions 2, 3, 5 and 6 to BNS s. 356.
- **Used in:** A-106, B-48, C-19, C-20.

### 77. *Toogood v. Spyring* (1834) 1 CM&R 181; *Adam v. Ward* [1917] AC 309; *Horrocks v. Lowe* [1975] AC 135; *Chatterton v. Secretary of State for India* [1895] 2 QB 189; *Wason v. Walter* (1868) LR 4 QB 73 · privilege
- **Facts.** *Adam*: the secretary of the Army Council, writing in the course of his duty, published a letter that was defamatory of an army officer †. *Horrocks*: a councillor, speaking at a council meeting, accused a fellow councillor of improper dealing over a sale of land †.
- **Issue.** When is a defamatory statement privileged?
- **Held.** *Absolute privilege* covers parliamentary proceedings, judicial proceedings and communications of State between officers (*Chatterton*); *Wason* protects a fair report of Parliament. *Qualified privilege* covers a statement made honestly in discharge of a duty or protection of an interest to someone with a corresponding duty or interest (*Toogood*; *Adam v. Ward*). It is lost if the dominant motive is malice (*Horrocks*).
- **Principle.** Absolute privilege: no action even if malicious. Qualified privilege: lost by malice, excess or publication to an unconcerned person.
- **After.** India: Arts. 105(2), 194(2) and 361A of the Constitution; Exceptions 4, 8, 9 and 10 to BNS s. 356. *Reynolds v. Times Newspapers* [2001] 2 AC 127 (no general privilege for political information, but "responsible journalism" may qualify); replaced in England by the Defamation Act, 2013, s. 4.
- **Used in:** A-107, A-108, B-09, B-49, C-03, C-19.

### 78. *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) 7 SCC 221 and *R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1994) 6 SCC 632 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** *Swamy*: petitioners challenged ss. 499 and 500 IPC (criminal defamation) and CrPC ss. 199(1)–(4) as violating Art. 19(1)(a). *Rajagopal*: a prisoner (Auto Shankar) wrote his life story; the State tried to stop its publication.
- **Issue.** Is criminal defamation constitutional, and may a public official stop publication about his official acts?
- **Held.** *Swamy* (2016): ss. 499–500 IPC are a reasonable restriction on free speech under Art. 19(2); reputation is part of the right to life under Art. 21. *Rajagopal* (1994): publication based on public records is protected; a public official cannot sue for acts in his official duty unless the statement is false and made with reckless disregard for truth, or malice.
- **Principle.** Free speech (Art. 19(1)(a)) is balanced against reputation (Art. 21). Public officials have less protection for official conduct.
- **After.** BNS s. 356 ‡ re-enacts IPC ss. 499–500. The Court has distinguished *New York Times v. Sullivan* (US) but has taken a similar approach in *Rajagopal*.
- **Used in:** A-109, B-50, C-19.

## 2G · Unit 5 · Consumer protection

### 79. *Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta* (1994) 1 SCC 243 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** An allottee under a housing scheme of the Lucknow Development Authority did not get possession of the house in time and found the construction defective †. The State Commission awarded compensation.
- **Issue.** Is a development authority's housing construction a "service", and can compensation be awarded for harassment?
- **Held.** Yes. Housing construction is a service, and a statutory authority is a service provider. Compensation may be awarded for harassment and mental agony, and the authority may recover it from the officials who were at fault.
- **Principle.** A public authority performing a service for a consideration is within the Act; delay in delivery is a deficiency in service.
- **After.** *Ghaziabad Development Authority v. Balbir Singh* (2004) 5 SCC 65 † (compensation may include interest and mental agony, but should be realistic); *Bangalore Development Authority v. Syndicate Bank* (2007) 6 SCC 711 † (allotment of sites is a service, unlike regulatory functions). The 2019 Act expressly lists housing construction in s. 2(42).
- **Used in:** A-115, B-54, C-22.

### 80. *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha* (1995) 6 SCC 651 · Supreme Court, 3 judges
- **Facts.** The Indian Medical Association challenged the application of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 to doctors and hospitals.
- **Issue.** Are medical services "service" under the Act?
- **Held.** Yes, if rendered for a consideration. Services by private doctors and hospitals for a fee are within the Act. Services rendered free to every patient, or under a contract of personal service, are not. Where a hospital charges some patients and treats others free, the free patients are also within the Act. A salaried hospital doctor works under a contract **of** service (the hospital is the service provider); a private practitioner works under a contract **for** services.
- **Principle.** Doctors and hospitals are covered if the service is for a consideration and not under a contract of personal service.
- **After.** *Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia* (1998) 4 SCC 39; *Savita Garg v. Director, National Heart Institute* (2004) 8 SCC 56 †; *Charan Singh v. Healing Touch Hospital* (2000) 7 SCC 668 † (a hospital is liable only for proven negligence); *V. Kishan Rao* (2010) 5 SCC 513. *D.K. Gandhi* (2024) recommended re-examination of *V.P. Shantha* by a larger bench; on 10 February 2026 the Supreme Court issued notice on a petition by healthcare providers seeking to exclude medical professionals from s. 2(42) ‡. No decision found; *V.P. Shantha* stands until then.
- **Used in:** A-82, A-118, B-35, B-51, C-16, C-22.

### 81. *Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases* (2024 INSC 410) · Supreme Court (14 May 2024) ‡
- **Facts.** The National Commission had held that advocates are liable for deficiency in service. The Bar of Indian Lawyers challenged this.
- **Issue.** Are the services of advocates "service" under s. 2(42)?
- **Held.** No. The legal profession is *sui generis* and an advocate works under a contract of **personal service** (the client's control), which is excluded from the definition. The remedy for professional misconduct is under the Advocates Act, 1961. The Court also said that *V.P. Shantha* should be reconsidered by a larger bench ‡.
- **Principle.** Contract of personal service is excluded from "service".
- **After.** The status of the reference on doctors remains open (see above). Check the Supreme Court's website before the exam †.
- **Used in:** A-118, B-51, C-16, C-22.

### 82. *Laxmi Engineering Works v. P.S.G. Industrial Institute* (1995) 3 SCC 583; *Cheema Engineering Services v. Rajan Singh* (1997) 1 SCC 131 †; *Lilavati Kirtilal Mehta Medical Trust v. Unique Shanti Developers* (2019) † · commercial purpose
- **Facts.** *Laxmi Engineering*: a purchaser bought a machine (a lathe or similar) for his industrial institute, which turned out to be defective †. *Cheema*: a person bought machinery to earn his livelihood by self-employment †.
- **Issue.** When is a purchase for a "commercial purpose", so that the buyer is not a consumer?
- **Held.** It is a **question of fact**; the Court looks at the **dominant purpose**. Goods bought for large-scale profit-making are for a commercial purpose; goods bought exclusively for a livelihood by self-employment are not. *Lilavati*: the test is whether there is a close and direct nexus to a profit-generating activity †.
- **Principle.** Commercial-purpose exclusion in s. 2(7), with the self-employment Explanation (a) ‡.
- **Used in:** A-113, B-53, C-22.

### 83. *Pioneer Urban Land & Infrastructure Ltd v. Govindan Raghavan* (2019) 5 SCC 725; *Ireo Grace Realtech v. Abhishek Khanna* (2021) 3 SCC 241; *Imperia Structures Ltd v. Anil Patni* (2020) 10 SCC 783 · flat-buyers
- **Facts.** Flat buyers in Gurgaon paid a large part of the price, but the builders did not hand over possession by the promised date. The builder's agreement contained clauses that were one-sided (e.g., forfeiture on delay by the buyer, but a small sum on delay by the builder).
- **Issue.** Can a buyer be made to wait indefinitely and can the builder rely on one-sided clauses? Do RERA remedies exclude the Act?
- **Held.** The clauses were an **unfair contract/unfair trade practice**; the buyer could not be forced to wait indefinitely and could claim a **refund with interest** (*Pioneer*; *Ireo*). The remedies under RERA and the Consumer Protection Act are **concurrent**; the buyer may choose (*Imperia*).
- **Principle.** A builder-buyer agreement with one-sided terms is an unfair contract (s. 2(46)) and may be an unfair trade practice (s. 2(47)).
- **After.** The 2019 Act expressly defines unfair contracts; State and National Commissions hear unfair-contract complaints.
- **Used in:** A-12, B-05, B-54, B-55, C-02, C-22.

### 84. *Regional Director, ESIC v. Francis De Costa* (1996) 6 SCC 1 † and *General Manager, Telecom v. M. Krishnan* (2009) 8 SCC 481 · services outside the Act
- **Facts.** *De Costa*: a complaint about medical benefit under the Employees' State Insurance scheme †. *Krishnan*: a dispute about a telephone bill.
- **Issue.** Are statutory benefit schemes and telephone-bill disputes within the Act?
- **Held.** *De Costa* †: the ESI medical benefit is not a service for consideration, because the employee contributes under a statute. *Krishnan*: disputes about telephone bills were for arbitration under s. 7B of the Telegraph Act, not for the consumer forum.
- **Principle.** The Act does not apply to services outside its definition, or where another statute provides a special remedy.
- **After.** The 2019 Act names **telecom** as a service in s. 2(42), so check whether *Krishnan* still holds †.
- **Used in:** C-22.
