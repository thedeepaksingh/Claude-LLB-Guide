## B-21 · Discuss vicarious liability with reference to the master-servant relation

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q8 ("Discuss 'Vicarious Liability' with reference to Master-Servant relation."). 20-mark form: C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning and basis 3 · conditions 4 · cases and effect 3

### Answer

**Meaning.** A master is liable for a tort committed by his servant in the course of employment, though he himself is without fault. *Qui facit per alium facit per se*; *respondeat superior*. Reasons: the master controls the work and takes its profit, and can pay and insure.

**Conditions**
1. **A tort by the servant.** Master and servant are jointly and severally liable; the master may claim an indemnity.
2. **A master-servant relationship**, that is, a contract *of* service. The tests are control (*Dharangadhara Chemical Works*, 1957), integration, and the mixed test (*Ready Mixed Concrete*, 1968; *Silver Jubilee Tailoring House*, 1974).
3. **In the course of employment.** *Salmond's test:* an act authorised by the master, or an unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act.

**Cases.** *Limpus* (1862): a driver raced a rival bus against orders; master liable. *Beard* [1900] 2 QB 530: a conductor drove the bus; not liable. *Lloyd v. Grace, Smith* [1912] AC 716: a clerk's fraud; firm liable.

**India.** The State is liable like any master (*Vidhyawati*, AIR 1962 SC 933). The Motor Vehicles Act, ss. 146–149 and 166 protect third parties.

## B-22 · When is a servant's wrong "in the course of his employment"?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q9 (C-10, 20 marks: "main incidents of master's liability for the wrong done by servants in the course of their employment"). 20-mark form: C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Salmond's test 3 · prohibited acts and cases 4 · fraud, crime and modern test 3

### Answer

**Salmond's test.** A wrongful act is in the course of employment if it is (a) **authorised** by the master, or (b) a wrongful and **unauthorised mode of doing an authorised act**.

**Cases**
- *Limpus v. London General Omnibus* (1862): a driver raced a rival bus against orders; liable, as an authorised act done in a prohibited manner.
- *Beard* (1900): a conductor drove the bus; driving was outside his job; not liable.
- *Poland v. John Parr* [1927] 1 KB 236: a carter struck a suspected thief to protect the employer's goods; liable.
- *Century Insurance* [1942] AC 509: a petrol-lorry driver lit a cigarette; liable.
- *Twine v. Bean's Express* (1946): a forbidden lift; not liable.
- *Rose v. Plenty* [1976] 1 WLR 141: a milkman let a boy help despite a ban; liable.
- *Warren v. Henlys* [1948]: assault from personal revenge; not liable.

**Prohibition.** A ban on the *mode* of work does not take an act outside the employment; a ban on the *kind of work* does.

**Fraud and crime.** The master is liable if the wrong is closely connected with the job (*Mohamud v. Morrison* [2016] UKSC 11), not for a servant's own purposes (*Morrison*, [2020] UKSC 12).

## B-23 · When is an employer liable for the torts of an independent contractor?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Dec 2023 Q6 (B-51) and Dec 2025 Q3 (A-44) asked the contrast. 20-mark form: C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** general rule 2 · exceptions with cases 6 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**General rule.** An **independent contractor** works under a contract *for* services and controls how he does the work. His employer is **not** liable for his torts (unlike a master for his servant).

**Exceptions: the employer is liable where**
1. he **authorised or ratified** the wrongful act;
2. he was **negligent in selecting** the contractor or in giving instructions;
3. the work is **inherently dangerous** and the duty is **non-delegable**: *Honeywill & Stein v. Larkin Bros.* [1934] 1 KB 191 (a photographer's magnesium flash set a cinema on fire; employer liable);
4. the work affects **support** or the **highway**: *Bower v. Peate* (1876); *Dalton v. Henry Angus* (1881);
5. a **statutory duty** cannot be delegated;
6. the tort is one of **strict or absolute liability** (*Rylands v. Fletcher*; *M.C. Mehta*);
7. the employer **interferes** and takes control.

**Difference from a servant.**

| | Servant | Independent contractor |
|---|---|---|
| Contract | *Of* service | *For* services |
| Control | What and how | Result only |
| Employer's liability | Yes | Generally no |

**Conclusion.** The exceptions prevent an employer from escaping liability for dangerous work by hiring a contractor.

## B-24 · Explain the liability of the State in tort. Is sovereign immunity still available?

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q10 (C-11, 20 marks). 20-mark form: C-11.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 300 and origin 3 · cases 5 · present position 2

### Answer

**Law.** Art. 300(1): the Union and the States may sue and be sued in the like cases as the Dominion and the Provinces could before the Constitution. Parliament has made no law, so the rule is judge-made.

**Origin.** *P. & O. Steam Navigation Co. v. Secretary of State* (1861): the Government was liable for negligence in a Government dockyard, as it was an undertaking a private person could carry on, but not for sovereign acts (war, acts of State).

**Cases**
- ***Vidhyawati*** AIR 1962 SC 933: a Government jeep driven rashly killed a pedestrian; State liable as any master.
- ***Kasturilal*** AIR 1965 SC 1039: police seized gold, and a constable misappropriated it; State **not** liable, as seizure is a sovereign power. The Court urged legislation.
- ***Nagendra Rao*** (1994) 6 SCC 205: seized foodgrains rotted through negligence; State liable; *Kasturilal* confined to its facts.
- ***Nilabati Behera*** (1993) 2 SCC 746: custodial death; sovereign immunity is no defence to violation of fundamental rights.

**Present position.** The sovereign/non-sovereign test no longer governs ordinary torts. Immunity is confined to core sovereign acts (war, acts of State, legislation, judicial functions). Two months' notice is needed under CPC s. 80.

## B-25 · Explain public-law compensation for violation of fundamental rights by the State

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2024 Q10 (C-11). 20-mark form: C-11.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** concept 3 · cases 5 · relation to tort and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Concept.** Under Arts. 32 and 226 the Supreme Court and High Courts may award **compensation** against the State for violating a fundamental right, particularly Art. 21. It is a remedy in **public law**, independent of the civil action in tort, and the defence of **sovereign immunity does not apply**. Some writers call it "constitutional tort".

**Cases**
- ***Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*** (1983) 4 SCC 141: a man was detained 14 years after acquittal; ₹30,000 awarded.
- ***Bhim Singh v. State of J&K*** (1985) 4 SCC 677: a legislator was illegally detained to stop him attending the Assembly; ₹50,000.
- ***Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa*** (1993) 2 SCC 746: custodial death; compensation under Art. 32, without prejudice to a civil suit.
- ***D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.*** (1997) 1 SCC 416: guidelines for arrest; compensation for custodial death.
- ***Chairman, Railway Board v. Chandrima Das*** (2000) 2 SCC 465: a foreign national was raped at the Railways' guest house; ₹10 lakh; Art. 21 protects non-citizens.
- ***Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity*** (1996): refusal of emergency treatment in government hospitals.

**Relation to tort.** The award is set off against damages in a later civil suit; amounts rise with the facts.

## B-26 · Who are joint tortfeasors? State the nature of their liability

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q4 (A-58, 4 marks: "What is meant by 'Joint Tortfeasors'?"); Dec 2024 Q12 and Dec 2023 Q10 (C-12, 20 marks). 20-mark form: C-12.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · when persons are joint tortfeasors 3 · liability 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** Joint tortfeasors are two or more persons responsible for the **same tort**, so that the cause of action against each is the same (Winfield).

**When (*The Koursk* [1924] P 140, Scrutton L.J.):** (1) agent and principal, or master and servant; (2) breach of a joint duty; (3) concerted action or common design (*Brooke v. Bool* [1928] 2 KB 578: two persons looked for a gas leak with a naked flame and caused an explosion). Persons whose independent acts cause the same damage are **not** joint tortfeasors but concurrent tortfeasors (*Thompson v. LCC* [1899]; *Drinkwater v. Kimber* [1952]).

**Liability**
1. **Joint and several**: each is liable for the whole damage; the plaintiff may sue all or any one, and recover once.
2. **Release** of one releases all (*Cocke v. Jennor*); a covenant not to sue does not.
3. **Judgment** against one barred an action against the others at common law (*Brinsmead v. Harrison*), changed in England by statute.
4. **Contribution** is allowed in England (1935 and 1978 Acts); in India there is no statute †.
5. **Composite negligence** in motor claims: recovery in full from either (*T.O. Anthony*, 2008).
