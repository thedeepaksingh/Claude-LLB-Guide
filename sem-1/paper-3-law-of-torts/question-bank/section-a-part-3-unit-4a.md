<a id="a-63"></a>
**A-63 · Meaning of negligence** · *Related: B-27, C-13*

Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do (Alderson B., *Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks*, 1856). As a tort it is the breach of a legal duty to take care, causing damage to the plaintiff (Winfield). It is also used for carelessness as a state of mind.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-64"></a>
**A-64 · Essential conditions of liability for negligence** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q1*

(1) A legal duty of care owed by the defendant to the plaintiff (*Donoghue v. Stevenson*); (2) breach of that duty by conduct below the standard of a reasonable man; (3) damage to the plaintiff caused by the breach and not too remote (*Wagon Mound*). The plaintiff must prove all three.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-65"></a>
**A-65 · Subjective theory of negligence** · *Related: B-27, C-13*

Supported by Austin and earlier Salmond, it regards negligence as a state of mind: culpable carelessness or inadvertence. A person who did his honest best is not negligent. It is criticised for confusing negligence with mental attitude, for being unable to explain wilful neglect, and for making proof difficult. The courts do not follow it in the civil law.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-66"></a>
**A-66 · Objective theory of negligence** · *Related: B-27, C-13*

Supported by Winfield, Pollock and Holmes, it regards negligence as a type of conduct: failure to reach the standard of the reasonable man, whatever the defendant's state of mind. *Vaughan v. Menlove* (1837): that a farmer acted to the best of his judgment was no defence. It is the theory the courts apply, though it may be harsh on persons of low ability.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-67"></a>
**A-67 · *Donoghue v. Stevenson* (1932)** · *Related: B-28, C-13*

Mrs Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle, bought by a friend, that contained a decomposed snail, and was ill. With no contract with the manufacturer, she sued him. The House of Lords (3:2) held that a manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer. It stated the neighbour principle and the general duty of care.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-68"></a>
**A-68 · The neighbour principle** · *Related: B-28*

Lord Atkin in *Donoghue v. Stevenson*: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour." Neighbours are persons so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation. It is the foundation of the modern duty of care.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-69"></a>
**A-69 · The *Caparo* test** · *Related: B-28, C-13*

*Caparo Industries v. Dickman* (1990) requires, for a duty of care: (1) reasonable foreseeability of damage; (2) proximity between the parties; and (3) that it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Courts proceed incrementally, by analogy with decided cases. It replaced the two-stage test in *Anns v. Merton*, which was overruled in *Murphy v. Brentwood* (1991).

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-70"></a>
**A-70 · The reasonable man** · *Related: B-29*

The standard of care is that of the reasonable man ("the man on the Clapham omnibus"), an objective standard. He weighs the probability and gravity of harm, the cost of precautions and the utility of the act. Special standards: professionals (*Bolam*), learners (*Nettleship v. Weston*: competent driver), children (the standard of a child of that age).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-71"></a>
**A-71 · The *Bolam* test** · *Related: B-35, C-16*

*Bolam v. Friern Hospital* (1957): a doctor is not negligent if he acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion skilled in that art, even if others would have acted differently. *Bolitho* (1998) adds that the opinion must be logical. For disclosure of risks the test is the material risk (*Montgomery*, 2015).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-72"></a>
**A-72 · The "but for" test** · *Related: B-30*

Causation in fact asks: would the damage have happened but for the defendant's breach? If yes, there is no liability. *Barnett v. Chelsea & Kensington HMC* (1969): a doctor sent away a man poisoned with arsenic, but he would have died anyway; the negligence did not cause death. Where several causes contribute, a material contribution suffices (*Bonnington Castings*, 1956).

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-73"></a>
**A-73 · *The Wagon Mound* (1961)** · *Related: B-30, C-13*

Oil spilled by the defendant's ship in Sydney harbour was ignited by sparks from welding and burned the plaintiff's wharf. The Privy Council held that only damage of a kind that was reasonably foreseeable is recoverable, and replaced the direct-consequences test in *Re Polemis* (1921). Foreseeability of fire damage was not established, so the claim in negligence failed.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-74"></a>
**A-74 · Thin-skull (egg-shell skull) rule** · *Related: B-30*

A defendant must take his victim as he finds him: if some injury is foreseeable, he is liable for the full extent of it, though the victim's special weakness made it worse than expected (*Smith v. Leech Brain*, 1962: a burn on the lip triggered a pre-existing cancer). The rule applies to both physical and psychiatric injury, once the kind of harm is foreseeable.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-75"></a>
**A-75 · *Res ipsa loquitur*** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q3 and Dec 2023 Q2*

"The thing speaks for itself." Where the accident is of a kind that does not ordinarily happen without negligence, and the thing was under the defendant's control, the accident is itself evidence of negligence and the defendant must explain (*Byrne v. Boadle*, 1863; *Scott v. London & St Katherine Docks*, 1865). In India: *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* (1966).

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-76"></a>
**A-76 · Conditions of *res ipsa loquitur*** · *Related: B-32, C-14*

(1) The thing causing damage was under the defendant's management or control; (2) the accident is such as in the ordinary course of things does not happen if proper care is used; (3) the cause is unknown to the plaintiff and unexplained by the defendant. It shifts the evidential burden to the defendant, not the legal burden (*Henderson v. Henry E. Jenkins*, 1970).

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-77"></a>
**A-77 · Contributory negligence** · *Related: B-33, C-14*

The plaintiff's failure to take reasonable care for his own safety, which helps cause his damage. It was once a complete bar (*Butterfield v. Forrester*, 1809); now damages are reduced in proportion to his share of responsibility (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945, UK). Indian courts also apportion. *Froom v. Butcher* (1976): 25% off for not wearing a seat-belt.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-78"></a>
**A-78 · *Davies v. Mann* (1842) and the last opportunity rule** · *Related: B-31, B-33*

The plaintiff left his donkey in the road with its forefeet tied; the defendant's wagon, driven too fast, ran it down. The defendant was held liable because he had the last opportunity of avoiding the accident, though the plaintiff had been careless. The rule was narrowed later (*Loach*, 1916; *Swadling v. Cooper*, 1931) and replaced by apportionment.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-79"></a>
**A-79 · Nervous shock** · *Related: B-34, C-15*

A recognisable psychiatric illness caused by a sudden shock from what a person sees or hears, such as an accident to another. Mere grief or fright is not enough. The law developed from *Coultas* (1888) through *Dulieu v. White* (1901), *Hambrook* (1925), *Bourhill v. Young* (1943), *McLoughlin* (1983) and *Alcock* (1992), which laid down control mechanisms for secondary victims.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-80"></a>
**A-80 · Primary and secondary victims** · *Related: B-34, C-15*

A primary victim is within the zone of physical danger and need only show that some personal injury was foreseeable (*Page v. Smith*, 1996). A secondary victim, who witnesses harm to another, must show a close tie of love and affection, proximity in time and space, perception by own unaided senses, and a sudden shock (*Alcock*, 1992).

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-81"></a>
**A-81 · *Hedley Byrne v. Heller* (1964)** · *Related: B-36*

The House of Lords held that a duty of care may arise for careless words causing economic loss where there is a special relationship and the speaker assumes responsibility, knowing that the other will rely on the statement. The bank there escaped liability because of a disclaimer. It created liability for negligent misstatement, distinct from deceit, which needs fraud.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-82"></a>
**A-82 · Medical negligence** · *Related: B-35, C-16*

A doctor must show the skill of an ordinary competent member of the profession; an error of judgment is not negligence (*Bolam*; *Laxman Joshi*, 1969). He owes duties in deciding to take the case, in deciding the treatment and in giving it. Treatment without informed consent is a battery (*Samira Kohli*). Hospitals are vicariously liable; paid medical services are "service" (*V.P. Shantha*).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-83"></a>
**A-83 · *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005)** · *Related: B-35, C-16*

For criminal prosecution of a doctor for causing death by negligence, the negligence must be gross, a high degree of negligence or recklessness. A private complaint should be backed by the credible opinion of another competent doctor, and the police should obtain an independent medical opinion first. See BNS s. 106(1): up to two years for a registered medical practitioner ‡.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-84"></a>
**A-84 · Employer's duty to employees** · *Related: C-13*

An employer owes a personal, non-delegable duty to take reasonable care for his employees' safety, by providing competent staff, adequate plant and equipment, a safe place of work and a safe system of work (*Wilsons & Clyde Coal v. English*, 1938). Breach gives a negligence claim; the Employees' Compensation Act, 1923 gives statutory no-fault compensation.

*Word count: 54*
