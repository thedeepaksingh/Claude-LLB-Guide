## C-13 · What do you mean by negligence? Explain, with the help of the two rival theories, the nature and definition of negligence. What are its essentials?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q12 ("What do you mean by negligence? Explain with the help of two rival theories the nature and definition of negligence", 20 marks). Short forms: Dec 2022 Q1 (A-64, essential conditions of liability for negligence). Related: A-63–A-74, B-27–B-30.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and definitions 3 · two theories 6 · essentials: duty 4, breach 3, damage 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** *Negligence* has two meanings in law. As a **state of mind** it means carelessness, the opposite of intention, and it is a mental element in other torts and in crime. As an **independent tort** it means the breach of a legal duty to take care which results in damage to the plaintiff. This answer deals with both, as the question asks for the theories.

**1. Definitions**
- **Alderson B., *Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co.*** (1856) 11 Ex 781: "Negligence is the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided upon those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do."
- **Winfield & Jolowicz:** the breach of a legal duty to take care which results in damage, undesired by the defendant, to the plaintiff.
- **Salmond:** negligence is the breach of a duty to take care; the word is used both for a type of conduct and for a mental element.

**2. The two rival theories**

| | **Subjective theory** | **Objective theory** |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Negligence is a **state of mind**: culpable carelessness or inadvertence | Negligence is a **type of conduct**: failure to reach the standard of the reasonable man |
| Supporters | **Austin**, and earlier **Salmond** | **Winfield**, **Pollock**, **Holmes** |
| Test | Was the defendant actually careless, did he fail to think of the consequence? | Did he fall below the standard of a reasonable man? His state of mind is irrelevant |
| Effect | A person who did his honest best is not negligent | A person of low ability is still negligent if he falls below the standard: *Vaughan v. Menlove* (1837), where the defendant built a haystack that heated and caught fire, and "the best of his judgment" was no defence |
| Criticism | Confuses negligence with mental attitude; cannot explain **wilful** neglect (one who knows the risk and does nothing is not "inadvertent"); hard to prove | Ignores individual capacity; harsh on beginners and the young |

*Position.* The courts apply the **objective** test, and negligence is a breach of duty, not a state of mind. The subjective idea survives in the criminal law, where negligence is the mental element (BNS ss. 106, 125, 281). Even in tort the objective standard is adjusted for children (judged by a child of the same age), professionals (the skill of an ordinary competent member of the profession) and learners (*Nettleship v. Weston*: the competent-driver standard, which is objective).

**3. Essentials of the tort of negligence**

```
Duty of care  →  Breach of the duty  →  Damage caused by the breach (not too remote)
```

**(a) A legal duty of care owed by the defendant to the plaintiff**
- *Heaven v. Pender* (1883): a duty arises where, without ordinary care, a person would be in danger.
- ***Donoghue v. Stevenson*** [1932] AC 562. A friend bought Mrs Donoghue a bottle of ginger beer at a café. The opaque bottle contained the decomposed remains of a snail, and she became ill. She had no contract with the café or the manufacturer. The House of Lords (3:2) held that the manufacturer owed a duty to the **ultimate consumer**. **Lord Atkin's neighbour principle:** "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour"; neighbours are "persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation".
- ***Caparo Industries v. Dickman*** [1990] 2 AC 605: the present test has three parts: (i) **foreseeability** of damage; (ii) **proximity** between the parties; (iii) it is **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty. Courts now work **incrementally**, by analogy with decided cases. (*Anns v. Merton* [1978], a two-stage test, was overruled by *Murphy v. Brentwood* [1991].)
- ***Hedley Byrne v. Heller & Partners*** [1964] AC 465: a duty arises for careless advice where there is a **special relationship** and an **assumption of responsibility**, even without a contract.
- **Pure economic loss:** *Spartan Steel v. Martin & Co.* [1973] QB 27: a contractor cut an electric cable and stopped the plaintiff's factory; loss from the melt in progress (physical damage) was recoverable, but the profit lost during the stoppage was not.
- **Recognised duty relationships:** manufacturer and consumer; driver and road users; doctor and patient; employer and employee; occupier and visitor; solicitor and client; teacher and pupil.
- **India.** The Indian courts accept the *Donoghue* principle: *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* AIR 1966 SC 1750 (a municipal body and its clock tower); *M.S. Grewal v. Deep Chand Sood* (2001) 8 SCC 151 † (a school and the students in its charge on an outing).

**(b) Breach of the duty.** The standard is that of the **reasonable man** ("the man on the Clapham omnibus"). The court weighs:

| Factor | Case |
|---|---|
| Probability of harm | *Bolton v. Stone* [1951] AC 850: a cricket ball was hit out of the ground about six times in thirty years and hurt a passer-by; the risk was so small that a reasonable man need not guard against it |
| Gravity of harm | *Paris v. Stepney BC* [1951] AC 367: a one-eyed mechanic lost his remaining eye; the employer should have given goggles because the harm to him would be graver |
| Cost of precautions | *Latimer v. A.E.C.* [1953] AC 643: the factory floor was flooded and slippery; sawdust was spread; the employer did all that was reasonable |
| Social utility | *Watt v. Hertfordshire CC* [1954] 1 WLR 835: a fireman was hurt in an emergency lifting a heavy jack; the risk was justified by the aim of saving life |
| Skilled persons | *Bolam v. Friern Hospital* [1957] 1 WLR 582; *Bolitho v. City & Hackney HA* [1998] AC 232 (C-16) |

**(c) Damage resulting from the breach**
- **Causation in fact: the "but for" test.** *Barnett v. Chelsea & Kensington HMC* [1969] 1 QB 428: a casualty doctor sent away a man who had been poisoned with arsenic; he died. He would have died even with treatment, so the negligence was not the cause and there was no liability. Where several causes contribute: *Bonnington Castings v. Wardlaw* [1956] AC 613 (material contribution).
- **Intervening act (*novus actus interveniens*).** A fresh act of a third party or of the plaintiff that breaks the chain of causation: *McKew v. Holland & Hannen & Cubitts* [1969] 3 All ER 1621 (the plaintiff's unreasonable act broke the chain). A natural response to danger does not, as in *Scott v. Shepherd* (1773) (the squib case).
- **Remoteness.** *Re Polemis* [1921] 3 KB 560 (all direct consequences recoverable) was replaced by ***The Wagon Mound (No. 1)*** [1961] AC 388: oil spilled in Sydney harbour; sparks from welding set it alight and burned the wharf; only damage of a **kind reasonably foreseeable** is recoverable. *Hughes v. Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837: boys playing with a paraffin lamp near a manhole were burned in an explosion; the **kind** of harm (burns) was foreseeable though not the way it happened. The **thin-skull rule**: the defendant takes the victim as he finds him (*Smith v. Leech Brain* [1962] 2 QB 405).

**4. Proof and defences.** The plaintiff bears the burden of proving the three elements; *res ipsa loquitur* may help him (C-14). The defences are contributory negligence (C-14), *volenti non fit injuria* (C-07), inevitable accident and statutory authority (C-08, C-09).

**5. Remedies.** Damages for personal injury (pain and suffering, loss of earnings, medical costs, loss of amenities), for death (Fatal Accidents Act, 1855; Motor Vehicles Act, s. 166) and for property damage.

**Conclusion.** Negligence is the breach of a legal duty of care, measured against the reasonable man, which causes foreseeable damage. The objective theory is the one the courts apply, and the tort rests on duty, breach and damage.

## C-14 · Explain the doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur*, and discuss contributory negligence as a defence in an action for negligence

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. *Res ipsa loquitur* is set at 4 marks in Dec 2024 Q3 and Dec 2023 Q2 (A-75). Contributory negligence is within the syllabus line "negligence". Related: A-75–A-78, B-32, B-33.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** *res ipsa loquitur*: meaning, cases, conditions, effect, India 10 · contributory negligence: rule, cases, Indian law, distinctions 9 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** In an action for negligence the plaintiff must ordinarily prove duty, breach and damage. *Res ipsa loquitur* helps him to prove **breach** where he cannot know what happened. Contributory negligence is the defence that the plaintiff's own carelessness helped to cause his injury.

**PART A: RES IPSA LOQUITUR**

**1. Meaning.** "The thing speaks for itself." In some accidents the fact of the accident, taken with the circumstances, is itself evidence of the defendant's negligence, so that the plaintiff need not prove how the defendant was negligent.

**2. Cases**
- ***Byrne v. Boadle*** (1863) 2 H & C 722. A barrel of flour fell from a window of the defendant's warehouse and hit a passer-by. The court held that the accident itself was evidence of negligence; barrels do not fall out of windows without somebody's carelessness.
- ***Scott v. London & St Katherine Docks*** (1865) 3 H & C 596. Sugar bags fell from a crane. Erle C.J. stated the rule: where the thing is shown to be under the management of the defendant or his servants, and the accident is such as in the ordinary course of things does not happen if those who have the management use proper care, it affords reasonable evidence, in the absence of explanation by the defendants, that the accident arose from want of care.

**3. Conditions**
1. The thing that caused the harm was under the **control** (management) of the defendant or his servants.
2. The accident is of a kind that **does not ordinarily happen without negligence**.
3. The **actual cause is unknown** to the plaintiff, and the defendant offers no explanation.
If the facts are fully known, there is nothing for the doctrine to say.

**4. Effect.** It shifts the **evidential burden** to the defendant, who must show how the accident could have happened without negligence. It does not (normally) shift the legal burden of proof (*Henderson v. Henry E. Jenkins* [1970] AC 282). It is a rule of evidence, not of law, and the court may still find that the defendant's explanation is enough.

**5. Indian cases.** ***Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti*** AIR 1966 SC 1750: the clock tower in Chandni Chowk, Delhi, fell and killed several people. The Supreme Court applied the doctrine: the structure was in the Corporation's control, towers do not ordinarily collapse if properly inspected, and the Corporation could not show that it had taken due care. *Shyam Sunder v. State of Rajasthan* AIR 1974 SC 890 † accepted the doctrine in India. *Syad Akbar v. State of Karnataka* AIR 1979 SC 1848 † shows its limits: it does not help where the cause is known. It has also been applied to medical negligence (*V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital* (2010) 5 SCC 513 †) and to motor-accident claims.

**PART B: CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE**

**6. Meaning.** The plaintiff has himself failed to take reasonable care for his own safety, and that failure has contributed to his damage.

**7. Development**
- **Common law: a complete bar.** ***Butterfield v. Forrester*** (1809) 11 East 60: the defendant had left a pole across the road. The plaintiff, riding violently at dusk, did not see it and was thrown. He could not recover; if he had been using ordinary care he would have seen it.
- **The "last opportunity" mitigation.** ***Davies v. Mann*** (1842) 10 M & W 546 (the *donkey case*): the plaintiff left his donkey on the road with its forefeet tied. The defendant's wagon, going too fast, ran it down. The defendant was liable, because he had the **last opportunity** to avoid the accident. *British Columbia Electric Rly v. Loach* [1916] 1 AC 719 and *Swadling v. Cooper* [1931] AC 1 narrowed the rule.
- **Apportionment.** The Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945 (UK), s. 1 replaced the all-or-nothing rule: damages are reduced "to such extent as the court thinks just and equitable having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility for the damage".

**8. Illustrations of apportionment**
- ***Froom v. Butcher*** [1976] QB 286: a 25% reduction for not wearing a seat-belt (15% if it would have made the injuries less severe). ***Capps v. Miller*** [1989] 1 WLR 839: 10% for an unfastened motor-cycle helmet.
- ***Jones v. Livox Quarries*** [1952] 2 QB 608: a worker riding on the back of a vehicle against orders was partly to blame for his injury.
- **Children** are judged by the standard of their age (*Gough v. Thorne* [1966] 1 WLR 1387). A **rescuer's** rashness may lead to a reduction.

**9. Indian law.** There is no general statute on apportionment, but the Indian courts follow the English rule: contributory negligence **reduces** the damages and is not a bar; in motor-accident claims the tribunals and courts apportion the blame (for example, between the driver and a pedestrian who crossed carelessly). The Motor Vehicles Act, s. 129 (protective headgear) and s. 138(3) with the Central Motor Vehicles Rules (seat belts) †, make failure to wear them evidence of carelessness.

**10. Distinctions**

| | Contributory negligence | Composite negligence | *Volenti* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose fault | The **plaintiff's** own | Two or more **defendants** | The plaintiff's **consent** to the risk |
| Effect | Damages reduced | Plaintiff recovers all from any defendant | Complete defence |
| Case | *Froom v. Butcher* | *T.O. Anthony v. Karvarnan* (2008) 3 SCC 748 | *Smith v. Baker* |

**Conclusion.** *Res ipsa loquitur* is an aid to proof that moves the burden of explanation to the defendant when the circumstances themselves suggest negligence; it applies in India, as *Subhagwanti* shows. Contributory negligence, once a complete bar, now only reduces damages in proportion to the plaintiff's share of responsibility.

## C-15 · In the light of the principle of negligence, discuss the concept of "nervous shock", referring to relevant decided cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q12 (20 marks; this wording). Short form: Dec 2024 Q8 (B-34, 10 marks). Related: A-79, A-80.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and link with duty 3 · the cases in order 9 · primary and secondary victims, Alcock control mechanisms 5 · India and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** "Nervous shock" is the term used for a **recognisable psychiatric illness** caused by what a person sees or hears, usually an accident to another person. It is not enough to be upset, grieved or frightened. The questions are whether a **duty of care** was owed to the plaintiff, whether the illness was a **foreseeable** result of the defendant's carelessness, and whether it was caused by the shock. The courts have developed the law cautiously, because they fear a flood of claims.

**1. Early law**
1. ***Victorian Railways Commissioners v. Coultas*** (1888) 13 App Cas 222 (Privy Council). The plaintiff was frightened by a near-collision with a train at a level crossing and became ill. No recovery: shock without physical impact was too remote.
2. ***Dulieu v. White*** [1901] 2 KB 669. A barmaid, who was pregnant, was frightened when the defendant's horse-van crashed into the pub, and she became seriously ill. Recovery was allowed for illness caused by **fear for her own safety**.
3. ***Hambrook v. Stokes Bros*** [1925] 1 KB 141. A mother saw a runaway lorry going down the hill where her children were; she feared for them, and later heard that one was injured, and she died. Recovery was allowed for shock caused by fear for **others** (the children).
4. ***Bourhill v. Young*** [1943] AC 92. A pregnant fishwife heard (but did not see) a motorcycle collision some distance away, and later saw blood on the road; she claimed that she had suffered shock. The House of Lords refused recovery: the motorcyclist owed no duty to a person outside the area of foreseeable harm, and a person of **normal fortitude** is the test.

**2. Modern law**
5. ***McLoughlin v. O'Brian*** [1983] 1 AC 410. The plaintiff was told of a road accident to her family, and saw them in hospital about two hours later, still covered in blood and mud and in distress. She suffered severe shock. The House of Lords allowed recovery: she came on the **immediate aftermath**.
6. ***Alcock v. Chief Constable of South Yorkshire*** [1992] 1 AC 310 (the Hillsborough disaster). Relatives who watched the football disaster on television, or identified bodies hours later, were denied recovery. The House laid down the **control mechanisms** for a **secondary victim** (one who is not in physical danger himself):
   - a **close tie of love and affection** with the victim (presumed for spouses and parent-child);
   - **proximity in time and space** to the accident or its immediate aftermath;
   - perception by the plaintiff's **own unaided senses** (a live broadcast is not enough);
   - a **sudden shock** and a recognised psychiatric illness.
7. ***Page v. Smith*** [1996] AC 155 distinguished **primary victims** (within the zone of physical danger; need only show that some personal injury was foreseeable) from **secondary victims**, who must meet the *Alcock* tests.
8. ***White v. Chief Constable of South Yorkshire*** [1999] 2 AC 455: police officers on duty at Hillsborough were neither primary victims nor "rescuers" in the relevant sense, and their claims failed.
9. ***Wilkinson v. Downton*** [1897] 2 QB 57: a practical joker told a woman that her husband had broken both his legs; she became ill. The defendant was liable for the **intentional** infliction of nervous shock (the principle was explained in *Rhodes v. OPO* [2015] UKSC 32 †).
10. **Stress at work:** *Walker v. Northumberland CC* [1995] 1 All ER 737 (an employer owes a duty to protect employees from foreseeable psychiatric injury caused by overwork).

**3. Summary of the conditions today**
- The plaintiff must suffer a **recognised psychiatric illness**, not mere grief.
- Primary victims: foreseeability of **some** physical or psychiatric injury is enough.
- Secondary victims: the four *Alcock* conditions; a person of **normal fortitude** is the test of foreseeability.
- The plaintiff must have been present or come on the **immediate aftermath**.
- The thin-skull rule applies once the duty is established.

**4. England since.** *Paul v. Royal Wolverhampton NHS Trust* [2024] UKSC 1 refused to extend secondary-victim claims to relatives who witnessed a death caused by medical negligence †. (Check for later developments.)

**5. India.** The Indian courts accept that damages are available for mental suffering caused by negligence, but I did not find a leading Supreme Court decision setting out the nervous-shock rules (†). Tribunals award compensation for **loss of consortium** and other non-pecuniary heads in death cases (*National Insurance Co. v. Pranay Sethi* (2017) 16 SCC 680), and the Consumer Commissions award compensation for mental agony. Indian judges cite the English cases as persuasive.

**Conclusion.** The law of nervous shock is an application of the ordinary principles of duty, foreseeability and causation, with control mechanisms added to limit liability. The trend runs from no recovery (*Coultas*), through recovery for fear for oneself (*Dulieu*) and for others (*Hambrook*), to the present restricted rules (*Alcock*).

## C-16 · Discuss professional negligence, with particular reference to the liability of doctors

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Related: A-82, A-83, B-35.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** standard of care: *Bolam* and *Bolitho* 5 · duties of a doctor and civil liability, Indian cases 6 · criminal liability: *Jacob Mathew*, BNS s. 106 5 · consent, hospitals, consumer law 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** A professional person (doctor, lawyer, architect, engineer, accountant) who holds himself out as having special skill owes a duty to use that skill with reasonable care. Medical negligence is the commonest case. It may lead to a civil claim in tort, a complaint under the Consumer Protection Act, or a criminal prosecution.

**1. The standard of care**
- ***Bolam v. Friern Hospital Management Committee*** [1957] 1 WLR 582. The patient underwent electro-convulsive therapy without a relaxant drug or restraint and fractured his pelvis. McNair J. held that a doctor is **not negligent if he acts in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion** skilled in that art, even if other doctors would have acted differently.
- ***Bolitho v. City & Hackney Health Authority*** [1998] AC 232: the professional opinion must be **logical** and able to withstand analysis; the court is not bound by it if it is not.
- ***Montgomery v. Lanarkshire Health Board*** [2015] UKSC 11: on advice about risks, the test is what a reasonable patient would regard as a **material risk**, and not what the doctors consider enough to disclose (a departure from *Bolam* in cases about consent).
- The standard is the skill of an **ordinary competent member** of the profession, not the highest skill. A mere **error of judgment**, or a treatment that fails, is not negligence.

**2. The Indian law**
- ***Dr. Laxman Balkrishna Joshi v. Dr. Trimbak Bapu Godbole*** AIR 1969 SC 128. A boy with a fractured elbow was treated by a doctor who tried to set it without adequate anaesthesia and the boy died of shock †. The Supreme Court held the doctor liable and stated that a medical practitioner owes **three duties**: a duty of care in **deciding whether to take the case**, in **deciding what treatment to give**, and in **administering that treatment**.
- ***Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel*** (1996) 4 SCC 332 †: a person who practises a system of medicine in which he is not qualified is negligent *per se*.
- ***Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia*** (1998) 4 SCC 39: parents may claim for the negligent treatment of a child; the hospital was liable for its doctors and nurses.
- ***Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda*** (2008) 2 SCC 1: treatment without **informed consent** is a trespass to the person; consent to one procedure does not cover another unless it is an emergency.
- ***Martin F. D'Souza v. Mohd. Ishfaq*** (2009) 3 SCC 1: guidelines on obtaining expert opinion before action; later explained and relaxed in ***V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital*** (2010) 5 SCC 513 †, which also held that *res ipsa loquitur* can apply in a medical case.
- ***Kusum Sharma v. Batra Hospital*** (2010) 3 SCC 480: a summary of principles; a doctor is not liable merely because the result was bad, and professionals should not be harassed.
- **Hospitals** are vicariously liable for their staff (Unit 3); *Savita Garg v. Director, National Heart Institute* (2004) 8 SCC 56 †.

**3. Criminal liability**
- ***Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab*** (2005) 6 SCC 1. A patient with breathing difficulty in a Ludhiana hospital died after the oxygen cylinder was found empty; the doctor was prosecuted for causing death by negligence. The Supreme Court held that for a professional to be criminally liable the negligence must be **gross**: a very high degree of negligence, recklessness. Mere lack of care or an error of judgment is not enough. A private complaint should not be entertained unless it is supported by the **credible opinion of another competent doctor**, and an investigating officer should obtain an independent opinion, preferably from a government doctor, before proceeding; arrest should not be routine.
- ***Dr. Suresh Gupta v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi*** (2004) 6 SCC 422: the same standard.
- **BNS s. 106(1)** (formerly IPC s. 304A): causing death by a rash or negligent act is punishable with up to **five years**' imprisonment and fine; where the act is done by a **registered medical practitioner** in a medical procedure, the term is up to **two years** and fine ‡.

**4. Consumer law.** Medical services given for payment are "service" under the Consumer Protection Act: *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha* (1995) 6 SCC 651; free services are excluded, and services under a contract of personal service are excluded (Unit 5). In *Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi* (2024) the Supreme Court held advocates' services outside the Act and said *Shantha* should be reconsidered by a larger bench ‡; on 10 February 2026 notice was issued on a petition to exclude qualified doctors ‡. Until a larger bench decides, *Shantha* stands.

**5. Defences.** The doctor is not liable if he followed an accepted practice (*Bolam*), obtained valid consent, or if the harm was an inherent risk of the treatment which was properly explained. Contributory negligence by the patient (e.g. ignoring advice) reduces damages.

**6. Other professionals.** Lawyers, architects, engineers and accountants owe similar duties; a mere error of judgment is not negligence, but ignorance of basic law or failure to follow instructions can be.

**Conclusion.** A professional is judged by the standard of an ordinary competent member of the profession. Civil liability rests on a breach of this standard; criminal liability needs gross negligence. The Supreme Court protects doctors against harassment while holding hospitals and practitioners accountable. The law on whether doctors are covered by the Consumer Protection Act is under reconsideration.
