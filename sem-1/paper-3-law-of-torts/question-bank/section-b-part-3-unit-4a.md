## B-27 · What are the essential conditions of liability for negligence?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q1 (A-64, 4 marks: "What are essential conditions of liability for negligence?"). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 2 · three elements with cases 6 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Definition.** *Alderson B., Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks* (1856): the omission to do something which a reasonable man would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do. As a tort it is the breach of a legal duty to take care, causing damage (Winfield).

**Essentials**
1. **A legal duty of care** owed by the defendant to the plaintiff. *Donoghue v. Stevenson* [1932] AC 562: a manufacturer owes a duty to the ultimate consumer. The test now: foreseeability, proximity, and that it is fair, just and reasonable (*Caparo v. Dickman*, 1990).
2. **Breach of the duty**: failure to reach the standard of the reasonable man, judged by the probability and gravity of harm, cost of precautions and social utility (*Bolton v. Stone*, 1951; *Paris v. Stepney BC*, 1951; *Latimer v. A.E.C.*, 1953).
3. **Damage caused by the breach**, and not too remote: "but for" causation (*Barnett v. Chelsea Hospital*, 1969); damage of a foreseeable kind (*The Wagon Mound*, 1961).

The plaintiff proves all three, helped sometimes by *res ipsa loquitur*.

**Conclusion.** Without a duty, a breach or resulting damage, there is no liability. Defences: contributory negligence, *volenti*, inevitable accident.

## B-28 · Explain how the law decides whether a duty of care exists

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2022 Q12 (C-13). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** *Donoghue* 4 · *Caparo* 3 · special cases and India 3

### Answer

***Donoghue v. Stevenson*** [1932] AC 562. A friend bought Mrs Donoghue ginger beer in an opaque bottle; it contained a decomposed snail and she was ill. She had no contract with the manufacturer. The House of Lords (3:2) held that the manufacturer owed a duty to the **ultimate consumer**. **Lord Atkin's neighbour principle:** take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour: persons "so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation".

***Caparo Industries v. Dickman*** [1990] 2 AC 605: a duty exists if (i) harm is **reasonably foreseeable**; (ii) there is **proximity** between the parties; and (iii) it is **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty. Courts now decide **incrementally**, by analogy. 

**Special cases.** Careless statements: *Hedley Byrne v. Heller* [1964] AC 465 (special relationship; assumption of responsibility). Pure economic loss is generally not recoverable (*Spartan Steel*, 1973).

**India.** The courts apply *Donoghue*: *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* (1966) (a clock tower collapsed); *M.S. Grewal v. Deep Chand Sood* (2001) † (a school's duty to students on an outing).

## B-29 · Explain the standard of care required of a reasonable man

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2022 Q1 (A-64). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** the standard 3 · factors with cases 5 · special classes 2

### Answer

**Standard.** The defendant must act as a **reasonable man** ("the man on the Clapham omnibus") would. The test is **objective**. *Vaughan v. Menlove* (1837): a farmer's haystack caught fire; that he acted to the best of his judgment was no defence. *Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks* (1856).

**Factors**
- **Probability of harm:** *Bolton v. Stone* [1951] AC 850: a cricket ball hit out of the ground about six times in thirty years injured a passer-by; the risk was too small to guard against.
- **Gravity of harm:** *Paris v. Stepney BC* [1951] AC 367: a one-eyed mechanic lost his sight; goggles were needed for him.
- **Cost of precautions:** *Latimer v. A.E.C.* [1953] AC 643: sawdust on a flooded floor was reasonable.
- **Social utility:** *Watt v. Hertfordshire CC* [1954] 1 WLR 835: a fireman hurt in an emergency; the risk was justified by saving life.

**Special classes**
- **Professionals:** the skill of an ordinary competent member (*Bolam*, 1957; *Bolitho*, 1998).
- **Learners:** the standard of a competent driver (*Nettleship v. Weston*, 1971).
- **Children:** the standard of a child of the same age (*Mullin v. Richards*, 1998).

**Conclusion.** The standard is fixed by the court, not by the defendant's own ability.

## B-30 · Explain causation and remoteness of damage in negligence

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2022 Q1 (A-64). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** causation in fact 3 · intervening act 2 · remoteness with cases 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Causation in fact: the "but for" test.** The damage must be one that would not have happened but for the breach. *Barnett v. Chelsea & Kensington HMC* [1969] 1 QB 428: a casualty doctor sent away a man poisoned with arsenic; he died, but would have died anyway; no liability. Where several causes contribute, a material contribution is enough (*Bonnington Castings v. Wardlaw*, 1956).

**Intervening act (*novus actus interveniens*).** A fresh, independent act of a third party or of the plaintiff breaks the chain (*McKew v. Holland & Hannen*, 1969). A natural response to danger does not (*Scott v. Shepherd*, 1773).

**Remoteness.** *Re Polemis* [1921] 3 KB 560 (all direct consequences) was replaced by ***The Wagon Mound (No. 1)*** [1961] AC 388: oil spilled in Sydney harbour was set alight by sparks from welding and burned a wharf; only damage of a **kind reasonably foreseeable** is recoverable. *Hughes v. Lord Advocate* [1963] AC 837: boys with a paraffin lamp were burned in an explosion; burns were foreseeable though the manner was not. **Thin-skull rule:** the defendant takes his victim as he finds him (*Smith v. Leech Brain*, 1962).

**Conclusion.** Liability needs actual causation and foreseeable damage.

## B-31 · (Problem) A horse is left on the road with its leg tied. A scooterist runs into it and injures it. Can the owner of the horse recover damages?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q7 (10 marks, problem question). Related: *Davies v. Mann* (the "donkey case"); C-14 (contributory negligence).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** issue 1 · rule 3 · application 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Facts.** A horse was left on the road with a leg tied; a scooterist ran into it and injured it.

**Issue.** Is the scooterist liable in negligence, though the owner's own conduct contributed?

**Rule.** Negligence needs a duty, breach and damage. A road user must take reasonable care for others, including animals on the road. ***Davies v. Mann*** (1842): a donkey left on the road with its forefeet tied was run down by a wagon going too fast; the driver, who had the **last opportunity** to avoid it, was liable. Today the owner's carelessness is contributory negligence and **reduces** damages (1945 Act in England; Indian courts apportion blame).

**Application.** The scooterist had to keep a proper look-out and a safe speed. If the horse was visible and ordinary care would have stopped him, he was negligent, as in *Davies v. Mann*. The owner was also careless in leaving a tied horse on a public road, and shares the blame.

**Conclusion.** The owner **can recover**, with damages reduced by his share. If reasonable care could not have revealed the horse (night, a bend), there is no breach, and the claim fails.

*Word count: 190*

## B-32 · Explain the doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur*

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q3 and Dec 2023 Q2 (A-75, 4 marks each: "Explain 'Res Ipsa Loquitur'"). 20-mark form: C-14.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · cases 3 · conditions 3 · effect and India 2

### Answer

***Res ipsa loquitur*** means "the thing speaks for itself". The accident itself is evidence of negligence, so the plaintiff need not prove how the defendant was careless.

**Cases**
- ***Byrne v. Boadle*** (1863) 2 H & C 722: a barrel of flour fell from a warehouse window and hit a passer-by; the accident was evidence of negligence.
- ***Scott v. London & St Katherine Docks*** (1865) 3 H & C 596: sugar bags fell from a crane; where the thing is under the defendant's management and such accidents do not ordinarily happen with proper care, it is reasonable evidence of negligence.

**Conditions**
1. The thing was under the defendant's **control**.
2. The accident is of a kind that **does not ordinarily happen without negligence**.
3. The **cause is unknown** to the plaintiff.

**Effect.** It shifts the **evidential burden** to the defendant; the legal burden normally stays (*Henderson v. Henry E. Jenkins*, 1970). It does not apply once the facts are known.

**India.** *Municipal Corporation of Delhi v. Subhagwanti* AIR 1966 SC 1750: a municipal clock tower collapsed and killed people; the doctrine applied. *Shyam Sunder v. State of Rajasthan* (1974) † accepted it. It is used in medical cases (*V. Kishan Rao*, 2010 †).

## B-33 · Explain contributory negligence and its effect on damages

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: B-31 (the horse problem). 20-mark form: C-14.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · development 4 · India and examples 3 · distinctions 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** The plaintiff failed to take reasonable care for his own safety, and this helped to cause his damage.

**Development**
- **Common law bar.** *Butterfield v. Forrester* (1809): the plaintiff rode violently at dusk and hit a pole the defendant had left across the road; no recovery.
- **Last opportunity.** *Davies v. Mann* (1842): a donkey left on the road with forefeet tied was run down; the defendant, who had the last chance to avoid it, was liable. *British Columbia Electric Rly v. Loach* (1916) and *Swadling v. Cooper* (1931) narrowed the rule.
- **Apportionment.** The Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act, 1945 (UK), s. 1: damages are reduced to the extent that is just and equitable, having regard to the claimant's share in the responsibility.

**Examples.** *Froom v. Butcher* [1976] QB 286: 25% reduction for not wearing a seat-belt. *Capps v. Miller* [1989]: 10% for an unfastened helmet. A child is judged by the standard of his age (*Gough v. Thorne*, 1966).

**India.** There is no general statute, but the courts apportion damages in motor-accident and other claims; it is not a complete defence.

**Distinctions.** Different from *volenti* (consent; a complete defence) and from composite negligence (several defendants).

## B-34 · In the light of the principle of negligence, discuss "nervous shock"

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q8 (this wording, 10 marks); Dec 2023 Q12 (C-15, 20 marks). 20-mark form: C-15.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · case line 5 · conditions 2 · India 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** A recognisable **psychiatric illness**, not mere grief or fright, caused by what a person sees or hears. The questions are duty, foreseeability and causation.

**Cases**
- *Victorian Railways v. Coultas* (1888): fright from a near-collision; no recovery.
- *Dulieu v. White* [1901] 2 KB 669: a pregnant barmaid, frightened by a horse-van crashing into the pub, became ill; recovery for fear for her **own** safety.
- *Hambrook v. Stokes Bros* [1925] 1 KB 141: a mother feared for her children; recovery for fear for **others**.
- *Bourhill v. Young* [1943] AC 92: a woman heard a distant collision; no duty to one outside the foreseeable area; **normal fortitude** is the test.
- *McLoughlin v. O'Brian* [1983] 1 AC 410: a mother saw her injured family in hospital two hours later; recovery, as she came on the **immediate aftermath**.
- *Alcock v. Chief Constable of South Yorkshire* [1992] 1 AC 310 (Hillsborough): a secondary victim needs a **close tie of love and affection**, **proximity** in time and space, perception by **own unaided senses**, and a **sudden shock**.
- *Page v. Smith* [1996]: a primary victim, within the zone of danger, needs only foreseeable personal injury.

**India.** English cases are persuasive; no leading Supreme Court decision †.

## B-35 · Explain medical negligence in civil and criminal law

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: Dec 2023 Q3 (A-115) on deficiency in service. 20-mark form: C-16.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** standard 3 · civil 3 · criminal 3 · consumer law 1

### Answer

**Standard.** A doctor is not negligent if he acts in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion (*Bolam v. Friern Hospital*, 1957), provided the opinion is logical (*Bolitho*, 1998). An error of judgment is not negligence. *Dr. Laxman Joshi v. Dr. Godbole* AIR 1969 SC 128: a doctor owes duties in deciding whether to take the case, what treatment to give, and in giving it.

**Civil.** *Samira Kohli* (2008): treatment without informed consent is a battery. *Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia* (1998): hospital liable for negligence to a child patient. *V. Kishan Rao* (2010) †: *res ipsa loquitur* may apply.

**Criminal.** *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1: criminal liability needs **gross** negligence or recklessness; a private complaint needs a credible opinion from another competent doctor. **BNS s. 106(1)**: up to five years, but up to **two years** for a registered medical practitioner in a medical procedure ‡.

**Consumer law.** Paid medical services are "service": *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha* (1995). The Supreme Court asked in 2024 for a larger bench to reconsider this ‡.

## B-36 · Explain liability for negligent misstatement and pure economic loss

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Related: C-21 (deceit). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** *Hedley Byrne* 5 · economic loss 3 · India and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Negligent misstatement.** At common law there was liability for physical harm from careless acts, but not for careless words causing economic loss. ***Hedley Byrne v. Heller & Partners*** [1964] AC 465: advertising agents asked about a customer's credit; the bank's reply was careless, and they lost money. The bank had disclaimed responsibility, so it was not liable, but the House of Lords held that a duty of care may arise for careless advice where there is a **special relationship** and the speaker **assumes responsibility** knowing that the other will rely on it. The duty needs reliance by the plaintiff and reasonableness of it.

**Pure economic loss.** Economic loss unconnected with physical damage is generally not recoverable. *Spartan Steel v. Martin & Co.* [1973] QB 27: a cable was cut by a contractor, stopping the plaintiff's factory; loss of the melt in progress (physical damage) was recoverable; profit lost during the stoppage was not. Recovery is allowed for statements under *Hedley Byrne*.

**Distinction from deceit.** Deceit needs knowledge or recklessness (*Derry v. Peek*); here carelessness suffices.

**India.** The courts accept the principle; professional negligence of advisers is also "deficiency in service" under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

*Word count: 194*
