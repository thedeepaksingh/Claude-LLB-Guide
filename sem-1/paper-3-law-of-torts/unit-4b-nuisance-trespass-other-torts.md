## 1. Background

1. Negligence protects against **careless** harm. Nuisance and trespass protect **interests in land** (and, for trespass, person and goods). The medieval writ of **trespass** covered direct interference with person, land and goods. **Trespass on the case** (the source of nuisance, negligence and deceit) covered indirect harm. That old line still explains the main difference: trespass is **direct** and actionable **without proof of damage**; nuisance and case torts are **indirect** and need **damage**.
2. These torts also carry criminal counterparts: **public nuisance** (BNS s. 270, formerly IPC s. 268 †), **criminal trespass** (BNS s. 329 †), **force, criminal force and assault** (BNS ss. 128–131, formerly IPC ss. 349–352 †), **wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement** (BNS ss. 126–127 †), **cheating** (BNS s. 318 †). The civil tort and the criminal offence may be pursued together.
3. In India the **constitutional dimension** is strong: false imprisonment by the police or the State is often decided under Arts. 21 and 22 and compensated as a public-law remedy (Unit 3).

## 2. Nuisance

### 2.1 Meaning
**Winfield:** "an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over, or in connection with it." It protects **use and enjoyment**, not just possession (that is trespass).

### 2.2 Kinds
| | Public nuisance | Private nuisance |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | An act or omission that materially affects the **reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of people** (BNS s. 270 †). *A-G v. PYA Quarries* [1957] 2 QB 169 (Romer L.J.): so widespread or indiscriminate in effect that it is not reasonable to expect one person to sue | Unlawful interference with a person's **use or enjoyment of his land** |
| Nature | A **crime**; also a tort for a person who suffers **special damage** | A **tort** only |
| Who sues | A-G on behalf of the public; an individual only if he shows **special (particular) damage** different in kind from the general public (*Rose v. Miles* (1815); *Benjamin v. Storr* (1874)). In India: CPC **s. 91** (suit by the Advocate-General, or by two or more persons with the court's leave) | The occupier (or owner) of the land affected (*Hunter v. Canary Wharf* [1997] AC 655) |
| Examples | Obstructing a highway; a dangerous quarry; selling food unfit for eating; a brothel; smoke from a factory affecting a neighbourhood | Smell, noise, smoke or vibration from a neighbour's land; overhanging branches |
| Remedy | Criminal prosecution; BNSS s. 152 (earlier CrPC s. 133) conditional order for removal of the nuisance †; civil suit for damages and injunction | Damages, injunction, abatement |

### 2.3 Essentials of private nuisance
1. **Unreasonable interference.** The question is whether it is unreasonable between neighbours: "give and take, live and let live" (*Bamford v. Turnley* (1862)).
2. **With the plaintiff's use or enjoyment of land** (or of a right such as an easement). There are three kinds: (a) **physical damage** to property (e.g. fumes killing trees, flooding); (b) **substantial interference with comfort and convenience** (noise, smell, vibration, smoke, light); (c) **interference with an easement** (e.g. a right to light).
3. **Damage** (actual and substantial). The court asks whether the plaintiff suffered more than the ordinary person must put up with.
4. **Standing.** Only a person with a **right in the land** can sue (*Hunter v. Canary Wharf*).

**Factors the court weighs**
| Factor | Case |
|---|---|
| **Locality** | *Sturges v. Bridgman* (1879) 11 Ch D 852 (a confectioner's noisy machinery disturbed a doctor's new consulting room; Thesiger L.J.: "what would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey"). For physical damage to property the locality does not matter (*St Helen's Smelting Co. v. Tipping* (1865) 11 HLC 642) |
| **Degree, duration and time** | *Halsey v. Esso Petroleum* [1961] 1 WLR 683 (night noise and smuts from an oil depot) |
| **Sensitivity of the plaintiff** | *Robinson v. Kilvert* (1889) 41 Ch D 88 (heat from a cellar harmed a neighbour's brown paper, which was unusually sensitive; no nuisance) |
| **Malice** | *Christie v. Davey* [1893] 1 Ch 316 (a music teacher's neighbour deliberately banged on the wall; nuisance); *Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v. Emmett* [1936] 2 KB 468 |
| **Utility of the defendant's act** | Not a defence: *Adams v. Ursell* [1913] 1 Ch 269 (a fried-fish shop in a good residential area) |
| **Responsibility for a nuisance caused by others** | *Sedleigh-Denfield v. O'Callaghan* [1940] AC 880 (an occupier who knows of and fails to stop a nuisance created by a trespasser is liable) |
| **Natural causes** | *Leakey v. National Trust* [1980] QB 485 (a measured duty) |

### 2.4 Indian cases
- ***Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand*** AIR 1980 SC 1622: residents of a ward suffered from open drains and the stench from an alcohol plant; the Magistrate ordered the municipality to build drains under CrPC s. 133. The Supreme Court (Krishna Iyer J.) upheld: a statutory body **cannot plead lack of funds** to avoid a public duty; the section is meant to protect the public.
- ***Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Assn.*** (2000) 7 SCC 282: noise from loudspeakers of a place of worship; no religion requires disturbing others by noise; Art. 25 is subject to public order and health.
- ***In re: Noise Pollution (V)*** (2005) 5 SCC 733 †: the right to sleep and to a quiet environment is a part of Art. 21; the Court restricted loudspeakers at night. The Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 fix noise limits by area and treat 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. as night †.
- ***M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*** (1997) 2 SCC 353 (Taj Trapezium) †: industrial pollution threatened the Taj Mahal; polluting industries were ordered to shift to natural gas or move.
- *Ram Lal v. Mustafabad Oil and Cotton Ginning Factory* AIR 1968 P&H 399 †: noise and dust from a ginning factory in a locality; the test of the **ordinary person's comfort** was applied.
- **Statutes:** Water Act, 1974; Air Act, 1981; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; NGT Act, 2010 (Unit 1).

### 2.5 Defences (Dec 2025 B8)
**Valid**
1. **Prescription.** A right to commit a nuisance can be acquired by **20 years'** uninterrupted use as of right (Indian Easements Act, 1882, s. 15; *Sturges v. Bridgman*). The 20 years run from when the plaintiff can first sue.
2. **Statutory authority** (*Allen v. Gulf Oil*; *Hammersmith Rly v. Brand*) (Unit 2).
3. **Consent** of the plaintiff (*volenti*).
4. **Act of a stranger / Act of God**, limited to what could not be prevented.
5. **Reasonable user / locality:** use of the land that is reasonable in the neighbourhood is not a nuisance.
6. **No actionable damage / plaintiff's abnormal sensitivity** (*Robinson v. Kilvert*).

**Not defences**
1. **Coming to the nuisance** (*Bliss v. Hall* (1838); *Miller v. Jackson* [1977] QB 966 (a cricket club); *Coventry v. Lawrence* [2014] UKSC 13).
2. **Public benefit or utility** (*Adams v. Ursell*); this may, however, affect the remedy (damages instead of an injunction).
3. **That the defendant took reasonable care**, as nuisance concerns the result.
4. **That others contribute** to the nuisance (*Lambton v. Mellish* [1894] 3 Ch 163: two noisy roundabouts; each liable).
5. **That the plaintiff came later** or that the nuisance existed before the plaintiff came.
6. **Planning permission** (not a defence by itself, though it may change the character of the locality: *Coventry v. Lawrence*).

### 2.6 Remedies
**Damages**; **injunction** (prohibitory or mandatory; the court may give damages instead where an injunction is oppressive, *Shelfer v. City of London Electric Lighting* [1895] 1 Ch 287, now applied flexibly in *Coventry v. Lawrence*); **abatement** (self-help: the occupier may remove the nuisance after notice, unless there is an emergency; no more than necessary: *Lemmon v. Webb* [1895] AC 1).

### 2.7 Nuisance v. Rylands v. Fletcher v. negligence v. trespass

| | Nuisance | *Rylands v. Fletcher* | Negligence | Trespass to land |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Interest | Use and enjoyment of land | Land and property | Person, property | Possession of land |
| Fault | Not necessary | Not necessary | Necessary | Not necessary |
| Damage | Needed | Needed | Needed | Not needed |
| Continuity | Usually continuing | Single escape | Single or continuing | Entry, direct |
| Nature | Indirect interference | Escape of dangerous thing | Breach of duty of care | Direct interference |

## 3. Trespass to the person

### 3.1 Assault and battery
- **Assault:** an act by which the defendant **intentionally or recklessly** makes the plaintiff reasonably **apprehend immediate unlawful force**. Words alone may be enough (*R v. Ireland* [1998] AC 147: silent telephone calls), but words may negate an apparent threat (*Tuberville v. Savage* (1669): "If it were not assize-time, I would not take such language from you"). *Stephens v. Myers* (1830): the defendant advanced with a clenched fist towards the chairman of a meeting and was stopped by a bystander; it was an assault because he had the present means to carry out the threat.
- **Battery:** the **direct and intentional application of force** to another person without lawful justification. Slightest touching is enough if without consent (*Cole v. Turner* (1704): "the least touching of another in anger is a battery"; but *Wilson v. Pringle* [1987] QB 237 asked for a "hostile" touching, a requirement Lord Goff doubted in *F v. West Berkshire HA* [1990] 2 AC 1, which preferred the test of contact beyond what is generally acceptable in ordinary life). *Fagan v. Metropolitan Police Commissioner* [1969] 1 QB 439 (a driver stopped his car on a constable's foot and kept it there; a continuing act).
- Both are actionable *per se*. Defences: consent, self-defence, lawful authority, necessity, parental discipline. **Medical treatment** without consent is a battery (*Samira Kohli*).
- **Criminal law:** force, criminal force and assault (BNS ss. 128–131, formerly IPC ss. 349–352 †).

### 3.2 False imprisonment (Dec 2022 A3)
**Meaning.** **Total restraint of a person's liberty, without lawful justification** (e.g. keeping him in a room, in a vehicle, in a prison beyond his term).

**Ingredients**
1. **Total restraint:** complete deprivation of liberty of movement. Partial obstruction is not false imprisonment if there is another way out (*Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742: part of Hammersmith Bridge was closed and the plaintiff was prevented from going the way he wished; he could go back).
2. **Without lawful authority or justification:** an arrest without a warrant in a case where the law does not allow it, or beyond the time permitted.
3. **Intention** (the act of restraint must be voluntary; negligence is not enough in general).
4. **Knowledge:** the plaintiff need not know (*Meering v. Grahame-White Aviation* (1919) 122 LT 44: a prisoner who did not know of the restraint was imprisoned; *Murray v. Ministry of Defence* [1988] 1 WLR 692).
5. **The means of restraint:** physical barriers, force, or assertion of authority which the plaintiff submits to. *Herd v. Weardale Steel* [1915] AC 67: a miner who refused to do work he thought unsafe, and was not at once taken up the pit shaft, was not falsely imprisoned, because the employer was not bound by the contract to carry him up at once. *Robinson v. Balmain New Ferry* (1910) AC 295: a condition that an exit fee be paid is reasonable and no false imprisonment.

**Remedies.** Damages (even nominal), **habeas corpus** (Art. 32/226; in India an order for release plus compensation), and criminal prosecution for wrongful confinement. **Limitation:** one year from when the imprisonment ends (Art. 73 ‡).

**Indian cases.** *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 (14 years' illegal detention after acquittal: ₹30,000); *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677 (MLA); *Sebastian M. Hongray v. Union of India* (1984) 3 SCC 82 † (missing persons, exemplary costs); *Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P.* (1994) 4 SCC 260 (arrest is not routine); *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416 (guidelines); *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) 8 SCC 273.

### 3.3 Malicious prosecution (not named in the syllabus but a standard head)
**Meaning.** Prosecution of the plaintiff **maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause**, which ends in his favour and causes damage.

**Essentials**
1. The defendant **prosecuted** the plaintiff (set the law in motion): a criminal proceeding (and in some cases civil proceedings that cause special damage).
2. The proceedings **ended in the plaintiff's favour** (acquittal, discharge, withdrawal).
3. **No reasonable and probable cause:** no honest belief in the guilt based on reasonable grounds (*Hicks v. Faulkner* (1878) 8 QBD 167).
4. **Malice:** an improper or wrongful motive, e.g. revenge or to extort money, other than bringing a guilty person to justice (*Glinski v. McIver* [1962] AC 726).
5. **Damage** to reputation, person (liberty) or property (*Savile v. Roberts* (1698) lists the three kinds of damage; *Berry v. British Transport Commission* [1962] 1 QB 306 †).

**Distinguish from false imprisonment:** in false imprisonment the **arrest** itself is unlawful and the **defendant** need not act with malice; in malicious prosecution the arrest or process may be lawful (on a magistrate's order), but the **prosecution** is malicious. The first is actionable *per se*; the second needs damage. *Mohammad Amin v. Jogendra Kumar Bannerjee* AIR 1947 PC 108 † is an Indian authority on what counts as prosecution. **Limitation:** one year from the date of acquittal or other termination (Art. 74 ‡).

**Abuse of process** (*Grainger v. Hill* (1838)): using legal process for an improper purpose, even where it was properly started.

## 4. Trespass to land

### 4.1 Meaning and essentials
**Trespass to land** is the **direct, unjustified interference with the possession of land** of another: entering, remaining on it, throwing or placing an object on it. Actionable *per se*.

1. **Possession** by the plaintiff (not ownership). A tenant may sue the landlord.
2. **Direct interference**, by entry, remaining, or placing a thing on the land (*Holmes v. Wilson* (1839): supports built to prop up a road that was too close; continuing trespass).
3. **Intentional or negligent** (a mistaken belief that the land is one's own is no defence; *Basely v. Clarkson* (1681): the defendant mowed the plaintiff's grass by mistake and was liable).
4. **Without consent or lawful justification.**
- *Entick v. Carrington* (1765) 19 St Tr 1029: officers of the King's Messenger entered the plaintiff's home on a general warrant; they were held liable for trespass. This is the source of the rule that officials need **lawful authority** for any interference.
- **Air-space and sub-soil.** Ownership extends to a reasonable height and depth; *Bernstein v. Skyviews & General* [1978] QB 479 (an aircraft flying high above the land is no trespass).

### 4.2 Defences
Licence (consent, including implied); justification by law (arrest, search warrant, distress); necessity; defence of person or property; right of way or an easement; entry by a public officer.

### 4.3 Trespass *ab initio* (Dec 2025 A2)
**Meaning.** A person who enters land **lawfully** (by authority of the law, not of the owner) but then commits a **positive wrongful act** (an abuse of that authority) is treated as a trespasser **from the very beginning**, not only from the time of the abuse.
**Source.** ***The Six Carpenters' Case*** (1610) 8 Co Rep 146a: the six carpenters entered an inn and ordered wine, as the law allowed them as customers. They then refused to pay. They were held **not** trespassers from the beginning, since *refusing to pay* was a mere omission (non-feasance). The Court said, however, that if they had done a **positive act** (misfeasance) after lawful entry, such as taking away the wine, the law would treat the entry as unlawful from the start.
**Limits.** (i) It applies only to entry **by authority of the law** (e.g. a distrainor, a sheriff, a customs officer), **not** to entry by the owner's licence. (ii) The later act must be a **positive** abuse, not an omission. (iii) The doctrine is **criticised and rarely used** today (*Elias v. Pasmore* [1934] 2 KB 164 limited it; *Cinnamond v. British Airports Authority* [1980] 1 WLR 582 denied its application to licensees) †.

### 4.4 Remedies
Damages; injunction; ejectment and recovery of possession (Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 5–6: a dispossessed person may sue within **six months** under s. 6 without proving title); the right to use reasonable force to eject a trespasser (but not unreasonable force: *Bird v. Holbrook*).

## 5. Trespass to goods and conversion

| Wrong | Meaning | Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Trespass to goods** | Direct interference with goods in the plaintiff's **possession**: taking, moving, damaging | *Kirk v. Gregory* (1876) 1 Ex D 55 (moving a dead person's jewellery from one room to another, where it was stolen) † |
| **Conversion** | A dealing with another's goods **inconsistent with his rights** (selling, destroying, refusing to return, using as one's own) so as to deny his title | *Hollins v. Fowler* (1875) LR 7 HL 757 (an innocent broker who sold cotton for a thief was liable); *Fouldes v. Willoughby* (1841) (removing a horse from a ferry was not conversion as it did not deny the owner's title) |
| **Detinue** | Wrongful refusal to give up goods after demand | In India the owner sues for recovery of specific movables (Specific Relief Act, s. 7) |

## 6. Deceit (fraud) (Dec 2022 C11)

### 6.1 Meaning
**Deceit** is a **false statement made knowingly, or without belief in its truth, or recklessly**, with the intention that the plaintiff should act on it, which the plaintiff does, and **suffers damage**.

### 6.2 *Derry v. Peek* (1889) 14 App Cas 337
**Facts.** Directors of a tramway company issued a prospectus stating that they had the right to use **steam power** instead of horses. They believed Parliament's special Act gave the right, subject to the Board of Trade's consent, and thought the consent was a formality. The Board later refused. The company failed, and Peek, who bought shares on the strength of the prospectus, sued the directors for deceit.
**Held (House of Lords; Lord Herschell).** Fraud is proved when it is shown that a false representation has been made (i) **knowingly**, (ii) **without belief in its truth**, or (iii) **recklessly, careless whether it be true or false**. An **honest belief**, even if unreasonable, negates deceit. The directors honestly believed; no deceit. (Parliament later enacted the Directors' Liability Act, 1890, to cover prospectuses.)

### 6.3 Essentials
1. **A false representation of fact** (not opinion, promise or law: *Bisset v. Wilkinson* [1927] AC 177). A statement of present intention is a statement of fact about state of mind (*Edgington v. Fitzmaurice* (1885) 29 Ch D 459: directors said that loan money would be used for development of the company, but meant to pay debts). It may be by words or conduct. **Silence** is not a misrepresentation unless there is a duty to speak, or the half-truth misleads (*Peek v. Gurney* (1873)).
2. **Knowledge of the falsity** or recklessness (*Derry v. Peek*).
3. **Intention that the plaintiff should act on it** (and he does).
4. **The plaintiff acted on it** (reliance); it need not be the sole inducement (*Edgington*).
5. **Damage** (actual loss).

### 6.4 Remedy and Indian law
**Damages** for all loss that flows directly from the fraud, whether foreseeable or not (*Doyle v. Olby (Ironmongers)* [1969] 2 QB 158; *Smith New Court Securities v. Scrimgeour Vickers* [1997] AC 254). Under the **Indian Contract Act, 1872**, s. 17 defines fraud; s. 19 makes a contract induced by fraud voidable at the option of the party deceived, who may also claim damages; s. 19A for undue influence. Cheating is an offence (BNS s. 318, formerly IPC s. 415 †). Where the false statement was made **honestly but carelessly**, the claim is for **negligent misstatement** (*Hedley Byrne*, Unit 4A), not deceit.

**Deceit v. negligent misrepresentation v. innocent misrepresentation**: deceit needs knowledge or recklessness; negligent misstatement needs a duty and carelessness; innocent misrepresentation gives rescission (Contract Act s. 19) and no damages in tort.

## 8. Comparison tables and quick-revision box

**False imprisonment v. malicious prosecution**

| Point | False imprisonment | Malicious prosecution |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong | Unlawful total restraint | Malicious institution of criminal proceedings |
| Nature | Trespass; direct | Action on the case; indirect |
| Proof of damage | Not needed | Needed |
| Malice | Not needed | Needed (with absence of reasonable cause) |
| Termination in plaintiff's favour | Not needed | Needed |
| Limitation | One year from end of imprisonment | One year from termination |

**Quick-revision box**
- Nuisance = unreasonable interference with use and enjoyment of land; public (special damage; CPC s. 91) v. private; defences: prescription (20 years), statutory authority, consent; **not** coming to the nuisance, utility.
- Cases: *St Helen's* (property damage), *Sturges* (locality), *Robinson v. Kilvert* (sensitivity), *Christie v. Davey* (malice), *Hunter* (standing), *Ratlam* (statutory duty).
- Trespass is direct and actionable *per se*; false imprisonment = total restraint without lawful authority (*Bird v. Jones*); the plaintiff need not know (*Meering*).
- Trespass *ab initio*: *Six Carpenters' Case*: lawful entry plus positive abuse; not for omissions or entry by licence.
- Deceit: *Derry v. Peek*: knowledge, no belief, or recklessness; five elements.

## 10. Current developments (to November 2026)

1. **BNSS and BNS.** The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 replaced the CrPC from 1 July 2024. The public-nuisance order is now under BNSS s. 152 and arrest provisions under BNSS ss. 35–36 † (check the sections in your copy).
2. **Noise and pollution.** Courts continue to order action against loudspeaker noise and to cite Art. 21; the Taj Trapezium and Delhi air-quality orders show the High Courts and the Supreme Court using nuisance ideas in environmental cases.
3. **England.** *Coventry v. Lawrence* (2014) changed how injunctions are decided, and *Fearn v. Tate Gallery* [2023] UKSC 4 held (by 3 to 2) that constant visual intrusion from a viewing platform can be a nuisance † (verify).
4. **Compensation for illegal detention** remains a public-law remedy, with the amounts rising over the years; there is no fixed scale.
