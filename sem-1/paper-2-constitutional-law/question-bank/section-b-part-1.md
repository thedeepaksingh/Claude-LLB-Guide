## B-01 · Federalism: is the Indian Constitution federal?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q6 ("What do you mean by Federalism? Is Indian Constitution federal? Discuss."). Related: Dec 2024 Q2, Dec 2023 Q3, Dec 2022 Q2 (A). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · federal features 3 · unitary features 3 · cases and conclusion 2

### Answer

Federalism divides powers between a central and regional governments, each independent in its sphere, under a written constitution interpreted by an independent judiciary (Wheare). The word "federal" is not used; Art. 1 calls India a "Union of States", which Ambedkar explained as indestructible, with no right of secession.

**Federal features:** written, supreme Constitution; dual polity; three Lists in the Seventh Schedule (Art. 246); independent judiciary deciding Union–State disputes (Art. 131); amendment of federal provisions needs ratification by half the States (Art. 368(2) proviso); Council of States.

**Unitary features:** Parliament may change the boundaries and names of States by ordinary law (Art. 3); residuary power with the Union (Art. 248); Union law prevails in the Concurrent List (Art. 254); Emergency and Art. 356; Governor appointed by the President; All-India Services; single citizenship; integrated judiciary.

**Cases:** *State of W.B. v. Union of India* (1963): not a federation in the strict sense. *Kesavananda* (1973) and *S.R. Bommai* (1994): federalism is part of the basic structure. *GNCTD v. Union of India* (2018, 2023): federalism is cooperative.

**Conclusion:** the Constitution is federal in structure with a strong unitary bias (quasi-federal), and federalism is an enforceable basic feature.

## B-02 · "Neither federal nor unitary but quasi-federal": do you agree?

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q6 ("Indian Constitution neither federal nor unitary but it is quasi-federal in nature. Do you agree? Discuss.")
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** thesis 1 · why not purely federal 3 · why not purely unitary 3 · cases and verdict 3

### Answer

I largely agree, with one qualification.

**Not purely federal.** K.C. Wheare called India quasi-federal: a unitary State with subsidiary federal features. Parliament can redraw States by ordinary law (Art. 3), so India is "an indestructible Union of destructible States" (*Babulal Parate*, 1960). The Union has residuary power (Art. 248) and overriding powers in the national interest (Arts. 249–253). The Governor is a Union appointee, Art. 356 can displace a State government, and States depend on Union grants (Art. 275).

**Not purely unitary.** The Constitution is written and supreme. Legislative, executive and financial powers are divided by the Seventh Schedule. States have their own elected governments. The Court decides Union–State disputes (Art. 131). Federal provisions need ratification by half the States (Art. 368(2)).

**The Court.** *State of W.B. v. Union of India* (1963): not a federation in the strict sense. *S.R. Bommai* (1994): federalism is a basic feature and States are not mere appendages of the Centre. *GNCTD* (2018, 2023): cooperative federalism.

**Qualification.** "Quasi-federal" describes the design, but since *Bommai* federalism is also a binding limit on the Centre.

## B-03 · "The Preamble gives direction and purpose to the Constitution"

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q6 ("Preamble gives direction and purpose to the Constitution. Describe."). Related: Dec 2023 Q5, Dec 2024 Q1, Dec 2025 Q1 (A). 20-mark form: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** content of Preamble 3 · how it gives direction 4 · limits and cases 3

### Answer

The Preamble, based on Nehru's Objectives Resolution (1946), says who made the Constitution ("We, the People of India"), what kind of State India is (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic), and what it aims to secure: **Justice** (social, economic, political), **Liberty** (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), **Equality** (status and opportunity) and **Fraternity** (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation). It gives the date of adoption, 26 November 1949.

**Direction.** The objectives are carried out by the provisions: Justice by Parts III and IV, Liberty by Arts. 19 and 25, Equality by Arts. 14–18, Fraternity by Arts. 17, 51A(e). Courts use it to resolve ambiguity. In *Kesavananda* (1973) the Preamble was held to be a part of the Constitution, and basic features such as democracy, secularism and the republican form were drawn from it. *LIC v. CERC* (1995) called it an integral part.

**Limits.** It is not enforceable and cannot override an express provision (*Berubari*, 1960). It was amended once, in 1976, to add "Socialist", "Secular" and "integrity"; *Balram Singh* (2024) confirmed that Art. 368 reaches it.

**Conclusion:** the Preamble gives purpose, and the articles give effect to it.

## B-04 · The doctrine of double jeopardy

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q8 ("What is the doctrine of double jeopardy? Explain."); Dec 2025 Q13(b) (short note). Fuller treatment: C-12.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning and source 2 · elements 3 · cases 3 · statutory rule and limits 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** Art. 20(2): "No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once." The doctrine comes from the common-law pleas of *autrefois acquit* and *autrefois convict* and the Fifth Amendment of the US Constitution.

**Elements.** The person must have been (i) **prosecuted** before a court or judicial tribunal, (ii) **punished**, (iii) for the **same offence**, and (iv) again prosecuted and punished.

**Cases.**
- *Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay* (1953): confiscation of gold by customs authorities was not a prosecution before a judicial tribunal, so a later trial was allowed.
- *S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India* (1954): a departmental inquiry is not a prosecution.
- *State of Bombay v. S.L. Apte* (1961): the bar applies only if the ingredients of the two offences are the same.
- *Sangeetaben Patel v. State of Gujarat* (2012): prosecutions under s. 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act and for cheating can both proceed; the ingredients differ.

**Statute.** CrPC s. 300, now **BNSS s. 337**, also bars a second trial after **acquittal**; s. 26 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 bars punishment twice.

**Conclusion.** Art. 20(2) is narrower than the American rule; it cannot be suspended in an Emergency.

## B-05 · Protection against ex post facto laws

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q6 ("What do you understand by protection against ex-post-facto law? Explain."); Dec 2025 Q4 (A). Fuller treatment: C-11.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text and meaning 3 · two limbs 2 · scope and exceptions with cases 3 · example and conclusion 2

### Answer

An *ex post facto* law punishes an act that was lawful when done, or increases its punishment. Article 20(1): "No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence."

**Two limbs:** (i) no conviction for an act not an offence when done; (ii) no heavier penalty than the law then allowed.

**Scope.**
1. Criminal laws only; civil and tax laws may be retrospective.
2. Available to any person; not suspended in an Emergency.
3. Procedure may change: no vested right in a mode of trial (*Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh*, 1953).
4. A retrospective law beneficial to the accused is valid (*Ratan Lal v. State of Punjab*, 1965).

**Illustration.** The BNS took effect on 1 July 2024; earlier acts are tried under the IPC.

**Conclusion:** Art. 20(1) secures certainty of the criminal law.

*Word count: 179*

## B-06 · Extended interpretation of Article 21

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q8 ("Write a note on extended interpretation of Article 21"). 20-mark forms: C-13, C-14, C-15.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** early law 2 · *Maneka Gandhi* 3 · heads of rights 4 · limit and conclusion 1

### Answer

Article 21 says: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law."

**Narrow beginning.** *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) read "procedure established by law" as any procedure in a validly enacted law, and held Art. 21 separate from Art. 19.

**Turning point.** *Maneka Gandhi* (1978): Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are interconnected; the procedure must be **fair, just and reasonable**; natural justice is included; "personal liberty" has the widest amplitude.

**Rights read into Art. 21.**
- Dignity: *Francis Coralie Mullin* (1981).
- Speedy trial and legal aid: *Hussainara Khatoon* (1980).
- Freedom from torture: *Sunil Batra* (1978); *D.K. Basu* (1997).
- Livelihood: *Olga Tellis* (1985).
- Health: *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity* (1996).
- Environment: *Subhash Kumar* (1991); *M.C. Mehta*.
- Privacy: *Puttaswamy* (2017).
- Education: *Unni Krishnan* (1993), then Art. 21A.
- Compensation: *Rudul Sah* (1983).
- Dignified death: *Common Cause* (2018).

**Limit.** A valid law with fair procedure can deprive life and liberty. *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) was overruled in *Puttaswamy*.

**Conclusion.** Art. 21 now guarantees the conditions of a dignified life.

*Word count: 170*

## B-07 · Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q7 ("Discuss the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy"); Dec 2024 Q8 (same, "Refer to decided cases"); Dec 2025 Q12 (20 marks: C-23).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** nature of each 2 · evolution with cases 6 · present position 2

### Answer

Part III rights are justiciable and secure political democracy; Part IV directives are non-enforceable (Art. 37) but "fundamental in the governance of the country" and aim at social and economic democracy.

**Evolution.**
- *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951): Directive Principles must run as subsidiary to Fundamental Rights.
- *In re Kerala Education Bill* (1958): the Court should harmonise the two.
- *Golaknath* (1967): rights are transcendental.
- 25th Amendment (1971) inserted **Art. 31C**: laws for Art. 39(b) and (c) are immune from Arts. 14 and 19.
- *Kesavananda* (1973): first part of Art. 31C upheld; the bar on judicial review struck down; Parts III and IV are complementary.
- 42nd Amendment (1976) extended Art. 31C to all directives.
- *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck down the extension: the Constitution rests on "the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV", which is basic structure.
- *Property Owners Association* (2024): not all private resources are "material resources of the community" under Art. 39(b).

**Use of directives.** To interpret and widen rights: equal pay (*Randhir Singh*, 1982), legal aid (Art. 39A), livelihood (*Olga Tellis*).

**Present position:** rights prevail in conflict, subject to Art. 31C as upheld; the Court harmonises both.

*Word count: 192*

## B-08 · Fundamental Duties

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q7 ("Write a short note on 'fundamental duties'"); Dec 2023 Q4, Dec 2024 Q5, Dec 2025 Q2 (A). 20-mark form: C-24.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** origin 2 · list 3 · nature and use 3 · criticism and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Origin.** The Swaran Singh Committee (1976) recommended duties; the **42nd Amendment (1976)** inserted Part IVA, Art. 51A, with ten duties. The **86th Amendment (2002)** added clause (k): eleven.

**The duties** (of every citizen): (a) abide by the Constitution, respect the Flag and Anthem; (b) cherish the ideals of the freedom struggle; (c) uphold sovereignty, unity and integrity; (d) defend the country; (e) promote harmony and renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women; (f) preserve the composite culture; (g) protect the environment and have compassion for living creatures; (h) develop scientific temper; (i) safeguard public property and abjure violence; (j) strive for excellence; (k) parents to provide education to children aged 6–14.

**Nature.** Citizens only; non-justiciable; no sanction in the text. Parliament can enforce them by law (for example, the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 and the Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984).

**Judicial use.** *Rural Litigation Kendra* (1985) and *M.C. Mehta* (environment); *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986) (no compulsion to sing); *AIIMS Students' Union* (2002) (a guide to interpretation).

**Criticism.** No sanction; vague terms; introduced during the Emergency.

**Conclusion.** The duties are the civic counterpart of rights.

*Word count: 191*

## B-09 · Reasonableness and non-arbitrariness as guides to Article 14

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q7 ("Doctrine of reasonableness and doctrine of non-arbitrariness are the guiding principles of equality clause. Discuss with the help of decided cases."). 20-mark form: C-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 14 text 1 · reasonable classification with cases 4 · non-arbitrariness with cases 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

Art. 14: "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws."

**Reasonableness (classification).** Art. 14 forbids class legislation but permits reasonable classification with (i) an **intelligible differentia** and (ii) a **rational nexus** to the object (*Ram Krishna Dalmia*, 1958). *Anwar Ali Sarkar* (1952) struck down a law that gave no guidance in choosing cases for special courts; *D.S. Nakara* (1983) struck down a pension classification by date of retirement.

**Non-arbitrariness.** *E.P. Royappa* (1974): equality is a dynamic concept; "equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies". *Maneka Gandhi* (1978): State action must be fair, just and reasonable. *R.D. Shetty* (1979) and *Ajay Hasia* (1981): the State's contracts and admissions must be non-arbitrary. *Shrilekha Vidyarthi* (1991): mass termination of government counsel was arbitrary.

**Legislation.** *McDowell* (1996) cautioned against striking down a law merely as arbitrary; *Shayara Bano* (2017) applied "manifest arbitrariness" to strike down instant triple talaq.

**Conclusion.** The two doctrines are complementary: classification tests the law, and non-arbitrariness tests the quality of State action.

*Word count: 172*

## B-10 · Cultural and educational rights of minorities: a critical note

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q7 ("Examine the Cultural and Educational Rights of Minorities as prescribed under Articles 29 and 30. Refer to decided cases."); Dec 2025 Q8 ("Write a critical note on Cultural and Educational Rights of Minorities…"). 20-mark form: C-20.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 29 2 · Art. 30 with cases 4 · recent 1 · criticism 3

### Answer

**Art. 29(1):** any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture may conserve it (*D.A.V. College*, 1971). **Art. 29(2):** no denial of admission to State-maintained or aided institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language (*Champakam*, 1951).

**Art. 30(1):** minorities, religious or linguistic, may establish and administer educational institutions of their choice; **30(1A)** protects against inadequate compensation on acquisition; **30(2)** bars discrimination in aid.

**Cases.** *Kerala Education Bill* (1958): regulation allowed, destruction of minority character not. *St. Xavier's College* (1974): right to administer is not a right to maladminister. *T.M.A. Pai* (2002, eleven judges): the **State is the unit**; unaided institutions have autonomy but no capitation fees. *Inamdar* (2005): no State quota in unaided institutions. *Pramati* (2014): the RTE Act and Art. 15(5) do not apply to minority institutions. *AMU* (8 Nov 2024, 4:3): *Azeez Basha* overruled; statutory incorporation does not bar minority status.

**Criticism.** "Minority" is undefined; State-as-unit creates anomalies; exemption from reservation and RTE leaves students without safeguards (the *Pramati* position was doubted in a September 2025 reference); status claims are used to avoid regulation.

**Conclusion.** Autonomy within the law, balanced against equality.

*Word count: 189*

## B-11 · The parliamentary form of government in India

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q3 ("Parliamentary form of Government", A, 4 marks). Part of the syllabus line "Form of Government" (C-01).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** features with Articles 5 · cases 2 · comparison 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

India follows the Westminster model at the Union and State levels.

**Features.**
1. **Nominal head:** the President (Arts. 52–53); the Governor in States (Art. 153).
2. **Real executive:** the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, which aids and advises the President (Art. 74); the President may ask for reconsideration once and is then bound (44th Amendment).
3. **Collective responsibility** to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)).
4. **Ministers are members of the legislature** (Art. 75(5): a non-member must be elected within six months).
5. **Lok Sabha may be dissolved**; the Government falls if it loses confidence.
6. **Bicameral Parliament** (Art. 79).
7. **Republic:** the head is elected, not hereditary (Arts. 54–55).

**Cases.** *Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955): the President is a constitutional head like the Crown. *Shamsher Singh* (1974): the President and Governors act on ministerial advice except where discretion is given by the Constitution.

**Compared with the presidential system.** Executive and legislature are fused, not separated; ministers answer collectively; separation of powers is functional, not rigid (basic structure: *Kesavananda*; *Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain*).

**Conclusion.** The system gives stable, responsible government.

## B-12 · Rule of law

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q1 ("Rule of law", A, 4 marks). Linked to Art. 14 (C-06).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Dicey 3 · Indian position with cases 4 · exceptions 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Dicey's meaning.** A.V. Dicey gave three ideas: (i) supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power; (ii) equality before the law, with all classes subject to the ordinary courts; (iii) the Constitution as the result of ordinary judicial decisions on individual rights.

**In India.** The Constitution adopts the first two ideas; the third differs because rights are written (Part III). The rule of law is part of the basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973; *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975).

**Provisions.** Art. 14 (equality before law); Art. 21 (procedure must be fair: *Maneka Gandhi*, 1978); Arts. 32 and 226 (remedies); Art. 13 (laws inconsistent with rights are void).

**Cases.** *Maneka Gandhi*: arbitrariness is opposed to the rule of law. *Som Raj v. State of Haryana* (1990): absence of arbitrary power is the first postulate of the rule of law. *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) was a lapse, overruled in *Puttaswamy* (2017).

**Exceptions.** The President and Governors are not answerable to any court for official acts (Art. 361); parliamentary privileges (Arts. 105, 194); foreign sovereigns and diplomats; Art. 31C.

**Conclusion.** The rule of law, rather than the will of any person, governs the State.

## B-13 · Article 356 and the *Bommai* guidelines

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; a recurring question under "Federal Structure" (C-01).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Article 356 3 · history 1 · *Bommai* holdings 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Art. 356.** If the President is satisfied, on a Governor's report or otherwise, that the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, he may assume the State's functions (President's Rule). Parliament must approve within two months (Art. 356(3)). The proclamation lasts six months and may be extended, but beyond one year only if an Emergency is in force and the Election Commission certifies that elections cannot be held (44th Amendment).

**History.** Often used against opposition-ruled States; the Sarkaria Commission (1988) said it should be a last resort.

***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994, nine judges).**
1. A proclamation is **justiciable**: the Court asks whether there was relevant material and whether the power was misused.
2. The Assembly may not be dissolved before Parliament approves.
3. A ministry's majority is tested **on the floor of the House**, not by the Governor's opinion.
4. The Court may restore a dismissed government.
5. **Secularism** and **federalism** are basic features.

*Rameshwar Prasad* (2006): the 2005 dissolution of the Bihar Assembly was unconstitutional.

**Conclusion.** *Bommai* made Art. 356 an exceptional power under judicial control.

## B-14 · Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?

**Asked as:** not asked as a separate question; part of every Preamble answer (C-02, B-03).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** *Berubari* 3 · *Kesavananda* 3 · later cases 2 · position and amendability 2

### Answer

***Berubari Union, In re* (1960).** The Supreme Court held that the Preamble is **not a part** of the Constitution. It is a key to the framers' minds and may resolve ambiguity, but it is not a source of power and cannot override an express provision.

***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973).** The thirteen-judge bench rejected that view. The Preamble is **a part** of the Constitution: it was adopted after the main provisions, it contains the basic features, and the Constitution is read in its light.

**Later cases.** *S.R. Bommai* (1994): secularism, a Preamble value, is a basic feature. *LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995): the Preamble is an integral part.

**Amendability.** The 42nd Amendment (1976) added "Socialist", "Secular" and "integrity". In *Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024) the Court held that Art. 368 extends to the Preamble and the date 26 November 1949 is no bar; the challenge was dismissed.

**Position.** The Preamble is a part of the Constitution, aids interpretation, is not directly enforceable, and may be amended without altering the basic structure.

*Word count: 182*

## B-15 · Secularism in the Indian Constitution

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q4 and Dec 2023 Q2 (related: "Secular state", "Socialist State", A, 4 marks). Fuller treatment: Unit 10 and C-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · constitutional basis 3 · cases 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** The State has no religion of its own and treats all religions equally (*sarva dharma sama bhava*). Indian secularism is "positive": the State keeps a principled distance, may regulate secular activity linked to religion and may reform religion (Art. 25(2)). The US keeps a strict wall of separation; France follows *laïcité*.

**Basis.** The word "secular" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but the idea is in Arts. 14–16 (no discrimination), 25–28 (freedom of religion; Art. 27, no tax for a religion; Art. 28, religious instruction), 29–30 (minorities) and 44.

**Cases.** *S.R. Bommai* (1994): secularism is a basic feature; a State government that acts against it can be dismissed under Art. 356. *Ismail Faruqui* (1994): a mosque is not essential to Islam, so a place of worship may be acquired by law. *Aruna Roy* (2002): teaching about religions is not religious instruction. *Abhiram Singh* (2017): an appeal to religion to seek votes is a corrupt practice. *Shayara Bano* (2017) and *Sabarimala* (2018): religious practice is subject to equality.

**Conclusion.** Secularism is a basic feature, meaning equal respect for all faiths and no State religion.

*Word count: 188*

## B-16 · The meaning of "State" in Article 12

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q13 and Dec 2025 Q3 (A) touch on it ("Against whom FRs are available"). Fuller treatment: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 2 · "other authorities" tests with cases 5 · judiciary and private bodies 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

Article 12 defines "the State" for Part III: the Government and Parliament of India; the Government and Legislature of each State; and all **local or other authorities** within India or under the control of the Government of India.

**Local authorities:** municipalities, panchayats, port trusts.

**Other authorities.** A narrow *ejusdem generis* reading (*University of Madras v. Santa Bai*, Madras High Court, 1954) was rejected in *Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal* (1967): any authority with power to make rules having the force of law. *Sukhdev Singh* (1975): statutory corporations such as ONGC and LIC. *R.D. Shetty* (1979) and *Ajay Hasia* (1981): the **instrumentality test**, which looks at State-held share capital, government finance meeting almost all expenses, monopoly status, deep and pervasive control, and public functions. *Pradeep Kumar Biswas* (2002, seven judges): is the body financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of the Government?

**Judiciary.** Its administrative acts are State action; a judicial order is not "law" (*Naresh Mirajkar*, 1967).

**Private bodies.** Not "State" (BCCI: *Zee Telefilms*, 2005), though Art. 226 may reach a public function.

**Conclusion.** "State" has a wide, functional meaning.

*Word count: 184*
