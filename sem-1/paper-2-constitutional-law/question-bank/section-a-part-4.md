<a id="a-140"></a>
**A-140 · Article 13: laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights**

Art. 13(1): pre-Constitution laws are void to the extent of inconsistency with Part III. Art. 13(2): the State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges those rights; such a law is void to that extent. "Law" includes ordinances, orders, rules, notifications, custom or usage having the force of law (Art. 13(3)). Art. 13(4), added by the 24th Amendment, excludes amendments under Art. 368, subject to the basic structure.

<a id="a-141"></a>
**A-141 · Article 22: protection against arrest and detention**

A person arrested must (i) be told the grounds of arrest as soon as may be, (ii) have the right to consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice, and (iii) be produced before a magistrate within **24 hours**, excluding journey time (Art. 22(1)–(2)). These do not apply to an enemy alien or a person held under a preventive detention law (Art. 22(3)). *D.K. Basu* (1997) laid down guidelines on arrest.

*Word count: 72*

<a id="a-142"></a>
**A-142 · Preventive detention (Art. 22(4)–(7))**

Preventive detention is detention to prevent a person from acting prejudicially, not to punish. Detention beyond **three months** needs the opinion of an Advisory Board, unless Parliament otherwise provides. The detainee must be told the grounds and given the earliest opportunity to make a representation, though facts against public interest may be withheld. *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) upheld the law; *A.K. Roy* (1982) upheld the National Security Act.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-143"></a>
**A-143 · Article 31A: saving of laws on estates and related matters**

Inserted by the First Amendment (1951), Art. 31A protects laws on the acquisition of estates and rights in them, management takeover of property, amalgamation of corporations, and changes in rights of managers, directors and mining leases from challenge under Arts. 14 and 19. It was made to protect land reform. Art. 31B and the Ninth Schedule go further.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-144"></a>
**A-144 · Article 15(2): access to public places**

No citizen shall, on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth, be subject to any disability, liability, restriction or condition about access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, or the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to public use. It binds **private persons** as well as the State.

*Word count: 72*

<a id="a-145"></a>
**A-145 · Article 16(1)–(3): equality of opportunity in public employment**

Art. 16(1): equality of opportunity in matters of employment or appointment to any office under the State. Art. 16(2): no discrimination only on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth or residence. Art. 16(3): only **Parliament** can prescribe a residence requirement for a State post. Exceptions: reservation for backward classes (16(4)), promotion (16(4A)), EWS (16(6)), and religious offices (16(5)).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-146"></a>
**A-146 · Doctrine of pith and substance**

When a law is challenged as outside the legislature's list, the court looks at its **true nature and character** (its pith and substance); if it relates mainly to a subject within the legislature's competence, it is valid even though it incidentally touches a subject in another list. The idea comes from the Privy Council (*Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee*, 1947) and was applied in *State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* (1951).

*Word count: 69*

<a id="a-147"></a>
**A-147 · Residuary powers**

Art. 248 gives Parliament exclusive power to legislate on any matter **not in the State List or the Concurrent List**, and Entry 97 of the Union List repeats it. Contrast the United States, where residuary powers belong to the States. It is one of the unitary tilts of the Indian system, with Arts. 249–252 and the repugnancy rule in Art. 254.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-148"></a>
**A-148 · The federal system during an Emergency**

Under a proclamation of Emergency (Art. 352) the federal structure bends towards the Centre: Parliament may legislate on State List matters (Art. 250); the Union may give directions to any State (Art. 353); the President may modify the division of revenues (Art. 354). During President's Rule under Art. 356, Parliament exercises the State's legislative power. *S.R. Bommai* (1994) made Art. 356 justiciable.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-149"></a>
**A-149 · Parliamentary and presidential systems**

In a **parliamentary** system the executive is drawn from and is collectively responsible to the legislature (Art. 75(3)); the head of State is nominal; the government can be removed by a vote of no confidence. In a **presidential** system (the United States) the President is elected separately, holds fixed office and is not responsible to the legislature. India follows the parliamentary model (*Shamsher Singh*, 1974).

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-150"></a>
**A-150 · Commissions on Union–State relations**

The **Sarkaria Commission** (appointed 1983, report 1988) rejected radical change, recommended using Art. 356 rarely and as a last resort, consulting States in Concurrent List matters and giving the Inter-State Council (Art. 263) a permanent role. The **Punchhi Commission** (appointed 2007, report 2010) recommended guidelines for Governors' discretion and limits on the use of Art. 356. *Bommai* (1994) took up the Sarkaria recommendations.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-151"></a>
**A-151 · Martial law (Art. 34)**

Art. 34 restricts the rights in Part III while **martial law** is in force in an area: Parliament may by law indemnify any government servant or other person for acts done to maintain or restore order there, and validate sentences, punishments and acts under martial law. Martial law (military rule in a disturbed area) differs from the Emergency under Arts. 352–360, and the Constitution does not define it.

*Word count: 68*
