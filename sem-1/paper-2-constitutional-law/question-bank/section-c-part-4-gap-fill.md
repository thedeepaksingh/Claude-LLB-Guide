## C-25 · Suspension of Fundamental Rights during an Emergency

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. It was a 4-mark question in Dec 2025 Q5 and Dec 2024 Q3 (A-05) and the syllabus names "suspension". Ten-mark version: B-18.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** declaring an Emergency 3 · Art. 358 4 · Art. 359 5 · cases 4 · safeguards and criticism 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Fundamental Rights are not absolute. In ordinary times Art. 19(2)–(6) allows reasonable restrictions, Art. 33 lets Parliament restrict the rights of the armed forces, and Art. 34 deals with martial law. In a national Emergency the Constitution allows much more: Art. 358 stops Art. 19 from limiting the State, and Art. 359 lets the President suspend the right to move the courts for other rights. The Emergency of 1975–77 showed how far this power could go, and the 44th Amendment (1978) rewrote Arts. 352, 358 and 359 as a result (the changes came into force on 20 June 1979).

**1. When can an Emergency be declared? (Art. 352)**
- **Ground.** The President must be satisfied that a grave emergency exists in which the security of India or any part of it is threatened by **war, external aggression or armed rebellion**. Before 1978 the third ground was "internal disturbance"; the 1975 Emergency was declared on that ground. There have been three national Emergencies: 1962 (China), 1971 (Pakistan) and 1975.
- **Safeguards.** (a) The **Union Cabinet's decision must be communicated to the President in writing** (Art. 352(3)). (b) The Proclamation must be laid before each House and ceases after **one month** unless both Houses approve it by a resolution passed by a majority of the total membership of the House and **two-thirds of the members present and voting**. (c) It lasts six months and can be renewed every six months by the same majority. (d) The President must revoke it if the House of the People passes a resolution disapproving it; **one-tenth of the members** can require a special sitting for that purpose within fourteen days.
- **Judicial review.** The 38th Amendment (1975) added a clause making the President's satisfaction final and not open to question in any court. The 44th Amendment omitted that clause.

**2. Article 358: Article 19 ceases to restrict the State**
While a Proclamation declaring that the security of India is threatened by **war or external aggression** is in operation, nothing in Art. 19 restricts the State's power to make any law or take any executive action it would otherwise be competent to make or take. Such a law ceases to have effect, to the extent of the incompetency, when the Proclamation ends, except for things done before.
- It operates **automatically**; no presidential order is needed.
- It affects **only Art. 19**.
- Since the 44th Amendment it applies **only to an Emergency on the ground of war or external aggression**, not armed rebellion.
- Art. 358(2): a law is protected only if it contains a **recital** that it is in relation to the Proclamation in operation when it was made, and executive action is protected only if taken under such a law.

**3. Article 359: suspension of the right to move the courts**
Where a Proclamation is in operation, the President may by order declare that the **right to move any court for the enforcement of such of the rights conferred by Part III as are mentioned in the order** (except Arts. 20 and 21), and all pending proceedings for their enforcement, remain suspended for the period of the Proclamation or a shorter period.
- The **rights themselves are not taken away**, only the remedy; when the order ends the rights revive.
- Only the rights **named in the order** are affected.
- **Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be named** (44th Amendment).
- The order may extend to the whole or any part of India (Art. 359(2)) and must be laid before each House of Parliament (Art. 359(3)).
- During the order, Part III does not restrict the State's power to make laws or take executive action in respect of the named rights, but, as in Art. 358, a law must carry the recital and lapses when the order ends (Art. 359(1A) and (1B)).

| Basis | Article 358 | Article 359 |
|---|---|---|
| Operation | Automatic | By presidential order |
| Rights affected | Art. 19 only | Any Part III right named in the order, except Arts. 20 and 21 |
| Effect | Art. 19 stops restricting State power | Right to move court to enforce the named rights is suspended |
| Ground of Emergency | War or external aggression only | Any Emergency under Art. 352 |
| Duration | Whole Emergency | As stated in the order, not beyond the Emergency |

**4. Cases**
- ***Makhan Singh Tarsikka v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1964 SC 381.** The presidential order of 3 November 1962, issued during the China war, suspended the right to move court to enforce Arts. 14, 21 and 22 for persons deprived of them under the Defence of India Act. The Supreme Court held that the order suspends the **right to move the court, not the rights**, and read it narrowly: a detenu could still challenge the detention on grounds outside the suspended Articles, such as mala fides. *K. Anandan Nambiar v. Chief Secretary, Madras* (1965) followed this.
- ***ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla*, (1976) 2 SCC 521 (5 judges, 4:1).** After the Emergency of 1975, an order suspended the right to move court to enforce Arts. 14, 21 and 22, and persons held under MISA sought habeas corpus. The majority held that while the order was in force no one could challenge detention in court, even for illegality or mala fides. **Khanna J. dissented**: Art. 21 is not the only source of the right to life and liberty, and the rule of law does not end with an Emergency. The 44th Amendment overcame the majority view for the future by putting Arts. 20 and 21 beyond Art. 359, and *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, (2017) 10 SCC 1 overruled the majority view.

**5. Safeguards after 1978 and criticism**
1. "Internal disturbance" replaced by the narrower "armed rebellion"; written Cabinet advice; special majority; approval within a month; six-month renewal; Lok Sabha power to end it.
2. Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. Art. 32(4) says that the right to move the Supreme Court cannot be suspended except as the Constitution itself provides, and Art. 359 is the provision that allows it.
3. The recital rule stops the State from using Emergency power for unrelated laws.

Criticism: "armed rebellion" is undefined; the Houses' approval depends on the government's majority; the other rights (including Art. 14 and the Art. 22 safeguards against preventive detention) can still be made unenforceable by a presidential order; and the real protection against misuse remains an independent judiciary, as the ADM Jabalpur experience shows.

**Conclusion.** Arts. 358 and 359 let the State act quickly in a real national crisis, and the amendments of 1978 closed the door that was used in 1975–77: life and personal liberty are now outside the reach of any Emergency order. The law since *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) and *Puttaswamy* (2017) treats Art. 21 as a right that cannot be given up even in a crisis.

## C-26 · The right to equality beyond Article 14: Articles 15, 16, 17 and 18

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks; the syllabus line is "Equality (Arts. 14–18)". Art. 14 was set in Dec 2022 Q11 (C-06) and reservation in Dec 2024 Q9 (C-07). Ten-mark versions: B-23, B-24, B-25.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and Art. 15 6 · Art. 16 6 · Art. 17 3 · Art. 18 2 · evaluation and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** Art. 14 states the general rule of equality. Arts. 15 to 18 apply it to four specific wrongs: discrimination on grounds such as religion and caste (Art. 15), unequal opportunity in public employment (Art. 16), untouchability (Art. 17) and titles (Art. 18). The Preamble promises "equality of status and of opportunity", and the Court treats the equality code as part of the basic structure.

**1. Article 15: prohibition of discrimination**
- **15(1):** the State shall not discriminate against any citizen **on grounds only of** religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. "Only" means that if a classification rests on another ground as well, Art. 15(1) is not breached.
- **15(2):** no citizen shall be subject to any disability regarding access to shops, restaurants, hotels, places of entertainment, wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort. Unlike most of Part III, it binds **private persons**.
- **Special provisions that are not discrimination:**
  - **15(3):** for women and children (*Government of A.P. v. P.B. Vijayakumar*, 1995: reservation for women in State services).
  - **15(4):** for socially and educationally backward classes and for SCs and STs, inserted by the First Amendment (1951) after *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan*, AIR 1951 SC 226.
  - **15(5):** for admission to educational institutions, including private unaided ones but excluding minority institutions (93rd Amendment, 2005; upheld in *Ashoka Kumar Thakur*, 2008, and in *Pramati*, 2014, for private unaided institutions).
  - **15(6):** up to 10% for economically weaker sections (103rd Amendment, 2019).
- **Sex discrimination.** *C.B. Muthamma* (1979) condemned Foreign Service rules that discriminated against married women; *Air India v. Nargesh Meerza* (1981) struck down the rule ending a hostess's service on her first pregnancy as arbitrary; *Anuj Garg v. Hotel Association of India* (2008) struck down a ban on women working in bars, saying that protective discrimination based on stereotypes is invalid; *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*, (1997) 6 SCC 241 laid down workplace guidelines on sexual harassment, now replaced by the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013; *Joseph Shine v. Union of India*, (2019) 3 SCC 39 struck down adultery (s. 497 IPC) as violating Arts. 14, 15 and 21. In *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India*, (2018) 10 SCC 1 s. 377 IPC was held unconstitutional as applied to consensual sex between adults.

**2. Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment**
- **16(1):** equality of opportunity in matters relating to employment or appointment to any office under the State.
- **16(2):** no discrimination **only** on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth or residence.
- **16(3):** only **Parliament** may prescribe a residence requirement for a State post.
- **Exceptions:** **16(4)** reservation for backward classes not adequately represented; **16(4A)** in promotion for SCs and STs (77th Amendment, 1995); **16(4B)** carry-forward of unfilled vacancies (81st Amendment, 2000); **16(5)** offices of religious institutions; **16(6)** up to 10% for economically weaker sections (103rd Amendment).
- **Cases.**
  - ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India*, 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217 (9 judges):** the 27% reservation for OBCs was upheld; the creamy layer must be excluded; reservation should in general not exceed 50%; no reservation in promotion (later overcome by the 77th Amendment).
  - ***M. Nagaraj*, 2006:** the amendments on promotion and carry-forward were upheld, but the State must show quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequate representation and administrative efficiency; *Jarnail Singh* (2018) refined this.
  - ***Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India*, (2023) 5 SCC 1 (judgment of 7 November 2022, 3:2):** the EWS amendment was upheld; the dissent (Bhat J. with Lalit CJI) held that excluding the poorest SC, ST and OBC people violates the equality code. A review petition was dismissed on 9 May 2023.
  - ***State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024, 7 judges, 6:1):** States may sub-classify Scheduled Castes on the basis of quantifiable, empirical data; *E.V. Chinnaiah* was overruled.

**3. Article 17: abolition of untouchability**
"Untouchability" is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden; the enforcement of any disability arising out of it is an offence. It binds the State and **private persons** (*State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale*, 1993). The word is not defined; it refers to the social practice of treating certain castes as unclean, and not to physical touch. Parliament has enacted the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** (the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed in 1976) and the **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**. *Subhash Kashinath Mahajan* (2018) limited automatic arrests under the 1989 Act; Parliament restored them by s. 18A (2018), and *Prathvi Raj Chauhan* (2020) upheld the amendment.

**4. Article 18: abolition of titles**
(1) The State shall not confer any title other than a military or academic distinction. (2) A citizen shall not accept a title from a foreign State. (3) A non-citizen holding an office of profit under the State needs the President's consent to accept a foreign title. (4) No person holding an office of profit or trust under the State may accept a present, emolument or office from a foreign State without the President's consent. In *Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India*, (1996) 1 SCC 361 (5 judges), the Court held that the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are **not titles** under Art. 18(1) but may not be used as prefixes or suffixes to the holder's name.

**5. Evaluation**
1. The equality code accepts both **formal equality** (Arts. 15(1), 16(2)) and **substantive equality** (Arts. 15(3)–(6), 16(4)–(6)); the courts have struggled to say where one ends and the other begins.
2. The 50% ceiling, the creamy layer, sub-classification and the EWS quota are all unsettled in some respect. The Court has said that the ceiling is not inflexible (*Janhit Abhiyan*) while several State laws that exceed it are being challenged.
3. Art. 15(2) and Art. 17 show that the Constitution treats some social wrongs as too serious to leave to State action alone.

**Conclusion.** Arts. 15 to 18 take the abstract promise of Art. 14 and apply it to caste, sex, public employment and social rank. They allow the State to treat unequals unequally but demand data, reasons and a ceiling. The law on reservation keeps changing, so the dates of each decision should be checked before the exam.

## C-27 · Freedom of religion: Articles 26, 27 and 28

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks; the syllabus line is "freedom of religion (Arts. 25–28)". Art. 25 was set in Dec 2024 Q11 (C-19). Ten-mark versions: B-36, B-37, B-38.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and Art. 26 text 4 · denomination and cases on Art. 26 6 · essential religious practices 4 · Arts. 27 and 28 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Art. 25 protects the individual's freedom of conscience and religion (see C-19). Arts. 26 to 28 protect the community's religious institutions and keep the State neutral between religions. Together they give India a form of secularism in which the State neither establishes a religion nor stays aloof from it: it regulates secular activity and reforms social practice, but does not promote any one faith. The Court has held secularism to be a basic feature (*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, (1994) 3 SCC 1).

**1. Article 26: freedom to manage religious affairs**
Subject to public order, morality and health, **every religious denomination or any section of it** has the right to (a) establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes; (b) manage its own affairs in matters of religion; (c) own and acquire movable and immovable property; and (d) administer such property **in accordance with law**.

- **Who is a "denomination"?** In *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt*, AIR 1954 SC 282, the Court defined it as a body of persons with a **common faith, a common organisation and a distinctive name**. The Mutt of the Madhva sect in that case was held to be one. Ayyappa devotees (*Sabarimala*, 2018) and the Auroville community (*S.P. Mittal*, 1983) were held not to be denominations.
- **Matters of religion.** *Shirur Mutt* held that these include ritual, ceremony and observances, and also the denomination's own decision about what is essential to its faith, so the State cannot override that decision. Administration of property is a **secular** matter that the State may regulate by law, but it cannot take away the right of administration altogether (see also *Ratilal Panachand Gandhi*, 1954).
- ***Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali*, AIR 1961 SC 1402:** only practices that are essential or integral to the religion are protected; superstitious accretions are not; and the claim of the Khadims to manage the Durgah was held to concern secular management, which the State may regulate.
- ***Sardar Syedna Taher Saifuddin Saheb v. State of Bombay* (1962):** the Bombay Prevention of Excommunication Act, 1949 was struck down because the power of the head of the Dawoodi Bohra community to excommunicate members on religious grounds is a matter of religion under Art. 26(b).
- ***Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore*, AIR 1958 SC 255:** where a denominational temple's right under Art. 26(b) clashes with a law throwing open Hindu temples to all sections under Art. 25(2)(b), the Court harmonised them: the law prevails except for the rites that are essential to the denomination's worship.

**2. Essential religious practices**
The Court protects under Arts. 25 and 26 only practices that are essential or integral to the religion; others may be regulated. *Shirur Mutt* is the source. Later cases: *Hanif Quareshi* (1958), cow sacrifice is not essential to Islam; *Acharya Jagadishwarananda Avadhuta* (1983), the *tandava* dance is not essential to Ananda Marga; *M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India*, (1994) 6 SCC 360, a mosque is not essential to Islam so the State could acquire the land; *Shayara Bano v. Union of India*, (2017) 9 SCC 1 (3:2), the practice of instant triple talaq was set aside; *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (2018, 4:1), the bar on women aged 10–50 at Sabarimala was not essential and violated Arts. 14, 15, 17 and 25.
- **Criticism.** "Essential" is not in the text; judges decide questions of theology; and oral and minority traditions are at risk. The alternative is a test of whether a practice is harmful or discriminatory.
- **Present position.** In the Sabarimala review (*Kantaru Rajeevaru*, 14 November 2019) wider questions on Arts. 25 and 26 were referred to a nine-judge bench, which has heard the reference; **check whether judgment has been pronounced**. The hijab case (*Aishat Shifa*, 13 October 2022, split verdict) also awaits a larger bench.

**3. Article 27: no tax for promotion of a religion**
No person shall be compelled to pay any **tax**, the proceeds of which are specially appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.
- It forbids a tax, not a fee for a service rendered.
- In *Shirur Mutt* the contribution under s. 76 of the Madras Act was held to be a tax, not a fee, and was void because the legislature had no power to levy it; Art. 27 was not breached because the proceeds were not used to promote a particular religion.
- *Prafull Goradia v. Union of India*, (2011) 2 SCC 568: Art. 27 is breached only if a substantial part of a tax is used for a particular religion; the Haj subsidy challenge failed. In *Rafique Shaikh Bhikan* (2012) the Court directed that the subsidy be phased out, and it ended in 2018.

**4. Article 28: religious instruction in educational institutions**
- **28(1):** no religious instruction in any institution **wholly maintained out of State funds**.
- **28(2):** this does not apply to an institution administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust that requires religious instruction.
- **28(3):** in an institution **recognised by the State or receiving State aid**, no person can be made to take part in religious instruction or worship without his consent (or that of his guardian, if a minor).
- Private, unaided and unrecognised institutions are outside the Article.
- *Aruna Roy v. Union of India*, (2002) 7 SCC 368: teaching about the philosophy and values of all religions, as in the National Curriculum Framework, is not "religious instruction".

**5. Arts. 25, 26, 27, 28 compared**
| Article | Holder | Content | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | Every person | Conscience, profession, practice, propagation | Public order, morality, health and other Part III rights; Art. 25(2) |
| 26 | Denomination | Institutions, management of religious affairs, property | Public order, morality, health |
| 27 | Every person | Freedom from tax for a particular religion | Does not bar fees or a general tax |
| 28 | Students and parents | Freedom from compulsory religious instruction or worship | Applies to State-funded, aided and recognised institutions |

**Conclusion.** Arts. 26 to 28 protect the community's right to run its own religious affairs while keeping public money and public education neutral between faiths. The central difficulty is the line between what is "essential" and what the State may reform; the nine-judge reference in the Sabarimala case should settle the test.

## C-28 · Directive Principles of State Policy: nature, classification and importance

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks in this form. The syllabus line is "Directive Principles of State Policy: importance and relationship with Fundamental Rights"; the relationship has been set at 10 and 20 marks in three successive years (C-23, B-07); this answer is the "importance" half. Ten-mark version: B-44.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** nature and source 4 · classification and key Articles 6 · importance with cases and laws 6 · criticism and conclusion 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** Part IV of the Constitution (Arts. 36–51) lists the principles that the State should follow in governing the country. They were borrowed from the **Irish Constitution (1937)**, and they carry forward the "Instrument of Instructions" of the Government of India Act, 1935. They state the aim of a **welfare State**. Dr Ambedkar described them as "instruments of instructions" to the legislature and the executive, and Granville Austin called the Fundamental Rights and the Directive Principles the "conscience of the Constitution". The framers made them non-justiciable because the State's resources were limited and because a court cannot compel a legislature to spend.

**1. Nature**
- **Art. 36:** "State" has the same meaning as in Part III.
- **Art. 37:** the provisions of Part IV "shall not be enforceable by any court", but the principles are "**nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country**" and it is the **duty of the State to apply them in making laws**.
- A law that ignores a directive is not void; a court cannot order the legislature to enact one.
- The Fundamental Rights protect political democracy; the Directive Principles aim at **social and economic democracy**.
- **Difference from Fundamental Rights.** Fundamental Rights are negative duties on the State and enforceable by writ; Directive Principles are positive duties, not enforceable, and need legislation. Rights benefit individuals; directives benefit society as a whole.

**2. Classification** (an academic device; the Constitution does not classify them)
| Group | Directives |
|---|---|
| **Socialistic** | Art. 38 (social order and reducing inequalities); Art. 39(a)–(f) (livelihood; distribution of material resources; no concentration of wealth; equal pay for equal work; health of workers; childhood); 39A (free legal aid); 41 (work, education, public assistance); 42 (humane conditions, maternity relief); 43 (living wage); 43A (workers' participation); 47 (nutrition, public health) |
| **Gandhian** | Art. 40 (village panchayats); 43 (cottage industries); 43B (co-operative societies); 46 (SCs, STs and weaker sections); 47 (prohibition of intoxicating drinks); 48 (agriculture, animal husbandry, cow slaughter) |
| **Liberal-intellectual** | Art. 44 (uniform civil code); 45 (early childhood care and education); 48A (environment, forests, wildlife); 49 (monuments); 50 (separating the judiciary from the executive); 51 (international peace and security) |

Some directives lie outside Part IV: Art. 335 (claims of SCs and STs to services), Art. 350A (instruction in the mother tongue) and Art. 351 (development of Hindi). Amendments have added or changed several: 39A, 43A and 48A and a reworded 39(f) (42nd Amendment, 1976), 43B (97th Amendment, 2011), and a rewritten Art. 45 (86th Amendment, 2002).

**3. Importance**
1. **A guide for legislation and administration.** Land reform and the abolition of zamindari (Art. 39(b) and (c)); Panchayati Raj through the 73rd Amendment (Art. 40); the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 (Art. 39(d)); the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Art. 39A); MGNREGA, 2005 (Art. 41); the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (Art. 42); the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 (Art. 48A); the National Food Security Act, 2013 (Art. 47); and the separation of judicial and executive magistrates in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (Art. 50).
2. **An aid to interpreting rights.** The Court reads Fundamental Rights in the light of the directives. *Randhir Singh v. Union of India* (1982): equal pay for equal work, in Art. 39(d), is made enforceable through Arts. 14 and 16. *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation*, (1985) 3 SCC 545: the right to livelihood is part of Art. 21, supported by Arts. 39(a) and 41. *Hussainara Khatoon* (1980): free legal aid (Art. 39A) is part of a fair procedure under Art. 21. *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996) and *M.C. Mehta*: Art. 48A and the right to a clean environment. *Unni Krishnan* (1993): Art. 45 helped establish the right to education up to 14, which led to Art. 21A.
3. **Balance with Fundamental Rights.** *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) held that the directives are subordinate to the rights. The position changed through *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), which upheld the first part of Art. 31C, and *Minerva Mills v. Union of India*, (1980) 3 SCC 625, which held that **harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is an essential feature of the basic structure**. See C-23.
   - Directives can also justify a restriction on a right: in *State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat* (2005) 8 SCC 534 (seven judges) a ban on cow slaughter was upheld as a reasonable restriction on Art. 19(1)(g) in the light of Art. 48.
   - Art. 44: the Court has repeatedly urged a uniform civil code (*Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985) 2 SCC 556; *Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) 3 SCC 635; *John Vallamattom v. Union of India* (2003) 6 SCC 611), but the 21st Law Commission (2018) found a uniform code neither necessary nor desirable at that stage and the 22nd Law Commission invited public views in June 2023.
4. **A yardstick for governments.** They give citizens a standard by which to judge a government's performance, and they inform the Preamble's promise of social, economic and political justice.
5. **Recent developments.** In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024, 9 judges) the Court held that not every privately owned resource is a "material resource of the community" under Art. 39(b); it applies only if it passes a test of the resource's nature, its effect on community welfare, its scarcity and the consequences of its concentration. The majority disapproved the wide reading of Art. 39(b) associated with Krishna Iyer J. in earlier cases (*State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy*, 1977, and *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.*, 1983). Art. 44 is under active debate: Uttarakhand enacted a uniform civil code (assent 13 March 2024, in force 27 January 2025), and Goa has had one since Portuguese times (see B-45).

**4. Criticism**
1. They are not enforceable, so governments can ignore them.
2. They are not ranked by priority and some conflict with each other or with rights.
3. They need resources that the Constitution does not provide.
4. Some (prohibition, cow protection) reflect particular social views.

Supporters answer that the directives are a moral and political guide; that courts have given many of them legal force through Art. 21; and that the people judge governments by them.

**Conclusion.** The Directive Principles are the promise of the Constitution to the poor: Part III restrains the State and Part IV directs it. They were non-justiciable by design, because the framers knew that the State had limited resources, but their force today comes from the courts' practice of reading them into the rights and from legislation passed to give effect to them.

## C-29 · The writs under Articles 32 and 226

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks; the syllabus line is "constitutional remedies (Art. 32)". Art. 32 was set in Dec 2023 Q12 (C-22). Ten-mark versions: B-40, B-41.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** source and nature of writs 3 · each writ with grounds and case 10 · limits 3 · Art. 226 comparison 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** A writ is a written order of a court commanding a person or authority to do or not to do something. The Constitution empowers the **Supreme Court** under Art. 32(2) and the **High Courts** under Art. 226 to issue "directions, orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari". They are English prerogative writs, but the Indian courts are not bound by the technical limits of English law: *T.C. Basappa v. T. Nagappa*, AIR 1954 SC 440. Art. 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, while Art. 226 is a constitutional power (see C-22).

**1. Habeas corpus ("you may have the body")**
It secures the release of a person who is unlawfully detained, by the State or by a private person.
- Any person can apply on behalf of the detenu; the court looks at the legality of the detention when it hears the petition; the physical production of the detenu is not essential (*Kanu Sanyal v. District Magistrate, Darjeeling*, (1973) 2 SCC 674).
- It has been extended to conditions inside a prison (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration*, (1978) 4 SCC 494) and to disappearances (*Sebastian Hongray*, 1984).
- Compensation was awarded in *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*, (1983) 4 SCC 141, where a prisoner was kept in jail for years after his acquittal.

**2. Mandamus ("we command")**
It commands a public authority to perform a **public or statutory duty**.
- Conditions: (i) a legal right in the petitioner; (ii) a legal public duty on the respondent; (iii) a demand and refusal or failure.
- It does not lie to enforce a discretionary or purely contractual duty, to compel a legislature to enact a law, or against the President or a Governor personally (Art. 361).
- It lies against public officials and authorities that are "State" under Art. 12, but not to enforce a private or contractual obligation, or a duty not shown to be public or statutory (*Praga Tools Corporation v. C.A. Imanual*, (1969) 1 SCC 585).
- *Vineet Narain v. Union of India*, (1998) 1 SCC 226: a "continuing mandamus" by which the Court keeps a case open and monitors the investigation and compliance.

**3. Prohibition ("to forbid")**
It forbids an inferior court, tribunal or quasi-judicial authority from exceeding its jurisdiction or acting against natural justice **while proceedings are pending** (*East India Commercial Co. v. Collector of Customs*, AIR 1962 SC 1893). It is **preventive**.

**4. Certiorari ("to be certified")**
It **quashes** an order already made by an inferior court, tribunal or quasi-judicial authority.
- Grounds: lack or excess of jurisdiction; an error of law apparent on the face of the record; breach of natural justice; fraud or bias. It does not lie for a mere error of fact or to re-appreciate evidence.
- *Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Syed Ahmad Ishaque*, AIR 1955 SC 233 set out the grounds; *Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan*, AIR 1964 SC 477 held that certiorari is not an appeal.
- It is **curative**, whereas prohibition is preventive. A judicial order of a High Court cannot be quashed by a writ of certiorari under Art. 32 (*Naresh Mirajkar*, 1967); the remedy is a review or a curative petition (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*, 2002).

**5. Quo warranto ("by what authority")**
It tests a person's **right to hold a public office**.
- The office must be public, created by statute or the Constitution, and substantive; the holder must lack the qualifications or have been appointed contrary to law.
- **Any person** may apply; no personal injury is needed (*University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao*, AIR 1965 SC 491).
- *Kumar Padma Prasad v. Union of India*, (1992) 2 SCC 428: the appointment of a High Court judge who lacked the constitutional qualification was quashed.

| Writ | Directed at | Purpose | Stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Habeas corpus | Person detaining | Release from unlawful detention | Any time |
| Mandamus | Public authority | Perform a public duty | After a demand and refusal |
| Prohibition | Inferior court or tribunal | Stop excess of jurisdiction | Before the decision |
| Certiorari | Inferior court or tribunal | Quash the order | After the decision |
| Quo warranto | Holder of a public office | Test the title to the office | While the person holds the office |

**6. Limits on the writs**
1. **Res judicata.** A dismissal on merits by the High Court under Art. 226 bars a petition on the same facts under Art. 32 (*Daryao v. State of U.P.*, AIR 1961 SC 1457); dismissal in limine or for delay does not.
2. **Delay (laches)** and the availability of an **alternative remedy** are grounds to refuse a writ under Art. 226. Under Art. 32 the Court cannot refuse merely because another remedy exists, but it may send the petitioner to the High Court when facts are disputed.
3. **Private persons** are covered only where they perform a public function or are "State".
4. **Suspension.** The right to move the Supreme Court cannot be suspended except as the Constitution provides (Art. 32(4); Art. 359).

**7. Beyond the writs**
Art. 32 also allows "directions" and "orders". The Court has used them to award **public-law compensation** (*Rudul Sah*; *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa*, (1993) 2 SCC 746), to lay down **guidelines** where the law is silent (*Vishaka*, 1997; *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.*, (1997) 1 SCC 416), and to hear **public interest litigation** (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981 Supp SCC 87).

**8. Art. 32 and Art. 226 compared**
Art. 32 is available only for Fundamental Rights and is itself a right, so the Supreme Court must entertain the petition; Art. 226 covers Fundamental Rights **and "any other purpose"**, is discretionary and is wider in subject. Both can issue the five writs. *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261 held that the High Courts' power under Art. 226 and the Supreme Court's power under Art. 32 are part of the basic structure.

**Conclusion.** The five writs are the working tools of constitutional remedies. Habeas corpus guards liberty, mandamus enforces public duties, prohibition and certiorari control tribunals before and after decision, and quo warranto checks usurpation of public office. The Court has widened them by compensation, guidelines and PIL, and has narrowed them by res judicata and the rule against reviewing judicial orders.
