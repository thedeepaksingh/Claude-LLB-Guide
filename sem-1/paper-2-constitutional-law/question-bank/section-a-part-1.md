<a id="a-01"></a>
**A-01 · Terms added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q1*

The 42nd Amendment added three words to the Preamble: **Socialist** (a welfare State that reduces inequality), **Secular** (no State religion; equal respect for all faiths) and **Integrity** (the unity and integrity of the Nation). It also inserted Part IVA (Fundamental Duties, Art. 51A) and Arts. 39A, 43A and 48A, and extended Art. 31C, which *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck down.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-02"></a>
**A-02 · Four Fundamental Duties** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q2; Dec 2024 Q5; Dec 2023 Q4 (as "Fundamental duties")*

Part IVA, Art. 51A (42nd Amendment, 1976; eleven duties since the 86th Amendment, 2002). Four duties of every citizen: (a) to abide by the Constitution and respect the National Flag and Anthem; (c) to uphold the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India; (g) to protect and improve the natural environment and have compassion for living creatures; (i) to safeguard public property and abjure violence. They are non-justiciable.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-03"></a>
**A-03 · Against whom Fundamental Rights are available** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q3*

Mainly against the **State** (Art. 12): the Government and Parliament, State Governments and Legislatures, and all local or other authorities, including instrumentalities of the State (*Ajay Hasia*, 1981). Some rights bind **private persons** too: Art. 15(2), Art. 17, Art. 23 (*PUDR*, 1982) and Art. 24. *Kaushal Kishor* (2023) held that Arts. 19 and 21 can also be enforced against private actors.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-04"></a>
**A-04 · Protection from ex post facto laws** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q4*

Art. 20(1) protects any person from (i) conviction for an act that was not an offence when committed, and (ii) a penalty greater than the law then in force allowed. It applies only to **criminal** laws; civil and tax laws may be retrospective. Procedural changes are allowed (*Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh*, 1953), and a retrospective law that benefits the accused is valid (*Ratan Lal*, 1965). It cannot be suspended in an Emergency.

*Word count: 72*

<a id="a-05"></a>
**A-05 · Suspension of Fundamental Rights** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q5; Dec 2024 Q3*

During a national Emergency, **Art. 358** automatically suspends the freedoms of Art. 19 when the Emergency is on the ground of war or external aggression. **Art. 359** lets the President by order suspend the right to move any court to enforce the Part III rights he names, but **not Arts. 20 and 21** (44th Amendment, 1978). Art. 33 (armed forces) and Art. 34 (martial law) also limit rights.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-06"></a>
**A-06 · Salient features of the Preamble** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q1; Dec 2023 Q5 (as "Preamble")*

The Preamble (i) shows that the Constitution derives from "We, the People of India"; (ii) describes India as a **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic**; (iii) states the objectives: **Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity**, assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; (iv) gives the date of adoption, 26 November 1949. It is a part of the Constitution (*Kesavananda*, 1973) and aids interpretation.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-07"></a>
**A-07 · Federalism** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q2*

Federalism divides powers between a central government and regional governments, each independent in its own sphere, under a written constitution interpreted by an independent judiciary (K.C. Wheare). Its marks are a written constitution, division of powers, a rigid amendment procedure and a supreme court. India is a "Union of States" (Art. 1): federal in structure, with a strong Centre.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-08"></a>
**A-08 · Federal structure of the Indian Constitution** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q3; Dec 2022 Q2 (as "Federal Constitution")*

**Federal features:** a written, supreme Constitution; dual polity; division of powers by the Seventh Schedule (Art. 246); independent judiciary and Art. 131; ratification by half the States for amending federal provisions (Art. 368(2)). **Unitary features:** Art. 3; residuary power (Art. 248); Emergency powers; Governors appointed by the President; single citizenship. *Bommai* (1994) holds federalism a basic feature.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-09"></a>
**A-09 · Right to Education** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q4*

Art. 21A (86th Amendment, 2002): the State shall provide free and compulsory education to children aged six to fourteen as it may by law determine. It follows *Mohini Jain* (1992) and *Unni Krishnan* (1993). The RTE Act, 2009 (in force 1 April 2010) gives effect, including a 25% quota in private unaided schools. Art. 51A(k) makes parents responsible; Art. 45 covers early childhood care.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-10"></a>
**A-10 · Rule of law** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q1*

Dicey's rule of law means supremacy of law over arbitrary power, equality before the law, and rights secured through the ordinary courts. In India it is part of the basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973; *Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975). Art. 14 forbids arbitrary State action (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978); Arts. 32 and 226 give remedies. Exceptions include Art. 361 (immunity of the President and Governors).

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-11"></a>
**A-11 · Socialist State** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q2*

"Socialist" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976). Indian socialism is democratic and welfare-oriented: it aims to reduce inequality (Arts. 38, 39) and secure social and economic justice, not to nationalise all property. *D.S. Nakara* (1983) used it to strike down an arbitrary pension classification; *Balram Singh* (2024) held that it does not bar private enterprise.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-12"></a>
**A-12 · Fundamental law** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q1*

A fundamental law is the supreme law of a State, from which every other law derives validity and against which every law is tested. The Constitution of India is such a law: Art. 13 voids laws inconsistent with Part III; legislative power is "subject to the provisions of this Constitution" (Art. 245); courts review laws (Arts. 32, 226); and the amending power cannot destroy the basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973).

*Word count: 69*

<a id="a-13"></a>
**A-13 · Parliamentary form of government** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q3*

India follows the Westminster model: a nominal head (the President; the Governor in States), a real executive in the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister (Arts. 74–75), collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)), ministers drawn from the legislature, and a Lok Sabha that can be dissolved. *Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955) and *Shamsher Singh* (1974) confirm that the President acts on ministerial advice.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-14"></a>
**A-14 · Secular State** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q4*

A secular State has no State religion and treats all religions equally. India's secularism is positive: the State keeps a principled distance, may regulate secular activity linked to religion, and may carry out social reform (Art. 25(2)). "Secular" was added to the Preamble in 1976; *Bommai* (1994) held secularism a basic feature. Arts. 14–16 and 25–30 give it effect.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-15"></a>
**A-15 · Right to property** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q5*

Originally a Fundamental Right (Arts. 19(1)(f) and 31), the right to property was removed from Part III by the **44th Amendment (1978)**. It is now a constitutional right under **Art. 300A**: "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law." The deprivation must be by a valid law (*Jilubhai Khachar*, 1995), and the Court has read in a public-purpose requirement (*K.T. Plantation*, 2011).

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-16"></a>
**A-16 · Union of States (Art. 1)**

Art. 1(1): "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." Dr Ambedkar explained that "Union" was chosen because the federation is not the result of an agreement between States and no State has a right to secede. Parliament may form new States and alter areas, boundaries and names by ordinary law (Arts. 2–4): "an indestructible Union of destructible States" (*Babulal Parate*, 1960).

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-17"></a>
**A-17 · Quasi-federal Constitution**

K.C. Wheare described the Indian Constitution as quasi-federal: a unitary State with subsidiary federal features. It has federal features (written Constitution, division of powers, Art. 131, rigid amendment of federal provisions) and unitary ones (Arts. 3, 248–253, 356; Governors appointed by the Centre; single citizenship). *State of W.B. v. Union of India* (1963) said that it is not a federation in the strict sense.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-18"></a>
**A-18 · Cooperative federalism**

The Union and States work together rather than at arm's length. Instruments include the Inter-State Council (Art. 263), the Finance Commission (Art. 280), the GST Council (Art. 279A), whose recommendations are persuasive, not binding (*Mohit Minerals*, 2022), and the water-disputes machinery (Art. 262). *GNCTD v. Union of India* (2018) treats Indian federalism as cooperative.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-19"></a>
**A-19 · Asymmetric federalism**

Different States have different arrangements. Arts. 371 to 371J make special provision for States such as Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Mizoram and Karnataka. Art. 370 gave special status to Jammu and Kashmir; its abrogation was upheld in *In re Article 370* (11 December 2023), where the Court accepted that the Constitution permits asymmetric federalism.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-20"></a>
**A-20 · Partly rigid and partly flexible**

Three methods of amendment exist. (i) Simple majority outside Art. 368, for example the formation of States (Arts. 2–4) and citizenship. (ii) Special majority (a majority of the total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting) for most provisions. (iii) Special majority plus ratification by half the States for federal provisions (Art. 368(2) proviso). The basic structure cannot be altered (*Kesavananda*).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-21"></a>
**A-21 · Separation of powers in India**

The Constitution does not adopt a strict separation. *Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955) said that the doctrine is not recognised in its absolute rigidity, but the functions of the three organs are sufficiently differentiated. Ministers sit in the legislature. *Kesavananda* (1973) and *Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) treat separation of powers as a basic feature.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-22"></a>
**A-22 · President as the constitutional head**

The President is the formal head (Arts. 52–53). By Art. 74(1) the President acts in accordance with the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers; the 44th Amendment lets him ask for reconsideration once, after which he is bound. *Shamsher Singh* (1974): the President and Governors act on ministerial advice except where the Constitution gives discretion.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-23"></a>
**A-23 · Objectives Resolution**

Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on 13 December 1946 and adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 22 January 1947, it declared India an independent sovereign republic and promised justice, equality and freedom to all, with safeguards for minorities and backward classes. It is the basis of the Preamble.

*Word count: 47*

<a id="a-24"></a>
**A-24 · Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?**

*Berubari* (1960) said no: the Preamble is a key to the framers' minds, not a part of the Constitution. *Kesavananda* (1973) overruled this: it is a part and contains the basic features. *LIC v. CERC* (1995) calls it an integral part. It aids interpretation but cannot override an express provision.

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-25"></a>
**A-25 · "We, the People of India"**

The words show that the Constitution derives its authority from the people, not from Parliament or any outside power. The Constituent Assembly was indirectly elected, so the phrase also states a principle of popular sovereignty. The Preamble ends with the people adopting, enacting and giving to themselves this Constitution on 26 November 1949.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-26"></a>
**A-26 · Democratic Republic**

"Democratic" means government by the will of the people through free and fair elections on universal adult franchise (Art. 326); free and fair elections are a basic feature (*Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975). "Republic" means that the head of State is elected and not hereditary: the President is elected by an electoral college (Arts. 54–55).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-27"></a>
**A-27 · Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity**

The Preamble secures **Justice**, social, economic and political (Part IV, Arts. 38–39); **Liberty** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship (Arts. 19, 25); **Equality** of status and of opportunity (Arts. 14–18); and **Fraternity** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation (Arts. 17, 51A(e)).

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-28"></a>
**A-28 · Amendability of the Preamble**

The Preamble has been amended once, by the 42nd Amendment (1976). *Kesavananda* (1973) accepted that it can be amended under Art. 368 without altering the basic structure. In *Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024) the Court held that Art. 368 extends to the Preamble, that the date 26 November 1949 is no bar, and dismissed the challenge to the 1976 words.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-29"></a>
**A-29 · "State" under Article 12**

Art. 12 defines "State" for Part III: the Government and Parliament of India; the Government and Legislature of each State; and all local or other authorities within India or under the control of the Government. Bodies that pass the instrumentality test (*Ajay Hasia*, 1981) are included; *Pradeep Kumar Biswas* (2002) asks whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the Government.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-30"></a>
**A-30 · Doctrine of eclipse**

A law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is not dead but overshadowed. It cannot be enforced while the inconsistency lasts and revives, without re-enactment, when the inconsistency is removed (*Bhikaji Narain Dhakras*, 1955). It applies to pre-Constitution laws (Art. 13(1)); a post-Constitution law that violates a right of all persons is stillborn (*Deep Chand*, 1959).

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-31"></a>
**A-31 · Doctrine of severability**

If only part of a law violates Part III and can be separated from the rest, only that part is void. The tests (*R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla*, 1957) are the legislature's intention, the separability of valid and invalid parts, and whether the rest forms a workable whole. If the parts are inseparable, the whole falls. *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) severed s. 14 of the Preventive Detention Act.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-32"></a>
**A-32 · Doctrine of waiver**

A person cannot waive a Fundamental Right, because the rights rest on public policy and the Constitution (*Basheshar Nath v. CIT*, 1959). There is no estoppel against the Constitution (*Olga Tellis*, 1985). A person cannot, for instance, consent to a law that violates Art. 14, and acquiescence cannot validate an unconstitutional act.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-33"></a>
**A-33 · Basic structure doctrine**

Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution under Art. 368 but cannot alter its basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973, 7:6). Basic features include supremacy of the Constitution, democracy, secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review and the balance between Parts III and IV (*Minerva Mills*, 1980). The list is open.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-34"></a>
**A-34 · Ninth Schedule (Art. 31B)**

Inserted by the First Amendment (1951) to protect land-reform laws. Laws placed in it are immune from challenge under Part III. In *I.R. Coelho* (2007) nine judges held that Ninth Schedule laws enacted after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed if they damage the basic structure or the essence of Fundamental Rights.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-35"></a>
**A-35 · Article 31C**

Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971): a law giving effect to Art. 39(b) or (c) cannot be challenged for violating Arts. 14, 19 or 31. *Kesavananda* (1973) upheld this but struck down the bar on judicial review. The 42nd Amendment extended it to all Directive Principles; *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck down that extension.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-36"></a>
**A-36 · Article 33**

Art. 33 allows Parliament to restrict or abrogate the Fundamental Rights of members of the armed forces, the police and similar forces, to ensure the proper discharge of their duties and discipline. Only Parliament may make such a law (Art. 35). Art. 34 permits restrictions on rights while martial law is in force, with indemnity for acts done.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-37"></a>
**A-37 · Who may claim Fundamental Rights**

Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30 are claimed by **citizens** only. Arts. 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22 and 23–28 are available to **all persons**, including foreigners (*NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh*, 1996). A company is a person for Art. 14 but not a citizen for Art. 19 (*State Trading Corporation*, 1963).

*Word count: 53*
