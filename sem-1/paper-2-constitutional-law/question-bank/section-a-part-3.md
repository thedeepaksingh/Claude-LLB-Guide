<a id="a-77"></a>
**A-77 · Article 23: traffic in human beings and forced labour**

Art. 23(1) prohibits **traffic in human beings, *begar* and other similar forms of forced labour**; contravention is an offence. It protects against the State and private persons alike. Art. 23(2) allows the State to impose **compulsory service for public purposes** (for example, national service), but only without discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste or class. Art. 23 covers citizens and non-citizens.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-78"></a>
**A-78 · *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (Asiad Workers' case, 1982)**

The Court held that "forced labour" under Art. 23 covers any labour or service obtained by force, and also **labour paid below the minimum wage**, because a person who accepts it from economic need has no real choice. Art. 23 operates against private contractors as well. *Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan* (1983) applied the same reasoning to famine-relief work paid below the minimum wage.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-79"></a>
**A-79 · Bonded labour**

Bonded labour is forced labour arising from a debt or advance. The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** abolished the system, freed bonded labourers and extinguished their debts. In *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) the Court, reading Art. 23 with Art. 21, ordered the identification, release and rehabilitation of bonded labourers in stone quarries, and held that the State must enforce the Act.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-80"></a>
**A-80 · Article 24: prohibition of child labour**

Art. 24: no child below **fourteen** shall be employed to work in any **factory or mine or in any other hazardous employment**. It is enforceable against employers. The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended in 2016, prohibits employment of children under 14 in all occupations (with limited exceptions for family enterprises and screen work) and of adolescents (14–18) in hazardous work.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-81"></a>
**A-81 · *M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996)**

Children were employed in the match and fireworks factories of Sivakasi. The Court held that they could not be employed in hazardous processes, but could work in non-hazardous packing, away from the factory, for limited hours. It directed offending employers to pay compensation of Rs 20,000 per child into a fund, and the State to find alternative employment for an adult in the family, or contribute to the fund.

*Word count: 69*

<a id="a-82"></a>
**A-82 · Article 23 and Article 24 compared**

Art. 23 covers *begar*, forced labour and trafficking of **any person**, of any age, in any employment. Art. 24 protects only **children below fourteen** and only against employment in factories, mines or hazardous work. Art. 23 has an exception (compulsory public service); Art. 24 has none. Both bind private employers and are supported by Arts. 39(e)–(f) and 45 of the Directive Principles.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-83"></a>
**A-83 · Human trafficking**

Trafficking, in particular of women and children, is "traffic in human beings" and is prohibited by Art. 23(1). The Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 punishes trafficking for prostitution, and **BNS s. 143** punishes trafficking of persons for exploitation (earlier IPC s. 370, as substituted in 2013). The Court has directed rehabilitation of victims and their children. Under Art. 35, only Parliament may prescribe punishment for the offences in Part III.

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-84"></a>
**A-84 · Article 25(1): freedom of conscience and religion**

Subject to **public order, morality and health** and the other provisions of Part III, **all persons** (citizens and non-citizens) are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and the right freely to **profess, practise and propagate** religion. The right is individual, covers belief and practice, and may be restricted on the stated grounds. The word "equally" reflects India's secular State, which treats all religions alike.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-85"></a>
**A-85 · Right to propagate religion**

In *Rev. Stainislaus v. State of M.P.* (1977) the Court held that "propagate" means to transmit or spread one's religion by exposition, and does **not** include a right to convert another person. Forcible or fraudulent conversion would infringe the freedom of conscience of the person converted, so the Orissa and Madhya Pradesh Freedom of Religion Acts, which prohibit conversion by force, fraud or allurement, were upheld.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-86"></a>
**A-86 · Essential religious practices (ERP)**

The Court protects under Arts. 25 and 26 only what is an **essential and integral part** of a religion. *Shirur Mutt* (1954) said that what is essential is decided by the doctrines of that religion itself; *Durgah Committee* (1961) held that superstitious or secular accretions are not protected. Critics say that the courts should not decide theology. *Ismail Faruqui* (1994) and *Sabarimala* (2018) turned on it.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-87"></a>
**A-87 · Article 25(2): State regulation and reform**

Art. 25 does not stop the State from (a) regulating or restricting **economic, financial, political or other secular activity** associated with religious practice, and (b) providing for **social welfare and reform**, or throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. *Venkataramana Devaru* (1958) upheld temple-entry law against a denomination's claim under Art. 26(b).

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-88"></a>
**A-88 · Explanations I and II to Article 25**

Explanation I: the wearing and carrying of **kirpans** is deemed to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion. Explanation II: in Art. 25(2)(b), the reference to **Hindus** includes persons professing the Sikh, Jain or Buddhist religion, and the reference to Hindu religious institutions is construed accordingly. So Art. 25(2)(b) reaches the institutions of all four communities.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-89"></a>
**A-89 · Article 26: freedom to manage religious affairs**

Subject to public order, morality and health, every **religious denomination or any section of it** has the right to (a) establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes; (b) manage its own affairs in matters of religion; (c) own and acquire property; and (d) administer such property **in accordance with law**. *Shirur Mutt* (1954) defined a denomination; *S.P. Mittal* (1983) held that Auroville is not one.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-90"></a>
**A-90 · Article 25 and Article 26 distinguished**

Art. 25 protects **individuals** (all persons) and is subject to public order, morality, health **and** the other provisions of Part III. Art. 26 protects **religious denominations** and is subject only to public order, morality and health. Art. 25 covers belief and practice; Art. 26 covers institutions, management of religious affairs, and property. Art. 25(2) allows State reform; Art. 26(d) subjects administration of property to law.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-91"></a>
**A-91 · Article 27: no tax for promotion of a religion**

No person shall be compelled to pay any **tax** whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination. A tax differs from a **fee**: in *Shirur Mutt* (1954) the contribution levied under the Madras Hindu Religious Endowments Act was held to be a fee for services, so Art. 27 did not apply. *Prafull Goradia* (2011) held that Haj subsidy from general revenue did not offend Art. 27.

*Word count: 75*

<a id="a-92"></a>
**A-92 · Article 28: religious instruction in educational institutions**

(1) No religious instruction in an institution **wholly maintained out of State funds**. (2) This does not apply to an institution administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust requiring it. (3) No person attending a State-recognised or State-aided institution shall be compelled to take part in religious instruction or worship without consent (of the guardian, if a minor). *Aruna Roy* (2002) held that teaching about religions is not religious instruction.

*Word count: 74*

<a id="a-93"></a>
**A-93 · *M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India* (1994)**

The Court upheld the acquisition of the disputed Ayodhya land under the Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act, 1993. It held that a **mosque is not an essential part of the practice of Islam**, so it can be acquired like other property; prayer may be offered anywhere, even in the open. The observation was referred for reconsideration, but a bench declined by a 2:1 majority in 2018, and the Ayodhya judgment followed in 2019.

*Word count: 75*

<a id="a-94"></a>
**A-94 · Triple talaq: *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017)**

By 3:2 the Court set aside instant triple talaq (*talaq-e-biddat*). Nariman and Lalit JJ. held it manifestly arbitrary under Art. 14; Kurian Joseph J. held it not an essential practice and contrary to Islamic law; Khehar CJI and Nazeer J. dissented. The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 makes the practice void and illegal and punishes it with up to three years' imprisonment.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-95"></a>
**A-95 · Sabarimala: *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (2018)**

By 4:1 (Indu Malhotra J. dissenting) the Court struck down Rule 3(b) of the Kerala Hindu Places of Public Worship Rules, which barred women aged 10–50 from the Sabarimala temple, as violating Arts. 14, 15, 17 and 25. On 14 November 2019 the review bench referred wider questions on essential practices to a larger bench; **check whether that reference has been decided**.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-96"></a>
**A-96 · The hijab case**

The Karnataka High Court (15 March 2022, *Resham v. State of Karnataka*) held that wearing the hijab is **not an essential religious practice** and upheld the order enforcing school uniform. In *Aishat Shifa v. State of Karnataka* (13 October 2022) the Supreme Court delivered a **split verdict**: Gupta J. upheld the ban, Dhulia J. set it aside. The matter went to a larger bench; **check its current status**.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-97"></a>
**A-97 · Article 29(1): protection of language, script and culture**

Any **section of citizens** residing in India or any part of it, having a distinct **language, script or culture**, has the right to conserve it. The right is given to citizens, and is not confined to minorities: a section may be a majority in a State. Art. 29(1) has no express restriction, but it does not by itself give a right to State aid for a language.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-98"></a>
**A-98 · Article 29(2): no denial of admission**

No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution **maintained by the State or receiving State aid** on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste or language. In *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) a communal order that reserved medical seats by religion and caste was struck down, which led to the First Amendment and Art. 15(4). It does not cover unaided private institutions.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-99"></a>
**A-99 · Article 30(1): minorities' right to establish and administer institutions**

**All minorities, whether based on religion or language**, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. It protects the right to set up the institution, choose its management and staff, and admit students, subject to reasonable regulation. *St. Stephen's College* (1992) and *T.M.A. Pai* (2002) explain its extent.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-100"></a>
**A-100 · Who is a minority?**

The Constitution does not define "minority". *Re Kerala Education Bill* (1958) treated a community numerically less than 50% as a minority. *T.M.A. Pai* (2002, eleven judges) held that, since States were reorganised on linguistic lines, the **State, not India**, is the unit for deciding both religious and linguistic minorities under Art. 30. Under the NCMEI Act, 2004 the National Commission decides whether an institution is a minority institution.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-101"></a>
**A-101 · Article 30 is not absolute**

The right under Art. 30(1) is subject to **regulatory measures** that secure educational standards, discipline, hygiene, and fair service conditions, so long as they do not destroy the minority character of the institution (*Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College*, 1974, nine judges). A regulation that takes away the management is invalid; one that ensures excellence is valid. Admissions must be fair and transparent in aided institutions.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-102"></a>
**A-102 · *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka* (2002)**

Eleven judges held: (i) the State is the unit for minority status; (ii) education is an "occupation" under Art. 19(1)(g), and private unaided institutions have autonomy in admission and fees, within limits (no capitation fee, no profiteering); (iii) minorities may administer, but aided institutions may be regulated. *P.A. Inamdar* (2005) held that the State may not impose its reservation policy on unaided institutions.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-103"></a>
**A-103 · Aligarh Muslim University and minority status**

*S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India* (1967) held that AMU, incorporated by the AMU Act, 1920, was not "established" by Muslims, so Art. 30 did not apply. In *Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal* (8 November 2024) a seven-judge bench, by 4:3, **overruled** *Azeez Basha*: an institution does not lose minority status because a statute incorporated it. A regular bench must decide AMU's status; **check the outcome**.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-104"></a>
**A-104 · Article 30(1A) and Article 30(2)**

**Art. 30(1A)** (44th Amendment, 1978): where the State compulsorily acquires the property of a minority educational institution, it must ensure that the amount fixed does not restrict or abrogate the right under Art. 30(1). **Art. 30(2)**: the State shall not, in granting aid, discriminate against an institution because it is managed by a minority, whether religious or linguistic.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-105"></a>
**A-105 · Article 29 and Article 30 compared**

Art. 29 gives rights to a **section of citizens** (minority or majority) to conserve language, script or culture, and against denial of admission on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language. Art. 30 gives rights only to **minorities** (religious or linguistic) to establish and administer educational institutions. Art. 29(1) concerns culture; Art. 30(1) concerns institutions. The two overlap where a minority school conserves its language.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-106"></a>
**A-106 · Article 32: right to constitutional remedies**

Art. 32(1) guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings to enforce Part III rights. Dr Ambedkar called it "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it". It is itself a fundamental right: *Prem Chand Garg* (1962) struck down a rule requiring petitioners to deposit security. *Romesh Thappar* (1950) was the first Art. 32 case. Judicial review is part of the basic structure.

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-107"></a>
**A-107 · Scope of Article 32**

Art. 32(2): the Supreme Court may issue **directions, orders or writs**, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, for enforcing Part III rights. Art. 32(3): Parliament may empower other courts to do so. Art. 32(4): the right cannot be suspended except as the Constitution provides (Art. 359). Art. 32 is confined to **fundamental rights**; the Court may mould relief.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-108"></a>
**A-108 · Habeas corpus**

The writ of *habeas corpus* ("you have the body") directs a person detaining another to produce him before the court and justify the detention. Production of the body is not always essential; the court tests the legality of detention (*Kanu Sanyal*, 1973). It is a remedy against unlawful executive and private detention. *ADM Jabalpur* (1976) denied it during the Emergency; that decision has since been disapproved.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-109"></a>
**A-109 · Mandamus**

*Mandamus* ("we command") directs a public authority to perform a **public legal duty** that it has refused to perform. The applicant needs a legal right to compel it. It does not lie against the President or Governors personally, against private individuals (except where they perform public duties), for discretionary duties, or to compel a legislature to make law.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-110"></a>
**A-110 · Certiorari and prohibition**

**Certiorari** quashes an order already made by a court or tribunal that acted without or in excess of jurisdiction, in breach of natural justice, or with an error apparent on the face of the record (*Hari Vishnu Kamath*, 1955; *Syed Yakoob*, 1964). **Prohibition** is issued while proceedings are pending, to stop a lower body from exceeding jurisdiction. Both lie against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies, and *A.K. Kraipak* (1969) extended natural justice to administrative action.

*Word count: 74*

<a id="a-111"></a>
**A-111 · Quo warranto**

*Quo warranto* ("by what authority") requires a person to show by what authority he holds a **public office**. The office must be public, created by statute or the Constitution, and substantive; the holder must lack the qualification or have been appointed contrary to law (*University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao*, 1964). Any interested person may apply, and the court may remove the holder.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-112"></a>
**A-112 · Article 32 and Article 226 compared**

Art. 32 can be invoked **only for fundamental rights**, and is itself a fundamental right; relief is a matter of right. Art. 226 lets High Courts issue writs for fundamental rights **and "any other purpose"**, and relief is discretionary. Art. 226 operates in the court's territory (subject to cause of action, Art. 226(2)). The power under both is part of the basic structure (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-113"></a>
**A-113 · Public interest litigation (PIL)**

PIL allows a public-spirited person to approach the court on behalf of those who cannot, because of poverty or ignorance. The Court relaxed *locus standi* in *S.P. Gupta* (1981); *Hussainara Khatoon* (1979–80) was an early PIL on undertrials; *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984) followed. PIL has produced orders on the environment, prisons and women's safety, but also drawn criticism for judicial overreach.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-114"></a>
**A-114 · Epistolary jurisdiction**

The Court treats a **letter or postcard** as a writ petition on behalf of persons who cannot approach it, so that procedure does not defeat justice. In *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) it acted on a prisoner's letter about torture in jail. Courts now accept such letters only after screening and may appoint an amicus curiae or a commission to investigate.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-115"></a>
**A-115 · Compensation under Article 32**

In *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) the Court awarded compensation to a man held in jail for fourteen years after acquittal, treating it as a public law remedy for breach of Art. 21. *Bhim Singh* (1985) and *Nilabati Behera* (1993, custodial death) followed; sovereign immunity is no defence. The right to sue in tort for damages remains open.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-116"></a>
**A-116 · Continuing mandamus**

In continuing mandamus the court retains jurisdiction and **monitors** implementation of its directions through periodic hearings, instead of deciding once and closing the case. *Vineet Narain v. Union of India* (1998) used it to supervise investigation of the "Jain hawala" case, and *M.C. Mehta* cases to monitor pollution control. Critics see it as the judiciary taking on executive functions.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-117"></a>
**A-117 · Abuse of PIL**

The Court has warned against "publicity interest litigation" and "private interest litigation" (*Ashok Kumar Pandey*, 2004; *Balco Employees' Union*, 2002). *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010) laid down guidelines: verify the petitioner's credentials and bona fides, ensure real public interest, avoid parallel proceedings, and impose **exemplary costs** on frivolous petitions.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-118"></a>
**A-118 · Nature of the Directive Principles (Art. 37)**

Part IV (Arts. 36–51) lists directions that guide the State in governing. Art. 37 says that they are **not enforceable by any court**, but are **fundamental in the governance of the country** and the State **shall apply** them in making laws. The idea comes from the Irish Constitution. They aim at a welfare State and social and economic democracy.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-119"></a>
**A-119 · Classification of DPSPs**

Commentators group them as **socialistic** (Arts. 38, 39, 41–43A: livelihood, equal pay, work, workers' participation), **Gandhian** (Arts. 40, 43, 43B, 46, 47, 48: panchayats, cottage industries, cooperatives, weaker sections, prohibition, cattle) and **liberal-intellectual** (Arts. 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50, 51: uniform civil code, early childhood care, environment, monuments, separation of judiciary, peace). The Constitution itself does not classify them.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-120"></a>
**A-120 · Article 39(b) and (c)**

Art. 39(b): the ownership and control of the **material resources of the community** should be distributed to subserve the common good. Art. 39(c): the economic system should not result in concentration of wealth. In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024, nine judges) the majority held that not every privately owned resource is a "material resource of the community"; the matter needs a case-by-case test.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-121"></a>
**A-121 · Article 40: village panchayats**

The State shall organise **village panchayats** and endow them with the powers and authority needed to function as units of self-government. It was a Directive Principle until the **73rd Amendment (1992)** inserted Part IX (Arts. 243–243O) and gave panchayats constitutional status, with three tiers, regular elections, reservation for SCs, STs and women, and State Finance Commissions.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-122"></a>
**A-122 · Article 44: Uniform Civil Code**

The State shall **endeavour to secure a uniform civil code** for citizens throughout India. The Court urged it in *Shah Bano* (1985), *Sarla Mudgal* (1995) and *John Vallamattom* (2003). Goa has a common civil code from Portuguese times, and Uttarakhand enacted a UCC in 2024 (in force January 2025). Opponents cite Art. 25 and the diversity of personal laws; **check State laws before the exam**.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-123"></a>
**A-123 · Article 45 and early childhood care**

Art. 45 originally directed free and compulsory education for children up to fourteen within ten years. The **86th Amendment (2002)** inserted Art. 21A (education for ages 6–14 as a fundamental right) and substituted Art. 45: the State shall **endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete six years**. Art. 51A(k) added a parental duty.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-124"></a>
**A-124 · Articles 41 to 43A: social and economic rights**

Art. 41: right to work, education and public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement (within economic capacity). Art. 42: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief. Art. 43: a living wage and decent standard of life. Art. 43A (42nd Amendment): workers' participation in the management of industries. Art. 43B (97th Amendment) concerns cooperative societies.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-125"></a>
**A-125 · Article 48: cattle preservation**

Art. 48 directs the State to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern lines and to prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle. *Mohd. Hanif Quareshi* (1958) upheld a ban on cows and calves but not on useless cattle. *State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat* (2005, seven judges) upheld a ban on slaughter of all cow progeny.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-126"></a>
**A-126 · Article 50: separation of the judiciary from the executive**

The State shall take steps to **separate the judiciary from the executive** in the public services of the State. It aims to keep the independence of the subordinate judiciary and it was implemented by the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which separated the magistracy into judicial and executive magistrates. Art. 50 guides legislation but is not enforceable.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-127"></a>
**A-127 · Article 51: international peace and security**

The State shall endeavour to (a) promote international peace and security; (b) maintain just and honourable relations between nations; (c) foster respect for **international law and treaty obligations**; and (d) encourage settlement of disputes by arbitration. In *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) the Court used Art. 51(c) and CEDAW to frame guidelines on sexual harassment at work.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-128"></a>
**A-128 · *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951)**

The Court held that Directive Principles, being non-justiciable, **cannot override Fundamental Rights**: the chapter on Fundamental Rights is sacrosanct, and Directive Principles must conform to it and run as subsidiary to it. A communal order reserving seats violated Art. 29(2). Parliament responded with the First Amendment (1951), which added Art. 15(4).

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-129"></a>
**A-129 · Harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles**

*Champakam* (1951) gave primacy to Fundamental Rights. *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) treated the two as complementary. *Minerva Mills* (1980) held that the Constitution rests on the **balance** between Parts III and IV; giving absolute primacy to either destroys it. Art. 31C survives only in its original form, for laws giving effect to Art. 39(b) and (c).

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-130"></a>
**A-130 · Importance of the Directive Principles**

They set the goals of a welfare State; they are a yardstick for judging a government's performance; the courts treat them as aids to interpret statutes and Fundamental Rights; and they have been read into Art. 21 (free legal aid under Art. 39A, education under Arts. 41 and 45, environment under Art. 48A). Many welfare laws, for instance minimum wages and land reform, trace back to them.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-131"></a>
**A-131 · Criticism of the Directive Principles**

Critics say that they are **non-justiciable**, so governments can ignore them; they are vague and not ranked in any order of priority; some conflict with Fundamental Rights; and they need money that the Constitution does not guarantee. Supporters answer that they are a moral and political guide, that people judge governments by them, and that courts have given many of them force through Art. 21.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-132"></a>
**A-132 · Origin of the Fundamental Duties**

The Constitution had no duties at first. During the Emergency, the **Swaran Singh Committee (1976)** recommended them, and the **42nd Amendment (1976)** inserted **Part IVA, Art. 51A**, with ten duties. The **86th Amendment (2002)** added clause (k), so there are now **eleven**. Commentators link the idea to other constitutions, such as that of the former USSR.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-133"></a>
**A-133 · Verma Committee (1999)**

The Justice J.S. Verma Committee on Fundamental Duties of Citizens listed existing laws that give effect to some duties, for example the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971, the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955, the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 and the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980. It recommended wider teaching of duties and effective enforcement of those laws.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-134"></a>
**A-134 · Nature of the Fundamental Duties**

The duties are addressed to **citizens**, are **not justiciable**, and carry **no direct sanction**; Parliament may make laws to enforce them. Courts use them as a guide to interpret statutes and to uphold reasonable restrictions on rights (*Rural Litigation Kendra*, 1985; *AIIMS Students' Union*, 2002). They are read together with the Directive Principles.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-135"></a>
**A-135 · Article 51A(k): duty of parents and guardians**

Art. 51A(k), inserted by the **86th Amendment (2002)**, makes it the duty of a parent or guardian to **provide opportunities for education to a child between six and fourteen years**. It complements Art. 21A and the RTE Act, 2009. It is the only duty that is addressed to a particular class (parents or guardians) rather than to every citizen.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-136"></a>
**A-136 · *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986)**

Three children of Jehovah's Witnesses were expelled from school for refusing to sing the National Anthem, though they stood respectfully. The Court held that no law compelled singing, and the expulsion violated their freedom of speech (Art. 19(1)(a)) and of conscience (Art. 25). Respectful standing satisfies Art. 51A(a), and the 1971 Act does not compel singing.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-137"></a>
**A-137 · Laws to protect national honour**

The **Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971** punishes insult to the National Flag, the Constitution and the National Anthem. The Flag Code of India governs display of the flag; *Union of India v. Naveen Jindal* (2004) held that flying the national flag is a right under Art. 19(1)(a), subject to reasonable restrictions. In *Shyam Narayan Chouksey* (2018) the Court made playing the anthem in cinemas optional.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-138"></a>
**A-138 · Duties with a social purpose**

Art. 51A requires citizens to renounce practices derogatory to the **dignity of women** (e), to value and preserve India's **composite culture** (f), to protect the **environment** and have compassion for living creatures (g), to develop **scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry** (h), to safeguard **public property** and abjure violence (i), and to strive for excellence (j).

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-139"></a>
**A-139 · Fundamental Rights and Fundamental Duties**

Duties are the counterpart of rights: rights depend on citizens respecting the Constitution, the nation and each other. Failure to perform a duty does not take away a right, and a citizen cannot be punished under Art. 51A itself; only a valid law can punish. The duties may help justify reasonable restrictions on rights, but they are not conditions of enjoying them.

*Word count: 62*
