# Paper II · Leading Cases Digest (facts · issues · holding · principle · what happened next)

> **Last updated:** 2026-10-28 (IST) · **Law stated as on:** 2026-10-28 · **Branch:** `paper/02-constitutional-law`
> **What this is.** A full treatment of the cases that the model answers rely on, grouped by unit. Each entry gives the **material facts**, the **issue(s)**, the **holding**, the **principle** to quote, and **subsequent developments**, plus the answers that use it. Use it to write a case in 2 lines (4 marks), 4–5 lines (10 marks) or a short paragraph (20 marks).
> **How certain is each citation?** Case name and year are reliable. A citation marked **†** was written from memory and has **not** been checked against a law report; the [source-verification log](source-verification-log.md) says what has and has not been checked. In the exam, write the name and year only if you are unsure of the citation.
> **Index of "used in".** `A-nn` = [Section A](section-a-part-1.md), `B-nn` = [Section B](section-b-part-1.md), `C-nn` = [Section C](section-c-part-1-units-1-5.md). Unit numbers refer to the unit files one level up.
> **Not a substitute for the report.** Short forms such as "Bhagwati J." identify the judge who wrote the passage; opinions in constitution benches are often separate.

## Units 1–2 · The Constitution, federalism and the Preamble

### 1. *Re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves* AIR 1960 SC 845 · Art. 143 reference
- **Facts.** Under the 1958 Nehru–Noon Agreement with Pakistan, half of the Berubari Union (West Bengal) was to be transferred. The President referred questions to the Supreme Court under Art. 143.
- **Issues.** Can Parliament give effect to the agreement by a law under Art. 3, or is a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 needed? Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?
- **Held.** Art. 3 does not authorise cession of Indian territory to a foreign State; an amendment under Art. 368 was necessary. The Preamble is a key to the minds of the makers, but is **not a part** of the Constitution and is not a source of power.
- **Principle.** Preamble as an aid to interpretation only (on this view).
- **After.** Parliament passed the Ninth Amendment (1960). The Preamble holding was **departed from in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** and *LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995) 5 SCC 482 †, which treat the Preamble as part of the Constitution.
- **Used in:** C-02, B-14, A-24.

### 2. *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) 4 SCC 225 · 13 judges (7:6)
- **Facts.** The head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala challenged the Kerala Land Reforms Act, 1963 (as amended in 1969) which limited the mutt's land. During the hearing the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments were also challenged. They had removed Part III restrictions on amendments (24th), curtailed the right to compensation and judicial review of laws made for Directive Principles (25th), and placed Kerala land laws in the Ninth Schedule (29th).
- **Issues.** Does Parliament's power to amend under Art. 368 include power to abridge or take away Fundamental Rights? Is it unlimited?
- **Held.** *Golaknath* (1967) was overruled. Parliament may amend **any** provision, including Part III, but **cannot alter or destroy the basic structure or framework** of the Constitution. The 24th Amendment was valid; the second part of Art. 31C (barring judicial review) was struck down; the first part was upheld. Basic features listed by various judges include supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, sovereignty and unity of India, the mandate to build a welfare State, individual freedom and dignity, and judicial review.
- **Principle.** Amending power is limited by the basic structure. Different judges listed different features, and the list is open.
- **After.** *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) (39th Amendment); *Minerva Mills* (1980); *Waman Rao* (1981) (the doctrine applies to amendments after 24 April 1973); *I.R. Coelho* (2007); *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn. v. Union of India* (2016) 5 SCC 1 † (NJAC and the 99th Amendment struck down). The Court has applied it only to amendments and, in some cases, to ordinary legislation on limited grounds.
- **Used in:** C-05, C-23, B-19, B-46, A-33.

### 3. *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974) 2 SCC 831 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** A Punjab judicial officer was discharged from service. The action was not taken by the Governor personally, but through the Council of Ministers; the officer said that the Governor had to exercise the power himself under Arts. 233–235 and 311.
- **Issue.** Does the President/Governor exercise powers personally or on the advice of the ministers?
- **Held.** The President and Governors are **constitutional heads**. Executive powers are exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, not personally, except where the Constitution gives a discretion (for example, the choice of Prime Minister when no party has a clear majority). The Government's action was valid.
- **Principle.** Parliamentary system: real power rests with the Council of Ministers responsible to the legislature. This follows *Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* AIR 1955 SC 549.
- **After.** Art. 74(1) (42nd and 44th Amendments) says the President shall act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers but may require reconsideration once. *Nabam Rebia* (2016) 8 SCC 1 † restricted a Governor's discretion. Governors' assent to Bills was considered in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025) † and the Presidential Reference on the subject (opinion dated 20 November 2025) †; both need checking before you rely on them.
- **Used in:** A-22, B-11, C-01.

### 4. *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) 3 SCC 1 · 9 judges
- **Facts.** In 1989 the Karnataka Governor reported that Chief Minister S.R. Bommai's government had lost its majority after defections, and the President dismissed it under Art. 356 without allowing a floor test. Proclamations in Meghalaya and Nagaland were heard with it. After the Babri Masjid demolition (December 1992), the BJP governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh were dismissed.
- **Issues.** Is a proclamation under Art. 356 open to judicial review? What are the limits of Art. 356? What is the place of secularism?
- **Held.** A proclamation is **justiciable**: the Court may examine whether there was any material, whether it was relevant, and whether the proclamation was mala fide. Majority is tested **on the floor of the House**, not by the Governor's opinion. Parliament must approve the proclamation; until it does, the Assembly cannot be dissolved, and the Court may restore a dismissed government if the proclamation is invalid. **Federalism and secularism are basic features.** The Karnataka dismissal was unconstitutional; the dismissals of the BJP governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh after the demolition were upheld on the ground of secularism **(check the position on Meghalaya and Nagaland in the report)**.
- **Principle.** Art. 356 is an exceptional power; the States are not mere appendages of the Centre.
- **After.** Applied in *Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006) 2 SCC 1 † (Bihar Assembly dissolved in 2005). Use of Art. 356 declined sharply after the decision.
- **Used in:** C-01, B-13, B-15, A-08, A-14.

### 5. *Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (2018) 8 SCC 501 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The elected Delhi Government and the Lieutenant Governor (LG) disputed who had the final say in administration after the Delhi High Court (2016) held that the LG had primacy.
- **Issue.** What is the effect of Art. 239AA on the powers of the LG?
- **Held.** The LG has **no independent decision-making power** in matters on which the Delhi Assembly may legislate. He must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except for matters outside the Assembly's power (police, public order and land). Differences go to the President, but this must not be used routinely. The Court spoke of **collaborative federalism** and constitutional morality.
- **After.** *GNCTD v. Union of India* (2023) 9 SCC 1 † (11 May 2023) held that the elected Government controls "services" within its field. Parliament then enacted the Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023; a challenge to that Act was referred to a larger bench **(check status)**.
- **Used in:** A-18, C-01.

### 6. *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) Supp SCC 1 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The Allahabad High Court (12 June 1975) set aside Mrs Gandhi's election for corrupt practice. The 39th Amendment inserted Art. 329A, removing the Prime Minister's election from the courts' reach and validating it.
- **Held.** Art. 329A(4) and (5) were struck down as violating the basic structure: free and fair elections, rule of law and judicial review (the separation of powers). Her election was upheld on the merits by applying the ordinary election law.
- **Principle.** Parliament cannot, by amendment, exercise a judicial function to validate a particular election.
- **Used in:** C-05, B-19.

### 7. *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (1980) 3 SCC 625 · 5 judges (4:1)
- **Facts.** The Government took over Minerva Mills under the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974. The owners challenged ss. 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment, which had extended Art. 31C to **all** Directive Principles and inserted Art. 368(4) and (5), barring judicial review of amendments.
- **Held.** Section 55 (Art. 368(4), (5)) was struck down: limited amending power is a basic feature, and Parliament cannot enlarge it. Section 4 (extension of Art. 31C to all Directive Principles) was struck down: the Constitution rests on the **balance** between Parts III and IV. Bhagwati J. dissented.
- **Principle.** "Harmony and balance" between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is basic; the original Art. 31C (for Art. 39(b) and (c)) survives.
- **After.** *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal* (1983) 1 SCC 147 upheld the surviving Art. 31C. *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024) held that Art. 31C remains in the Constitution in its original form.
- **Used in:** C-05, C-23, B-46, A-35, A-129.

### 8. *Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024) †
- **Facts.** Petitions (including by Dr Subramanian Swamy) argued that the 42nd Amendment could not add "socialist" and "secular" to the Preamble because the Preamble's adoption date (26 November 1949) cannot be altered.
- **Held.** The petitions were dismissed: the Preamble can be amended under Art. 368 (*Kesavananda*), and the words "socialist" and "secular" express values already in the Constitution.
- **Principle.** Amendability of the Preamble; secularism and socialism as constitutional values.
- **Check:** citation, bench and the exact reasoning before quoting it in detail.
- **Used in:** A-01, A-28, B-14.

## Unit 3 · Fundamental Rights: general

### 9. *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib Sehravardi* (1981) 1 SCC 722 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The Regional Engineering College, Srinagar, was a society registered under the J&K Societies Registration Act, funded and controlled by the Central and State Governments. A candidate who was refused admission said that the oral-interview marks used for selection were arbitrary and that the society was "State".
- **Issues.** Is the society an "authority" under Art. 12? Was the selection procedure valid?
- **Held.** The society was an **instrumentality or agency of the State** (State funding, deep and pervasive State control, governmental functions, management by State nominees). Its action had to satisfy Art. 14, and the allocation of a large share of marks to the interview was arbitrary. Bhagwati J. also held that the State cannot escape Part III by acting through a corporation.
- **Principle.** The *Ajay Hasia* tests of instrumentality; **non-arbitrariness** as part of Art. 14. Builds on *R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority* (1979) 3 SCC 489.
- **After.** *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology* (2002) 5 SCC 111 (7 judges) restated the test: whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by, or under the control of, the Government. *Zee Telefilms v. Union of India* (2005) 4 SCC 649 † held the BCCI not "State" but amenable to a writ for public duties.
- **Used in:** A-03, A-29, B-16, C-03.

### 10. *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 27 · 6 judges
- **Facts.** A Communist leader was detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950.
- **Held.** Art. 21's "procedure established by law" means procedure laid down by a valid, enacted law, not natural justice. Arts. 19, 21 and 22 are mutually **exclusive codes**; a preventive detention law did not have to meet Art. 19.
- **After.** *R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970) 1 SCC 248 † (Bank Nationalisation) held that the effect of a law on the right must be tested under all relevant Articles. *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) overruled the narrow view.
- **Used in:** A-64, A-65, C-13.

### 11. *Golaknath v. State of Punjab* AIR 1967 SC 1643 · 11 judges (6:5)
- **Facts.** The Golaknath family held over 500 acres of land in Punjab. The Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953 declared much of it surplus. It had been placed in the Ninth Schedule by the 17th Amendment (1964).
- **Issue.** Is a constitutional amendment a "law" under Art. 13(2)? Can Parliament abridge Part III?
- **Held.** Amendment is "law" under Art. 13(2); Parliament **cannot** abridge or take away Fundamental Rights. The Court overruled *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965), but applied **prospective overruling** so the earlier amendments stayed valid.
- **After.** Parliament passed the 24th Amendment (1971) to reverse it. *Kesavananda* (1973) overruled *Golaknath*.
- **Used in:** C-05, B-19, A-33.

### 12. *Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981) 2 SCC 362 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Challenge to agrarian-reform laws placed in the Ninth Schedule and to the Art. 31B protection.
- **Held.** *Kesavananda* applies **prospectively**: amendments before 24 April 1973 (the date of *Kesavananda*) are valid, but those after that date can be challenged for violating the basic structure.
- **After.** *I.R. Coelho* (below).
- **Used in:** A-34, C-05.

### 13. *I.R. Coelho (dead) v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007) 2 SCC 1 · 9 judges
- **Facts.** Challenge to laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973.
- **Held.** Placing a law in the Ninth Schedule does not give it **blanket immunity**. A law placed there after 24 April 1973 can be challenged if it damages or destroys the basic structure or violates Part III rights read with Arts. 14, 19 and 21 (the "rights test" and "effects test").
- **Principle.** Judicial review is part of the basic structure; the Ninth Schedule cannot be used to take away review.
- **After.** Used in challenges to State reservation laws (such as Tamil Nadu's 69%).
- **Used in:** A-34, C-05, B-19.

### 14. *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.* AIR 1955 SC 781 · eclipse
- **Facts.** The C.P. and Berar Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947, which authorised nationalised transport, was inconsistent with Art. 19(1)(g) when the Constitution began. The First Amendment (1951) later widened Art. 19(6).
- **Held.** A pre-Constitution law that conflicts with a Fundamental Right is not void for all purposes; it is **overshadowed (eclipsed)** and remains on the statute book. When the obstacle is removed by an amendment, the law revives and operates from that date.
- **Principle.** Doctrine of eclipse. *Deep Chand v. State of U.P.* AIR 1959 SC 648 † treated post-Constitution laws as void from the start; *State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills* (1974) 4 SCC 656 † applied the idea where the right was available only to citizens.
- **Used in:** A-30, B-17, C-04.

### 15. *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. Union of India* AIR 1957 SC 628 · severability
- **Facts.** The Prize Competitions Act, 1955 regulated gambling and non-gambling competitions. The petitioners ran a competition that was not gambling.
- **Held.** If the valid part of a law can be separated from the invalid part and the legislature would have enacted the valid part alone, only the invalid part is void. The Court examined the object of the Act and held it valid for gambling competitions.
- **After.** *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* 1992 Supp (2) SCC 651 severed para 7 of the Tenth Schedule and upheld the rest.
- **Used in:** A-31, B-17, C-04.

### 16. *Basheshar Nath v. CIT* AIR 1959 SC 149 · waiver †
- **Held.** A person cannot waive a Fundamental Right designed for the public benefit (here, equal protection under Art. 14) by agreement. *Olga Tellis* (1985) applied the same view (no estoppel against Fundamental Rights).
- **Check** the number of judges and the split before citing it in detail.
- **Used in:** A-32.

### 17. *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) 2 SCC 521 · 5 judges (4:1)
- **Facts.** During the Emergency (1975), the President suspended the right to move courts for enforcing Arts. 14, 21 and 22 (Art. 359). Persons detained under MISA applied for habeas corpus.
- **Held (majority).** While the order was in force, no one could challenge detention in court, even for mala fides or illegality. **Khanna J. dissented**: Art. 21 is not the only source of the right to life and liberty, and the rule of law continues even in an Emergency.
- **After.** The 44th Amendment (1978) provided that Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended under Art. 359. *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) disapproved the majority view as wrong.
- **Used in:** A-05, A-67, B-18, C-03.

## Unit 4 · Article 14

### 18. *State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar* AIR 1952 SC 75 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** The West Bengal Special Courts Act, 1950 let the State Government send "offences or classes of offences or cases" to special courts with a summary procedure.
- **Held.** The Act violated Art. 14: it did not give a principle on which cases would be chosen for the special procedure, so it allowed arbitrary discrimination.
- **After.** *Kathi Raning Rawat* (1952) distinguished a differently drawn law. *Budhan Choudhry v. State of Bihar* AIR 1955 SC 191 † set the two-fold test (intelligible differentia and rational nexus); *Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Tendolkar* AIR 1958 SC 538 † added the presumption of constitutionality.
- **Used in:** A-39, B-22, C-06.

### 19. *E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1974) 4 SCC 3 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Royappa, a senior IAS officer who was Chief Secretary, was moved to a newly created post and alleged mala fide.
- **Held.** The transfer was valid on the facts. Bhagwati J. said that equality is a **dynamic concept** that cannot be "cribbed, cabined and confined" within traditional limits; arbitrariness is the opposite of equality and violates Art. 14.
- **Principle.** Non-arbitrariness as a facet of Art. 14. Applied in *Maneka Gandhi* and *Ajay Hasia*. *State of A.P. v. McDowell & Co.* (1996) 3 SCC 709 cautioned against striking down legislation merely as arbitrary; *Shayara Bano* (2017) revived "manifest arbitrariness".
- **Used in:** A-40, B-22, C-06.

### 20. *D.S. Nakara v. Union of India* (1983) 1 SCC 305 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** A pension scheme gave a more liberal formula only to those who retired after 31 March 1979. Pensioners who retired earlier asked for the same.
- **Held.** Dividing pensioners by retirement date was arbitrary and violated Art. 14. Pension is not a bounty but deferred wages, a right to property. The cut-off date was struck down and the benefit was extended to all.
- **Principle.** Classification must have a nexus with the object; pensioners form a single class.
- **Used in:** A-39, C-06.

### 21. *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) 9 SCC 1 · 5 judges (3:2)
- **Facts.** Shayara Bano was divorced by *talaq-e-biddat* (instant triple talaq) delivered by letter. She challenged it, and also polygamy and *nikah halala*.
- **Issues.** Is triple talaq an essential part of Islam protected by Art. 25? Are personal laws subject to Part III? Is s. 2 of the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 void insofar as it recognises it?
- **Held (3:2).** Instant triple talaq was set aside. Nariman and Lalit JJ.: s. 2 of the 1937 Act is void to the extent that it recognises it, because it is **manifestly arbitrary** under Art. 14. Kurian Joseph J.: it is not an essential part of Islam and is contrary to the Quran. Khehar CJI and Nazeer J. dissented: personal law cannot be tested under Part III, but they asked Parliament to legislate.
- **After.** Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 made it void and punishable. Polygamy and *nikah halala* were left open.
- **Used in:** A-94, B-36, B-45, C-19.

### 22. *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 · 5 judges (unanimous)
- **Facts.** Petitions challenged s. 377 IPC as it applied to consensual sex between adults. The Court had upheld the section in *Suresh Kumar Koushal* (2013), reversing the Delhi High Court in *Naz Foundation* (2009).
- **Held.** Section 377 is unconstitutional **insofar as it criminalises consensual sexual conduct between adults in private** (Arts. 14, 15, 19 and 21). *Koushal* was overruled. Sexual orientation is part of identity and dignity; **constitutional morality** prevails over social morality.
- **After.** The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 has no equivalent of s. 377. In *Supriyo v. Union of India* (17 October 2023) † the Court (5 judges) declined to recognise a right to marry for same-sex couples and left the question to Parliament; review petitions were dismissed on 9 January 2025.
- **Used in:** A-43, B-22, C-06.

### 23. *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2019) 3 SCC 39 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** A petition challenged s. 497 IPC (adultery), under which only a man could be punished, and a woman's husband could prosecute, along with s. 198(2) CrPC.
- **Held.** Section 497 and s. 198 CrPC were struck down as violating Arts. 14, 15 and 21: they treat a wife as the husband's property and deny her autonomy and dignity. Art. 15(3) cannot be used to uphold such a law. *Sowmithri Vishnu* (1985) and similar decisions were overruled. Adultery remains a ground for divorce.
- **After.** The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 omitted adultery.
- **Note.** Earlier unit files cite this case as (2018) 2 SCC 189; the correct citation is (2019) 3 SCC 39 (the case was decided on 27 September 2018).
- **Used in:** A-43, B-23, C-06.

## Unit 5 · Articles 15–18

### 24. *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* AIR 1951 SC 226 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** A Madras Government Order (the "Communal G.O.") allotted seats in medical and engineering colleges by community. A Brahmin candidate was refused admission despite higher marks.
- **Held.** The order violated Art. 29(2) (no denial of admission on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language). Directive Principles (Art. 46) **cannot override** Fundamental Rights; they must run as subsidiary to them.
- **After.** The First Amendment (1951) added Art. 15(4). *Kesavananda* and *Minerva Mills* changed the relationship between Parts III and IV.
- **Used in:** A-98, A-128, C-23.

### 25. *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217 · 9 judges
- **Facts.** The Government accepted the Mandal Commission's report (Office Memorandum of 13 August 1990, 27% reservation for socially and educationally backward classes) and added 10% for economically weaker sections (25 September 1991).
- **Held (on the main points).** The 27% reservation is valid. The **creamy layer** must be excluded. Backwardness under Art. 16(4) is identified mainly, but not only, by caste. **Reservation under Art. 16(4) cannot be made in promotions**. Total reservation should **not exceed 50%** except in extraordinary situations. The 10% reservation on economic criteria alone was invalid. A permanent commission should examine inclusion and exclusion.
- **After.** The 77th Amendment (1995) added Art. 16(4A) (promotion for SC/ST), the 81st added 16(4B) (carry forward), and the 85th added consequential seniority. *M. Nagaraj v. Union of India* (2006) 8 SCC 212 † upheld them subject to quantifiable data on backwardness, inadequacy of representation and efficiency. *Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta* (2018) 10 SCC 396 † dispensed with proof of backwardness for SC/ST but kept the creamy-layer exclusion. *Jaishri Patil* (2021) 8 SCC 1 † (Maratha quota) refused to reopen *Indra Sawhney*. The 103rd Amendment (EWS) was upheld in *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022).
- **Used in:** A-45, A-46, A-48, B-24, C-07.

### 26. *Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (2023) 5 SCC 1 † · 5 judges (3:2) · judgment of 7 November 2022
- **Facts.** The 103rd Amendment (2019) inserted Arts. 15(6) and 16(6), permitting up to 10% reservation for economically weaker sections other than those covered by the existing reservations.
- **Held (3:2).** Maheshwari, Trivedi and Pardiwala JJ. upheld the amendment: economic criteria are a permissible basis; excluding SC, ST and OBC (who already have reservations) is not discriminatory; the 50% ceiling is not inflexible and is a rule for the existing reservation categories. Bhat J. (with Lalit CJI) dissented: exclusion of the poorest SC/ST/OBC people from a benefit given on the basis of poverty violates the equality code and is an attack on the basic structure.
- **After.** A review petition was dismissed on 9 May 2023 by a five-judge bench led by Chandrachud CJI. Several State laws now exceed 50% and are being challenged (including the Tamil Nadu 69% quota, protected by the Ninth Schedule).
- **Used in:** A-47, A-48, C-07.

### 27. *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024) · 2024 INSC 562 · 7 judges (6:1)
- **Facts.** A Punjab notification of 1975 reserved half of the Scheduled Caste seats for Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs. The Punjab Scheduled Castes and Backward Classes (Reservation in Services) Act, 2006, s. 4(5) re-enacted this. The Punjab and Haryana High Court struck it down on the strength of *E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of A.P.* (2005) 1 SCC 394.
- **Issue.** May States sub-classify Scheduled Castes for reservation?
- **Held (6:1; Trivedi J. dissenting).** Yes. SCs are not a homogeneous class for this purpose. A State may sub-classify if it has **quantifiable, empirical data** on the relative backwardness of the sub-group and inadequate representation in services; it must not act arbitrarily or for political gain. *E.V. Chinnaiah* was overruled. The President's Art. 341 list identifies the castes; it does not stop the State from distributing benefits among them.
- **Creamy layer.** In separate opinions, Gavai, Vikram Nath, Pankaj Mithal and S.C. Sharma JJ. said that the State should evolve a policy to identify the creamy layer among SC/ST and exclude it. That view was not part of the order, and the Union Government has said that it does not intend to apply it **(check the latest position)**.
- **Used in:** A-49, B-24, C-07.

### 28. *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Bhanwari Devi, a social worker in Rajasthan, was gang-raped in 1992 after trying to stop a child marriage. Women's groups filed a PIL under Art. 32 asking for protection against sexual harassment at work, for which there was no law.
- **Held.** Sexual harassment violates Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. In the absence of legislation the Court laid down the **Vishaka Guidelines**, relying on CEDAW through Art. 51(c) and Art. 253, to be followed until Parliament legislated.
- **After.** The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013. *Apparel Export Promotion Council v. A.K. Chopra* (1999) 1 SCC 759 † applied the guidelines.
- **Used in:** A-127, B-23, B-47.

### 29. *Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India* (1996) 1 SCC 361 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Challenge to the National Awards (Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri) as "titles" prohibited by Art. 18(1).
- **Held.** The awards are **not titles**; they honour merit and do not create a hereditary or social status. They must not be used as a prefix or suffix, and an award used in that way may be forfeited.
- **Used in:** A-51, B-25.

## Unit 6 · Article 19(1)(a): speech and expression

### 30. *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 124 · 6 judges
- **Facts.** The Madras Maintenance of Public Order Act, 1949 allowed the State to ban the entry or circulation of a publication to maintain public safety. It was used to ban *Cross Roads*, a journal printed in Bombay and critical of the Government's policy. Its editor Romesh Thappar petitioned directly under Art. 32 (one of the first Art. 32 cases).
- **Held.** The freedom of speech includes the freedom to **circulate** publications. The restriction was not within Art. 19(2), which then allowed limits only for "the security of the State" and so on, and not for "public order"; the section was therefore void. *Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* AIR 1950 SC 129 struck down pre-censorship of the *Organiser* on the same reasoning.
- **After.** The **First Amendment (1951)** re-wrote Art. 19(2) to add "public order", "friendly relations with foreign States" and "incitement to an offence", and made the restrictions "reasonable".
- **Used in:** A-53, A-54, B-26, C-08.

### 31. *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* AIR 1962 SC 955 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Kedar Nath Singh, a Forward Bloc member, was prosecuted for sedition under s. 124A IPC for a speech attacking the Congress Government.
- **Held.** Section 124A is valid, but only if read down: it punishes words or acts that have the **intention or tendency to create disorder or incite violence**. Criticism of the Government that does not do so is not sedition.
- **After.** In *S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India* (11 May 2022) † the Court kept s. 124A in abeyance. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 omits "sedition" but s. 152 punishes acts "endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"; challenges to s. 152 **need checking**.
- **Used in:** A-53, B-27, C-08.

### 32. *Dr Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar* AIR 1966 SC 740 · 5 judges †
- **Facts.** Lohia was detained under the Defence of India Rules, 1962 as prejudicial to public order.
- **Held.** The detention was invalid. Three concentric circles: **law and order** (widest), **public order**, and **security of the State** (narrowest). Only an act that affects the community or public at large, and not merely individuals, disturbs public order.
- **After.** *Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram* (1989) 2 SCC 574 † required a **proximate** link to public disorder, "like a spark in a powder keg".
- **Used in:** A-53, B-26.

### 33. *Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India* (1972) 2 SCC 788 · and *Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India* (1985) 1 SCC 641
- **Facts.** The Newsprint Control Policy fixed the page limit and prevented newspapers from increasing pages by cutting circulation (*Bennett Coleman*). In *Indian Express* the challenge was to customs duty on newsprint.
- **Held.** Freedom of the press is part of Art. 19(1)(a). A page limit is a restriction on circulation and content and fails Art. 19(2) (*Bennett Coleman*). A tax on newsprint is not immune from challenge and must not be used to curb the press (*Indian Express*; **check the exact order in the report**).
- **Used in:** B-28, C-09.

### 34. *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) 5 SCC 1 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** Two young women in Maharashtra were arrested in November 2012 for a Facebook post questioning the shutdown of Mumbai after Bal Thackeray's death. They were charged under s. 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 (offensive, annoying or inconvenient messages). Shreya Singhal, a law student, filed a PIL.
- **Held.** Section 66A was **struck down** as vague, overbroad and with a chilling effect on speech, not saved by Art. 19(2). The Court distinguished **discussion, advocacy and incitement**. Section 79 and the Intermediary Rules were **read down** (intermediaries must act only on a court order or Government notification). Section 69A (blocking) was upheld with safeguards.
- **After.** In *PUCL v. Union of India* (2022) † the Court noted that s. 66A was still being invoked and directed that no cases be registered under it.
- **Used in:** A-60, B-26, C-08.

### 35. *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** After the revocation of Art. 370 (August 2019), the Jammu and Kashmir administration shut down internet and telecom services and imposed s. 144 CrPC restrictions. The executive editor of *Kashmir Times* challenged them.
- **Held.** Freedom of speech and expression over the internet and the freedom of trade through it are protected by Arts. 19(1)(a) and (g). **Indefinite suspension** of the internet is not allowed. Suspension orders under the Telecom Suspension Rules, 2017 must be published, must be **necessary and proportionate**, temporary, and are open to judicial review. Section 144 cannot be used to suppress legitimate expression.
- **Used in:** A-56, B-26, C-08.

### 36. *Tata Press Ltd. v. Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd.* (1995) 5 SCC 139
- **Facts.** Tata Press published the *Tata Yellow Pages*. MTNL, which had the right to publish telephone directories, said that the advertisements breached its rights.
- **Held.** **Commercial speech** (advertising) is protected by Art. 19(1)(a), as the public has a right to receive information. Protection is lost if the advertisement is deceptive, unfair, misleading or untrue.
- **Used in:** A-55, C-08.

### 37. *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) 7 SCC 221 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** Petitions by Subramanian Swamy, Rahul Gandhi, Arvind Kejriwal and others challenged ss. 499–500 IPC and ss. 199(1)–(4) CrPC (criminal defamation).
- **Held.** The provisions are valid. Reputation is part of Art. 21 and of the right of fraternity; defamation as a ground in Art. 19(2) is a reasonable restriction, and the right to speech cannot be used to damage another's reputation.
- **After.** Replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, s. 356 (which also provides community service as a punishment).
- **Used in:** A-59, C-10.

### 38. *Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal* (2014) 4 SCC 257 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** A magazine reproduced a photograph of Boris Becker, a tennis star, with his fiancée Barbara Feltus, naked, in support of an anti-racism message. A criminal complaint was filed for obscenity under s. 292 IPC.
- **Held.** The photograph was not obscene. The Court replaced the English **Hicklin** test (effect on the most susceptible) with the **community-standards test**: the work must be judged as a whole, by contemporary standards, from the point of view of the average person.
- **Used in:** A-57, C-10.

### 39. *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986) 3 SCC 615 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Three children of Jehovah's Witnesses were expelled from school for not singing the National Anthem, as their religion allows prayer only to Jehovah. They stood up respectfully while it was sung.
- **Held.** The expulsion violated Arts. 19(1)(a) and 25(1). There is no law that requires anyone to sing; the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 punishes preventing singing or disturbing it. Freedom of conscience protects the right to remain silent.
- **Used in:** A-136, B-38, C-19, C-24.

### 40. *Re: Prashant Bhushan* (2021) 1 SCC 745 † · 3 judges
- **Facts.** On 27 June 2020 Prashant Bhushan, an advocate, tweeted that the last four Chief Justices had played a part in the destruction of democracy; on 29 June 2020 he tweeted about the Chief Justice of India (S.A. Bobde) sitting on a motorcycle during the lockdown.
- **Bench.** Arun Mishra, B.R. Gavai and Krishna Murari JJ.
- **Held.** On 14 August 2020 he was held guilty of **criminal contempt**. On 31 August 2020 the Court imposed a fine of **one rupee** payable by 15 September 2020, with three months' imprisonment and a three-year practice bar as default. He paid the fine and filed a review.
- **Principle.** Free speech and contempt of court (Art. 19(2)); fair criticism of judges is permitted, but scandalising the court is not.
- **Used in:** A-58, C-10.

### 41. *Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (2023) 4 SCC 1 · 5 judges (4:1) · 3 January 2023
- **Facts.** On 29 July 2016 a mother and daughter were gang-raped on National Highway 91 near Bulandshahr, Uttar Pradesh. The family was dissatisfied with the police investigation. When the then State minister Azam Khan called the incident a "political conspiracy", the victim's father asked the Supreme Court for an FIR against him and the transfer of the case. The Court transferred the investigation, accepted the minister's unconditional apology (16 December 2016), and referred questions on ministers' statements to a Constitution Bench, together with two Kerala cases.
- **Bench.** Nazeer, Gavai, Bopanna, Ramasubramanian and Nagarathna JJ.; Ramasubramanian J. wrote for the majority.
- **Issues.** Are the grounds in Art. 19(2) exhaustive? Can Arts. 19 and 21 be enforced against persons other than the State? Does the State have a duty to protect Art. 21 from private threats? Is a minister's statement attributable to the Government (Art. 75(3))?
- **Held (4:1).** The grounds in Art. 19(2) are **exhaustive**: no further restriction may be added because the speech conflicts with another right. Arts. 19 and 21 can be enforced against **private persons** where needed (horizontal effect), and the State must protect Art. 21 rights against private threats. A minister's statement that violates a right is **not by itself a constitutional tort**, and is attributable to the Government only if it relates to government affairs or reflects official policy. **Nagarathna J. agreed in part and dissented in part** (reported to differ on writs against private persons and on the protection of speech that demeans dignity; **check the separate opinion**).
- **Used in:** A-03, C-03, C-08.

## Unit 7 · Article 20

### 42. *State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* AIR 1961 SC 1808 · 11 judges
- **Facts.** Accused persons in a criminal case were ordered to give specimen handwriting and thumb impressions for comparison. They said that this compelled them to be witnesses against themselves.
- **Held.** To be "a witness" means to give oral or written statements that convey personal knowledge relevant to the case. Handwriting, signature and thumb or finger impressions given for comparison are physical identification, not testimony, so Art. 20(3) is not violated. The Court partly **departed from *M.P. Sharma*** (1954) on the wide meaning of "witness".
- **Used in:** A-61, B-29, C-12.

### 43. *Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani* (1978) 2 SCC 424 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Nandini Satpathy, a former Chief Minister of Orissa, was questioned by the vigilance police about assets and refused to answer. She was prosecuted under s. 179 IPC for refusing to answer.
- **Held.** Art. 20(3) protects a person at the **investigation stage**, not only at trial, and covers a person who may be accused. She may refuse to answer questions that would tend to incriminate her; **compulsion** includes mental pressure and subtle forms. The right to silence is wide, though she must answer questions that do not incriminate her.
- **Used in:** A-61, B-29, C-12.

### 44. *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010) 7 SCC 263 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Appeals from several criminal cases in which investigating agencies had asked for narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-electrical-activation profile (BEAP) tests on accused persons without their consent.
- **Held.** Involuntary administration of the tests violates **Art. 20(3)** (the results are testimonial) and **Art. 21** (mental privacy and bodily integrity). Voluntary tests are permitted only with safeguards (National Human Rights Commission guidelines of 2000), and the results themselves are not substantive evidence, though facts found because of them may be admissible under s. 27, Evidence Act.
- **Used in:** A-61, A-63, B-29, C-12.

### 45. *Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay* AIR 1953 SC 325 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Customs officers confiscated gold that Maqbool Hussain had brought into India without permission. He was then prosecuted under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act.
- **Held.** Art. 20(2) bars a second prosecution and punishment only where the first was before a **court or judicial tribunal**. Customs authorities are not a judicial tribunal, so confiscation was not "prosecution and punishment", and the second trial was allowed.
- **Used in:** A-62, C-12.

### 46. *Ratan Lal v. State of Punjab* AIR 1965 SC 444 · 3 judges †
- **Facts.** A young man convicted of an offence under the IPC wanted the benefit of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958, which came into force after the offence.
- **Held.** Art. 20(1) bars only retrospective laws that make an act an offence or increase the penalty; a law that **reduces** the penalty or is beneficial can apply to a pending case.
- **Used in:** A-04, C-11.

## Unit 8 · Articles 21 and 21A

### 47. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) 1 SCC 248 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** The Regional Passport Officer impounded Maneka Gandhi's passport in July 1977 "in the interests of the general public" under s. 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967. No reasons or hearing were given.
- **Issues.** Does "personal liberty" include the right to go abroad? Must the procedure be fair? How do Arts. 14, 19 and 21 relate?
- **Held.** "Personal liberty" has the widest meaning, and the right to travel abroad is within it. The "procedure established by law" must be **right, just and fair**, and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; otherwise it is no procedure. Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are **not mutually exclusive**. The Passports Act was valid but the power must be exercised in accordance with natural justice; the Government agreed to give a hearing.
- **Principle.** Substantive due process was introduced into Art. 21; *Gopalan* was overruled.
- **After.** Opened the way for *Francis Coralie*, *Olga Tellis*, *Puttaswamy*, and the right to dignity and a clean environment.
- **Used in:** A-40, A-64, A-65, B-30, C-13.

### 48. *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) 3 SCC 545 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** In 1981 the Bombay Government announced that pavement and slum dwellers would be evicted and sent out of the city. Journalists and residents petitioned.
- **Held.** The right to life includes the right to **livelihood**, because no one can live without the means of living. Eviction must follow a **just and fair procedure**, with notice and a hearing, but the Court allowed removal after a reasonable time. There is no right to encroach on pavements, and Fundamental Rights cannot be waived or lost by estoppel.
- **Used in:** A-66, A-69, B-30, C-13.

### 49. *Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi* (1981) 1 SCC 608 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** A British woman detained in Tihar jail under COFEPOSA challenged limits on meeting her lawyer and family.
- **Held.** The right to life includes the right to live with **human dignity**: adequate nutrition, clothing, shelter, reading and writing, and meeting family and friends. Restrictions on a detainee must be reasonable and not arbitrary.
- **Used in:** B-30, C-14.

### 50. *Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar* (1980) 1 SCC 81 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** A newspaper report in 1979 showed thousands of undertrial prisoners in Bihar jails for periods longer than the maximum sentence for their offences. An advocate filed a writ petition.
- **Held.** A **speedy trial** is part of Art. 21. **Free legal aid** is an essential part of a fair procedure. The Court ordered the release of prisoners on personal bonds.
- **After.** Art. 39A; Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987; *Sheela Barse* and other cases.
- **Used in:** A-68, A-113, C-13.

### 51. *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1 · 9 judges (unanimous)
- **Facts.** Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (retired) challenged the Aadhaar scheme. The Union argued that privacy is not a fundamental right, relying on *M.P. Sharma* (8 judges, 1954) and *Kharak Singh* (6 judges, 1962); a nine-judge bench was constituted to decide the question.
- **Held.** The **right to privacy** is a fundamental right, intrinsic to life and liberty under Art. 21 and part of Part III. It covers bodily integrity, personal autonomy, informational privacy and decisional autonomy. A restriction must satisfy **legality, a legitimate aim, proportionality and procedural safeguards**. *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* were overruled to the extent that they said otherwise. The majority view in *ADM Jabalpur* was disapproved.
- **After.** *Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)* (2018) 1 SCC 809 † (4:1) upheld Aadhaar with limits. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 † now regulate personal data.
- **Used in:** A-67, B-31, C-16.

### 52. *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** An NGO asked the Court to declare that the right to die with dignity, including by a living will, is part of Art. 21.
- **Held.** The right to live with dignity includes the right to die with dignity in the case of terminal illness or a persistent vegetative state. **Passive euthanasia** (withdrawing life support) is permitted, and a person may make an **advance directive (living will)**; the Court laid down guidelines.
- **After.** The guidelines were simplified by a bench on 24 January 2023 †.
- **Used in:** A-71, B-32, C-14.

### 53. *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996) 2 SCC 648 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The accused were convicted of abetting a suicide (s. 306 IPC). They argued that, after *P. Rathinam v. Union of India* (1994) which struck down s. 309 IPC (attempted suicide), s. 306 was also invalid.
- **Held.** The right to life **does not include the right to die**; *Rathinam* was overruled and ss. 306 and 309 were valid. The Court distinguished the case of a dying person whose natural process of death is accelerated (euthanasia).
- **After.** The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, s. 115 presumes severe stress in an attempt to commit suicide. *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011) 4 SCC 454 † allowed passive euthanasia under court supervision; *Common Cause* (2018) followed.
- **Used in:** A-71, B-32.

### 54. *Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992) 3 SCC 666 · and *Unni Krishnan J.P. v. State of A.P.* (1993) 1 SCC 645 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** A Karnataka notification allowed private medical colleges to charge high fees from students not admitted to government seats. A student who could not pay challenged it (*Mohini Jain*).
- **Held.** *Mohini Jain*: the right to education flows from the right to life, and capitation fees are illegal. *Unni Krishnan* (5 judges): every child has a right to **free education until fourteen**; beyond that the right is limited by the State's economic capacity; capitation fees are illegal and the Court devised a **scheme** for private professional colleges.
- **After.** *T.M.A. Pai* (2002) overruled the scheme; the 86th Amendment (2002) added Art. 21A and changed Arts. 45 and 51A.
- **Used in:** A-09, A-72, B-33, C-17.

### 55. *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) 5 SCC 647 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Tanneries in Tamil Nadu discharged untreated effluent into the Palar river and its basin, polluting drinking water and farmland.
- **Held.** The **precautionary principle** and the **polluter pays principle** are part of Indian environmental law, under Arts. 21, 47, 48A and 51A(g). The Court accepted **sustainable development** as the balance between development and the environment, and directed that polluters compensate victims and restore the damage.
- **Used in:** A-75, B-47, C-15.

### 56. *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (Oleum gas leak) (1987) 1 SCC 395 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** In December 1985 oleum gas leaked from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilizers in Delhi, killing a person and affecting many. It came a year after the Bhopal disaster.
- **Held.** An enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is **absolutely liable** to compensate for harm, with no exceptions such as those in *Rylands v. Fletcher*. Compensation should relate to the **size and capacity** of the enterprise. The Court said it could develop new principles for Indian conditions.
- **Used in:** A-76, B-47, C-15.

### 57. *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) 2 SCC 684 · 5 judges (4:1)
- **Facts.** Challenge to the constitutional validity of the death penalty for murder (s. 302 IPC) and the requirement of "special reasons" in s. 354(3) CrPC.
- **Held.** The death penalty is valid but should be imposed only in the **rarest of rare** cases, after balancing aggravating and mitigating circumstances. Bhagwati J. dissented and later wrote that it was unconstitutional.
- **After.** *Machhi Singh* (1983) 3 SCC 470 † listed categories. *Mithu v. State of Punjab* (1983) 2 SCC 277 † struck down the mandatory death sentence in s. 303 IPC. *Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014) 3 SCC 1 (delay in deciding mercy petitions). The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 s. 104 † now deals with murder by a life convict (death or imprisonment for life).
- **Used in:** A-74, B-32, C-13.

### 58. *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Rudul Sah was acquitted by a Sessions Court in 1968 but remained in jail until 1982, 14 years later.
- **Held.** The Supreme Court can award **compensation** under Art. 32 for breach of Art. 21; a mere declaration would be meaningless. The petitioner could still sue for damages.
- **After.** *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677 †; *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) 2 SCC 746 (custodial death: strict liability in public law; no sovereign immunity).
- **Used in:** A-115, B-43, C-22.

## Unit 9 · Articles 23 and 24

### 59. *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (Asiad Workers' case) (1982) 3 SCC 235
- **Facts.** Before the 1982 Asian Games the Government built stadiums and flyovers in Delhi using contract labour, mostly migrant workers. A civil-liberties group's survey said that they were paid less than the minimum wage, that contractors took a commission from wages, and that labour laws were not followed. Children were also employed.
- **Issues.** Does Art. 23 cover payment below the minimum wage? Does it bind private contractors? Is construction "hazardous employment" under Art. 24?
- **Held.** "Forced labour" in Art. 23 covers any labour obtained by **force**, which includes **economic compulsion**: a worker who accepts less than the minimum wage out of need is a forced labourer. Art. 23 protects against the State and **private persons**. The Court also held that construction work is hazardous, so children below fourteen may not be employed in it even though the Employment of Children Act, 1938 did not list it.
- **Used in:** A-77, A-78, B-34, B-35, C-18.

### 60. *Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan* (1983) 1 SCC 525
- **Facts.** Workers on famine-relief road projects in Rajasthan were paid less than the minimum wage, and the State relied on the Rajasthan Famine Relief Works Employees (Exemption from Labour Laws) Act.
- **Held.** Payment below the minimum wage on relief work is forced labour; the State cannot take advantage of a famine to exploit the poor. The exemption was read down. Minimum wage is the minimum due even to workers on relief works.
- **Used in:** A-78, C-18.

### 61. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) 3 SCC 161 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Bandhua Mukti Morcha, led by Swami Agnivesh, wrote to the Court about bonded labourers living in inhuman conditions in stone quarries near Faridabad (Haryana). The Court treated the letter as a writ petition and appointed commissions to visit the quarries.
- **Held.** The right to life under Art. 21 includes **living with human dignity**, and Art. 23 forbids bonded labour. The State must identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labourers. The Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 must be enforced. The Court also upheld its own power to appoint commissions to find facts in a PIL.
- **Used in:** A-79, A-113, B-34, C-18, C-21.

### 62. *M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996) 6 SCC 756 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Children worked in the match and fireworks factories of Sivakasi.
- **Held.** Children may not be employed in the **hazardous** processes (such as making fireworks). In packing, away from the factory, they may work for limited hours. The Court directed offending employers to deposit **Rs 20,000 per child** into a fund, and the State to provide alternative employment for an adult in the family or contribute to the fund. Education must be provided.
- **After.** The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (as amended in 2016); the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.
- **Used in:** A-81, B-35, C-18.

## Unit 10 · Articles 25–28

### 63. *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt* AIR 1954 SC 282
- **Facts.** The Madras Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act, 1951 gave the Commissioner wide powers over mutts and temples. The head (Mahant) of the Shirur Mutt in Udupi, a Madhwa institution, challenged ss. 21, 30(2), 31, 55, 56, 63–69 and 76.
- **Held.** The mutt is a **religious denomination** within Art. 26 (a body with a common faith, common spiritual organisation and a distinctive name). Art. 26(b) protects its right to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, and what is **essential** to religion is decided by the **doctrines of that religion**. Management of property is a secular matter that the State may regulate, but it cannot take away the denomination's administration altogether (Art. 26(d)). Sections 21, 30(2), 31, 55 (in part), 56 and 63–69 were struck down as violating Arts. 19(1)(f), 25 and 26. Section 76(1) (a "contribution") was a **tax**, not a fee, since it had no relation to the cost of services; it was void because the State legislature could not impose it. It did not violate Art. 27, because its object was to administer religious institutions, not to promote a religion.
- **Principle.** Essential-practices test; denomination test; tax versus fee.
- **Used in:** A-86, A-89, A-91, B-36, B-37, B-38, C-19.

### 64. *Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali* AIR 1961 SC 1402 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The Durgah Khwaja Saheb Act, 1955 set up a committee to manage the Ajmer dargah. The *khadims* (hereditary attendants) said that the Act violated Art. 26.
- **Held.** The Act was valid. Only practices that are **religious and essential** are protected; a practice that is secular or a **superstitious belief** is not made part of religion by being long followed. The Court said that it must look at the history and tenets of the order.
- **Used in:** A-86, B-36.

### 65. *Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore* AIR 1958 SC 255
- **Facts.** A temple of the Gowda Saraswat Brahmin community (a denomination) in South Kanara was opened to all Hindus, including Harijans, under the Madras Temple Entry Authorisation Act, 1947.
- **Held.** Art. 25(2)(b) (opening Hindu public institutions to all classes) and Art. 26(b) (denominational management) conflict; the Court **harmonised** them. All Hindus may enter for darshan, but the denomination may keep rights over specific rites (*utsavas* and *poojas*) that are an essential part of its worship.
- **Used in:** A-87, B-37.

### 66. *Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh* (1977) 1 SCC 677 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The Orissa Freedom of Religion Act, 1967 and the Madhya Pradesh Dharma Swatantrya Adhiniyam, 1968 forbade conversion by force, fraud or allurement. They were challenged as violating the right to propagate in Art. 25.
- **Held.** To "propagate" means to transmit or spread one's religion by exposition of its tenets; it does **not** include a right to convert another person. A forced conversion impinges on the "freedom of conscience" of the person converted and may disturb public order. Both Acts were valid and within the State's power over public order.
- **Used in:** A-85, B-36, C-19.

### 67. *M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India* (1994) 6 SCC 360 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** After the Babri Masjid was demolished (6 December 1992), the Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act, 1993 took over the land and the suits. The Act was challenged under Arts. 25 and 26. A Presidential reference under Art. 143 on whether there was a temple earlier was returned unanswered.
- **Held.** The Act was valid, apart from s. 4(3) (abating the pending suits), which was struck down †. A **mosque is not an essential part of the practice of Islam**; prayer can be offered anywhere, even in the open, so a mosque can be acquired like other places of worship. Property of a religious body is not immune from compulsory acquisition.
- **After.** In *M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das* (2018) † a bench declined by 2:1 to refer the "essential practice" remark to a larger bench. The Ayodhya judgment (9 November 2019) decided the title suit.
- **Used in:** A-93, B-36, C-19.

### 68. *Aruna Roy v. Union of India* (2002) 7 SCC 368
- **Facts.** The National Curriculum Framework for School Education, 2000 proposed teaching about religions and values. The petitioners said that it violated Art. 28 and the secular scheme.
- **Held.** Teaching about the basic tenets of all religions and moral values is not "religious instruction" or worship under Art. 28. It was valid as long as it was educational and not devotional.
- **Used in:** A-92, B-38.

### 69. *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (Sabarimala) (2019) 11 SCC 1 · 5 judges (4:1) · judgment of 28 September 2018
- **Facts.** The Sabarimala temple (Lord Ayyappa) did not allow women aged 10 to 50 to enter. Rule 3(b) of the Kerala Hindu Places of Public Worship (Authorisation of Entry) Rules, 1965 allowed denominations to restrict women. A group of lawyers brought a PIL.
- **Held.** Rule 3(b) is unconstitutional. Worshippers of Ayyappa are **not a separate denomination**; the exclusion of women is not an essential religious practice; and it violates Arts. 14, 15, 17 and 25(1). Indu Malhotra J. dissented, saying that courts should not interfere in deep religious beliefs.
- **After.** On 14 November 2019 a five-judge bench hearing the review petitions (*Kantaru Rajeevaru v. Indian Young Lawyers Association* (2020) 2 SCC 1 †) referred wider questions (the scope of Art. 25 and 26, essential practices, and the meaning of "morality") to a larger bench, keeping the reviews pending. A nine-judge bench heard the reference; **check whether it has pronounced judgment**.
- **Used in:** A-95, B-36, C-19.

### 70. *Prafull Goradia v. Union of India* (2011) 2 SCC 568 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** The petitioner challenged the Haj Committee Act, 2002 and the Government's subsidy for Haj travel under Art. 27.
- **Held (28 January 2011).** The writ was dismissed. Art. 27 is breached only if a **substantial part** of a tax is utilised for a particular religion; the petitioner could not show that, and facilities for other religions were also provided, so there was no discrimination under Arts. 14 and 15.
- **After.** *Union of India v. Rafique Shaikh Bhikan* (2012) † directed that the subsidy be phased out in ten years; it ended in 2018.
- **Used in:** A-91, B-38.

## Unit 11 · Articles 29–30

### 71. *In re Kerala Education Bill, 1957* AIR 1958 SC 956 · 7 judges (Art. 143)
- **Facts.** The President referred questions about the Kerala Education Bill, which regulated aided schools and allowed the State to take over a school's management.
- **Held.** Art. 30(1) is not absolute, but the right to administer cannot be defeated by conditions of aid. The State can regulate to secure education standards, but cannot interfere so as to destroy the minority character. Arts. 29(2) and 30(1) are to be read together. A minority is a community **numerically less than 50%** of the population.
- **Used in:** A-100, B-39, C-20.

### 72. *Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society v. State of Gujarat* (1974) 1 SCC 717 · 9 judges
- **Facts.** The Gujarat University Act, 1949 (as amended in 1972) required affiliated colleges to follow the University's rules on staff, including disciplinary action. A Jesuit minority college challenged it.
- **Held.** The provisions that took away the management of the minority college (including disciplinary control of staff) violated Art. 30(1). **Regulatory** measures for educational standards, hygiene and discipline are permitted, but a measure that takes away the **right to administer** is not. Minority institutions that seek affiliation do not lose Art. 30 rights.
- **Used in:** A-101, B-39, C-20.

### 73. *St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi* (1992) 1 SCC 558 †
- **Facts.** St. Stephen's College (Church of North India) reserved 50% of seats for Christians and used interview marks for selection, while Delhi University's rules required admission on marks alone.
- **Held.** A minority institution can give **preference** to its community subject to a reasonable limit (50% was accepted) provided it admits others on merit. Admissions to the non-minority seats had to follow the University's rule of selection on marks; **check what the Court said about interviews**. Aided minority institutions must follow regulations of general application.
- **After.** *T.M.A. Pai* (2002) overruled the 50% ceiling. The percentage is left to a reasonable limit set by the State.
- **Used in:** A-99, B-39, C-20.

### 74. *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka* (2002) 8 SCC 481 · 11 judges
- **Facts.** After *Unni Krishnan* (1993) set a scheme for private professional colleges, private and minority institutions challenged it, and the question of minority status also arose.
- **Held.** (i) A minority is determined with the **State as the unit** (for both religious and linguistic minorities) under Art. 30. (ii) Education is an **occupation** under Art. 19(1)(g); unaided private institutions have the right to establish and administer, with autonomy in admission and fees within limits (no capitation fee; no profiteering). (iii) Aided minority institutions can be regulated, but cannot be made to give up their character. (iv) The *Unni Krishnan* scheme was unconstitutional. The Court left the implementation to later benches.
- **After.** *Islamic Academy of Education v. State of Karnataka* (2003) 6 SCC 697 †; *P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra* (2005) 6 SCC 537 † (7 judges): a State cannot impose its reservation policy on unaided private institutions, minority or non-minority. The 93rd Amendment (Art. 15(5), 2005) then permitted it for non-minority institutions; *Pramati* (2014) upheld it.
- **Used in:** A-72, A-100, A-102, B-33, B-39, C-20.

### 75. *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014) 8 SCC 1 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Challenge to Art. 15(5) (93rd Amendment) and to the RTE Act, 2009 as it applied to unaided and minority schools.
- **Held.** Art. 15(5) is valid and does not damage the basic structure. The RTE Act cannot apply to **minority institutions**, aided or unaided, as it would affect their Art. 30(1) right.
- **Used in:** A-44, A-73, B-33.

### 76. *S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India* AIR 1968 SC 662 · 5 judges, and *Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal* (8 November 2024) · 7 judges (4:3)
- **Facts.** The Aligarh Muslim University (AMU) was established by the Aligarh Muslim University Act, 1920. Amendments in 1951 and 1965 changed its governance, and the petitioner said the University was established by Muslims and so was protected by Art. 30(1).
- **Held in *Azeez Basha* (1967).** The University was established by an **Act of Parliament** (the Central Legislature), not by the Muslim minority, so Art. 30(1) did not apply.
- **After.** The AMU (Amendment) Act, 1981 said that it was established by the Muslim community; the Allahabad High Court struck this down (2006). In *Naresh Agarwal* (8 November 2024) Chandrachud CJI (with Khanna, Pardiwala and Misra JJ.) **overruled *Azeez Basha***: an institution does not lose minority status merely because a statute incorporated it; the Court asks who established it and who was the "brain" behind it. Surya Kant, Datta and S.C. Sharma JJ. dissented. A regular bench is to decide AMU's status; **check the outcome**.
- **Used in:** A-103, B-39, C-20.

## Unit 12 · Article 32, the writs and PIL

### 77. *Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner, U.P.* AIR 1963 SC 996 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Rule 12 of Order XXXV of the Supreme Court Rules required a petitioner under Art. 32 to deposit security for costs.
- **Held.** The rule was invalid: it imposes a financial burden on a guaranteed Fundamental Right. **Art. 32 is itself a Fundamental Right**, and the Court's rule-making power under Art. 145 cannot restrict it.
- **Used in:** A-106, B-41, C-22.

### 78. *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* 1981 Supp SCC 87 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** The "Judges' Transfer Case". Lawyers challenged the Government's transfer of High Court judges and its non-appointment of additional judges, and the primacy of the executive in judicial appointments.
- **Held.** **Locus standi** is relaxed: any member of the public with sufficient interest, acting bona fide, may seek redress for a legal wrong to persons who cannot approach the court. The Court also held that the executive's view prevailed in judicial appointments.
- **After.** The appointments holding was reversed in the *Second Judges Case* (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Assn.* (1993) 4 SCC 441 †) and the *Third Judges Case* (1998) †.
- **Used in:** A-113, B-42, C-21.

### 79. *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997) 3 SCC 261 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** Art. 323A and 323B and the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 excluded the High Courts' jurisdiction over service matters.
- **Held.** Power of judicial review of the High Courts under **Arts. 226/227** and of the Supreme Court under **Art. 32** is part of the basic structure; the exclusion clauses were unconstitutional. Tribunals work as courts of first instance, with appeals to the High Court's division bench. *S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987) was overruled.
- **Used in:** A-112, B-41, C-22.

### 80. *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Sunil Batra, a death-row prisoner, wrote to a Supreme Court judge about the torture of another prisoner by a head warder. The Court treated the letter as a petition for habeas corpus.
- **Held.** Prisoners do not lose Fundamental Rights; **habeas corpus** can be used to challenge unlawful conditions in custody, such as torture and solitary confinement. It is an early example of **epistolary jurisdiction**.
- **Used in:** A-108, A-114, B-40, C-21.

### 81. *Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Syed Ahmad Ishaque* AIR 1955 SC 233
- **Facts.** An election tribunal's order was challenged by *certiorari* on the ground of an error of law.
- **Held.** The Court explained the scope of **certiorari**: it lies for want or excess of jurisdiction, a breach of natural justice, and an error of law apparent on the face of the record.
- **Used in:** A-110, B-40.

### 82. *Vineet Narain v. Union of India* (1998) 1 SCC 226 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** The "Jain hawala" diaries of 1991 showed payments to politicians. The CBI did not investigate the leads.
- **Held.** The Court supervised the investigation by **continuing mandamus** and gave directions on the independence of the CBI and the Central Vigilance Commission. The Single Directive (requiring Government sanction before investigating senior officers) was held invalid.
- **After.** Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003; *Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI* (2014) 8 SCC 682 † struck down s. 6A DSPE Act.
- **Used in:** A-116, B-42, C-21.

### 83. *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010) 3 SCC 402 · 2 judges
- **Facts †.** An appeal from a PIL decided by the Uttarakhand High Court gave the Court the occasion to review the working of PIL and its abuse.
- **Held.** The Court issued **guidelines**: courts should encourage genuine PILs and discourage frivolous ones; verify the petitioner's credentials and bona fides; ensure that the petition has real public interest; avoid litigation with a private motive; and impose **exemplary costs** on petitioners who abuse the process.
- **Used in:** A-117, B-42, C-21.

## Unit 13 · Directive Principles

### 84. *Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum* (1985) 2 SCC 556 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Shah Bano, aged 62, was divorced by her husband, an advocate, by triple talaq after 43 years of marriage. She sought maintenance under s. 125 CrPC.
- **Held.** Section 125 CrPC applies to all, whatever their religion, and is secular; a divorced Muslim woman who cannot maintain herself can claim maintenance beyond the *iddat* period. The Court said that a Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44) would help national integration.
- **After.** Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. *Danial Latifi v. Union of India* (2001) 7 SCC 740 † upheld the Act but read it to give a "reasonable and fair provision" for the divorced wife's future.
- **Used in:** A-122, B-45, C-23.

### 85. *Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) 3 SCC 635 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** Hindu husbands converted to Islam to marry again without dissolving their first marriage.
- **Held.** A second marriage after conversion, with the first marriage subsisting, is void and the husband is guilty of **bigamy** under s. 494 IPC. The Court asked the Government to consider a Uniform Civil Code.
- **After.** *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2000) 6 SCC 224 † clarified the limits of the ruling.
- **Used in:** A-122, B-45.

### 86. *John Vallamattom v. Union of India* (2003) 6 SCC 611 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** The petitioner, a priest, challenged s. 118 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925, which limited a Christian's bequests for religious or charitable purposes.
- **Held.** Section 118 violates Art. 14 and was struck down. The Court again regretted that Art. 44 had not been implemented.
- **Used in:** A-122, B-45.

### 87. *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.* (1983) 1 SCC 147 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The Coking Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act, 1972 was challenged for violating Arts. 14 and 19; the Union relied on Art. 31C.
- **Held.** The Act was protected by Art. 31C (as it survived *Minerva Mills*) because it gave effect to Art. 39(b) and (c); the Court spoke widely about Art. 39(b) in line with Krishna Iyer J.'s minority view in *Ranganatha Reddy*.
- **After.** *Property Owners Association* (2024) held that *Sanjeev Coke* erred in relying on the minority view of Krishna Iyer J.
- **Used in:** A-35, B-46.

### 88. *Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar* AIR 1958 SC 731 · 5 judges, and *State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat* (2005) 8 SCC 534 · 7 judges
- **Facts.** Cattle-slaughter laws in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh banned slaughter of cows and cattle. Butchers said that the laws violated Art. 19(1)(g).
- **Held in *Quareshi*.** A total ban on slaughter of cows and calves, and of bulls and bullocks that are useful, is valid under Art. 19(6) and Art. 48; the ban on slaughtering animals that are no longer useful is an unreasonable restriction.
- **Held in *Mirzapur Moti Kureshi*.** *Quareshi* was overruled on this point: a ban on slaughter of all cow progeny, including bulls and bullocks, is a reasonable restriction in the light of Art. 48, Art. 51A(g) and the importance of cattle to the economy.
- **Used in:** A-125.

### 89. *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024) · 9 judges
- **Facts.** Chapter VIII-A of the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Act, 1976 (inserted 1986) let the State acquire old "cessed" buildings in Mumbai for restoration and tenants, saying it gave effect to Art. 39(b). The Bombay High Court upheld it under Art. 31C. The 2019 amendment allowed the State to take over buildings that owners did not restore.
- **Issue.** Is every privately owned resource a "material resource of the community" under Art. 39(b)?
- **Held (Chandrachud CJI for seven; Nagarathna J. concurring in part; Dhulia J. dissenting).** **No.** Private property can be a material resource of the community only if it satisfies a test: the nature of the resource, its effect on community welfare, scarcity, and the consequence of concentration in private hands. The Court said that Krishna Iyer J.'s wide reading in *Ranganatha Reddy* was wrong and that *Sanjeev Coke* erred in relying on it. All nine agreed that **Art. 31C survives** in its original form (as upheld in *Kesavananda*) after *Minerva Mills*.
- **Used in:** A-120, B-46, C-23.

## Unit 14 · Fundamental Duties

### 90. *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of Uttar Pradesh* (1985) 2 SCC 431; (1986) Supp SCC 517
- **Facts.** A letter from the Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra, Dehradun, about limestone quarrying in the Mussoorie hills (Doon Valley) was treated as a petition. The Court appointed a committee to inspect the quarries.
- **Held.** The Court ordered several quarries to close in order to protect the ecological balance, saying that this was an inevitable cost of the right of people to live in a healthy environment under Art. 21. It was among the first cases on ecological balance and sustainable development, and the Court used Arts. 48A and 51A(g).
- **Used in:** A-75, A-134, B-47, C-15, C-24.

### 91. *AIIMS Students' Union v. All India Institute of Medical Sciences* (2002) 1 SCC 428 †
- **Held (on duties).** Fundamental duties, though not enforceable by a writ, are a **valuable guide and aid to interpretation** of constitutional and legal issues; rights and duties are correlative.
- **Check:** the facts and the outcome of the case in the report, and the exact words, before quoting.
- **Used in:** A-134, C-24.

### 92. *Union of India v. Naveen Jindal* (2004) 2 SCC 510 · 2 judges
- **Facts.** An industrialist flew the national flag at his factory. The Flag Code of India then restricted display to Government buildings and a few occasions.
- **Held.** The right to fly the national flag with respect and dignity is a part of **freedom of expression** under Art. 19(1)(a), subject to reasonable restrictions under Art. 19(2). The Flag Code (an executive instruction) is not "law" and cannot restrict it, but must be read with Art. 51A(a).
- **Used in:** A-137, C-24.

### 93. *Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India* (2018) 2 SCC 574 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** By an interim order of 30 November 2016, the Court made the playing of the National Anthem in cinema halls before each film mandatory, with everyone standing.
- **Held (9 January 2018).** The order was modified: playing the anthem in cinemas is **optional**, and the Government may make rules under the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971.
- **Used in:** A-137, C-24.
