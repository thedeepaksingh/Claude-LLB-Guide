## C-08 · Expanding horizons of freedom of speech and expression

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q9 ("With the help of decided cases discuss expanding horizons of freedom of speech and expression"). Closely related: Dec 2023 Q9, Dec 2022 Q12 (answered at C-10).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text and meaning 3 · stages of expansion with cases 9 · limits 3 · recent developments 3 · criticism and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 19(1)(a) says only that "all citizens shall have the right to freedom of speech and expression". It does not mention the press, information, broadcasting or the internet. The Supreme Court has read the clause as a living guarantee, because free discussion is the foundation of democratic government. As Patanjali Sastri J. said in *Romesh Thappar*, freedom of speech and of the press lies at the foundation of all democratic organisations. The result is a right whose horizon has widened with every new medium and need.

**The frame.** The right belongs to **citizens**. It is not absolute: Art. 19(2) allows **reasonable restrictions** on eight grounds only: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offence. The grounds are **exhaustive** (*Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P.*, 2023).

**Stages of expansion**

| Stage | New content read into Art. 19(1)(a) | Leading cases and what they held |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Circulation and the press | Freedom to publish **and circulate**; no prior censorship | *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 124: a ban on circulating *Cross Roads* in Madras struck down, because "public order" was not then a ground in Art. 19(2). *Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* AIR 1950 SC 129: pre-censorship of the *Organiser* void. *Sakal Papers v. Union of India* AIR 1962 SC 305: a price-page schedule for newspapers struck down as it cut circulation. *Bennett Coleman v. Union of India* (1972) 2 SCC 788: newsprint quota struck down; the **direct effect** of a law on the right is tested. *Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India* (1985) 1 SCC 641: the press has no immunity from tax but arbitrary burdens on newsprint are open to challenge. |
| 2. The right to know | Information about government and candidates | *State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975) 4 SCC 428: public agents must be accountable, so there can be few secrets in a responsible government. *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India* (1981) Supp SCC 87: "open government". *Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms* (2002) 5 SCC 294: a voter may know a candidate's criminal record, assets and education. *PUCL v. Union of India* (2003) 4 SCC 399: the Court struck down s. 33B, RP Act. *PUCL v. Union of India* (2013) 10 SCC 1: the right to vote negatively (NOTA) is expression. Right to Information Act, 2005 now gives statutory form. |
| 3. Broadcasting and commercial speech | The right to **telecast**; **advertising** | *Secretary, Ministry of I&B v. Cricket Association of Bengal* (1995) 2 SCC 161: airwaves are public property; the right to use them for broadcast is within Art. 19(1)(a) but regulated. *Tata Press v. MTNL* (1995) 5 SCC 139: commercial speech that informs the public is protected. |
| 4. Silence and symbols | The right **not to speak**; flag-flying | *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala* (1986) 3 SCC 615: children who stood silently during the anthem could not be expelled. *Union of India v. Naveen Jindal* (2004) 2 SCC 510: flying the national flag is an aspect of expression, subject to the Flag Code. |
| 5. Art, cinema and unpopular opinion | Creative and dissenting expression | *K.A. Abbas v. Union of India* (1970) 2 SCC 780: pre-censorship of films upheld as a reasonable restriction. *S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram* (1989) 2 SCC 574: the State must protect a certified film against hostile crowds. *Bobby Art International v. Om Pal Singh Hoon* (1996) 4 SCC 1: *Bandit Queen*, nudity in context not obscene. *Aveek Sarkar v. State of W.B.* (2014) 4 SCC 257: community-standards test replaced *Hicklin*. *S. Khushboo v. Kanniammal* (2010) 5 SCC 600: expressing a view on premarital sex is not a crime. |
| 6. Protest | Peaceful assembly with speech | *Ramlila Maidan Incident, In re* (2012) 5 SCC 1; *Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan v. Union of India* (2018) 17 SCC 324: a right to protest, subject to regulation. |
| 7. The internet | Speech and trade online | *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637: freedom of speech over the internet is protected under Art. 19(1)(a), and trade under Art. 19(1)(g); indefinite shutdown is impermissible; orders must be published, necessary, proportionate and temporary, and are open to judicial review. |

**Where the horizon stops.** The Court has refused to include a right to call a **bandh** (*Communist Party of India (M) v. Bharat Kumar*, (1998) 1 SCC 201). Speech that is **incitement** may be restricted, but "discussion" and "advocacy" of even an unpopular cause are protected (*Shreya Singhal v. Union of India*, (2015) 5 SCC 1, which struck down s. 66A IT Act as vague and as having a chilling effect). Danger to public order must be real and proximate, "like a spark in a powder keg" (*Rangarajan*). Restrictions must satisfy the tests of reasonableness, now proportionality: legitimate aim, rational connection, necessity and balance (*Modern Dental College*, (2016) 7 SCC 353; *Puttaswamy*, (2017) 10 SCC 1; *Anuradha Bhasin*).

**Recent developments.**
1. *Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P.* (Constitution Bench, 3 January 2023): no restriction beyond Art. 19(2) can be added; Arts. 19 and 21 can operate against private persons as well.
2. *Electoral bonds* (*Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India*, 15 February 2024): five judges held that the voter's right to information extends to political funding; the scheme was struck down.
3. **Sedition.** On 11 May 2022 (*S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India*) the Court kept s. 124A IPC in abeyance. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (in force 1 July 2024) has no offence named sedition; s. 152 punishes acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, and critics say it is also broad.

**Criticism.** "Public order" and "security of the State" are elastic; criminal defamation survives (*Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India*, (2016) 7 SCC 221); internet shutdowns remain frequent; large platforms regulate speech but Art. 19 mainly binds the State.

**Conclusion.** From *Romesh Thappar* to *Anuradha Bhasin*, the Court has treated each new vehicle of expression as part of Art. 19(1)(a). The right is wide, but it remains a right against State action within eight listed grounds, and the test for every restriction is now reasonableness and proportionality.

## C-09 · Freedom of the press

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q13 ("Freedom of Press is part and parcel of freedom of speech and expression. Comment. Refer to decided cases.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** constitutional basis 4 · cases on press freedom 8 · limits 4 · recent and criticism 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Constitution does not name the press. Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression to citizens, and the Supreme Court has held that freedom of the press is **derived from it**; it is not a separate or superior right. The Constituent Assembly took the view, expressed by Dr Ambedkar, that the press needs no special mention because an editor or publisher is only exercising the citizen's right. The comment in the question is therefore correct: the press is "part and parcel" of Art. 19(1)(a), with the same limits in Art. 19(2).

**Why it matters.** The press is the main channel through which citizens receive information and opinion. Patanjali Sastri J. said in *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* (AIR 1950 SC 124) that freedom of speech and of the press lies at the foundation of all democratic organisations. In *Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India* (1985) 1 SCC 641 the Court called the press essential to public education and to the working of a free society.

**1. What the freedom includes and the cases**

| Facet | Case | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| Publication **and circulation** | *Romesh Thappar* (1950) | The Madras order banning entry and circulation of *Cross Roads* violated Art. 19(1)(a): "public order" was then not a ground in Art. 19(2). The First Amendment (1951) added it. |
| No **pre-censorship** | *Brij Bhushan v. State of Delhi* AIR 1950 SC 129 | An order requiring the *Organiser* to submit communal and Pakistan-related matter for scrutiny was a prior restraint and void. |
| **Circulation and economic controls** | *Sakal Papers (P) Ltd. v. Union of India* AIR 1962 SC 305 | The Daily Newspapers (Price and Page) Order fixed the number of pages for a price. Struck down: it cut circulation, and the State cannot justify it as a measure for economic ends because it is not within Art. 19(2). |
| **Direct effect test** | *Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India* (1972) 2 SCC 788 | The newsprint policy that limited pages and editions was struck down. The Court looked at the **effect** of the policy on the right, not its stated object. |
| **Taxation** | *Indian Express Newspapers v. Union of India* (1985) 1 SCC 641 | The press has no immunity from taxes, but a levy that is arbitrary or that burdens circulation can be tested under Arts. 14 and 19(1)(a). |
| **Wage regulation** | *Express Newspapers (P) Ltd. v. Union of India* AIR 1958 SC 578 | Fixing wages of working journalists under the Working Journalists Act was upheld; laws of general application to the industry are not an attack on press freedom. |
| **Privacy and defamation** | *R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N.* (1994) 6 SCC 632 | The press may publish matters of **public record** without liability; public officials cannot sue over official conduct unless the publication is false or in reckless disregard of truth; **prior restraint** is generally not allowed. |
| **Airwaves** | *Cricket Association of Bengal* (1995) 2 SCC 161 | Broadcasting is within Art. 19(1)(a); airwaves are public property, so licensing and regulation are permissible. |
| **Fair trial** | *Sahara India Real Estate Corp. v. SEBI* (2012) 10 SCC 603 | Courts may order postponement of publication in rare cases to protect the right to a fair trial (Art. 21), balanced against open reporting. |
| **Security claims and sealed cover** | *Madhyamam Broadcasting Ltd. v. Union of India* (5 April 2023) (the MediaOne case) | Refusing renewal of a channel's licence on the basis of undisclosed material shown only to the court was held disproportionate; the Court said the press must be able to "speak truth to power". |

**2. Limits.** The press enjoys no right higher than a citizen's. Its freedom is subject to Art. 19(2): defamation, contempt (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971), public order, decency or morality, and the others. The law of obscenity applies (*Aveek Sarkar*, 2014), and the Press Council of India Act, 1978 allows censure by the Council. The Court has also held that criminal defamation is a reasonable restriction (*Subramanian Swamy*, 2016).

**3. Recent developments.**
- *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637 held that the freedom of the press is protected over the internet, and that blanket shutdowns that stop reporting are impermissible.
- *Madhyamam* (2023), above: security claims need reasons that can be tested.
- The Court's 2024 decision in the electoral bonds case (*ADR*) upheld the public's right to know about political funding.
- The 2023 amendment to the IT Rules (government fact-check unit) was reportedly struck down by the Bombay High Court in September 2024; check the present state of the appeal before relying on it.

**4. Criticism.** Press freedom in India is exposed to indirect pressures that the courts struggle to reach: advertising dependence, defamation suits that work as intimidation, sedition-type charges against journalists, and shutdowns. The Court's formal protection is strong but enforcement is uneven.

**Conclusion.** The comment is right: the press has no free-standing constitutional right but shares and strengthens the citizen's right under Art. 19(1)(a). The cases from *Romesh Thappar* to *Madhyamam* show that the Court protects the press against prior restraint, indirect economic control and unexplained security claims, while keeping the Art. 19(2) limits that apply to every citizen.

## C-10 · Freedoms included in Art. 19(1)(a) and the restrictions on them

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q9 ("Discuss with the help of decided cases the right to freedom of speech and the restrictions imposed on it"); Dec 2022 Q12 ("Which different freedoms are included in 'right to freedom of speech'? What restrictions are imposed on them? Discuss in detail with decided cases")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text 2 · freedoms included 5 · restrictions ground by ground with cases 8 · tests of reasonableness 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 19(1)(a) guarantees to **citizens** the right to freedom of speech and expression. It is the first of the six freedoms in Art. 19(1) and the basis of the other democratic rights. Article 19(2) allows the State to impose **reasonable restrictions by law** on that right on eight grounds only.

**Part I. Freedoms included**

1. **Freedom of the press and of circulation:** *Romesh Thappar* (1950); *Sakal Papers* (1962); *Bennett Coleman* (1972); *Indian Express* (1985).
2. **Right to information and to know:** *Raj Narain* (1975); *ADR* (2002); *PUCL* (2003); Right to Information Act, 2005.
3. **Broadcasting:** *Cricket Association of Bengal* (1995).
4. **Commercial speech:** *Tata Press* (1995).
5. **Right to remain silent:** *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986).
6. **Flying the national flag:** *Naveen Jindal* (2004).
7. **Cinema, art and literature:** *K.A. Abbas* (1970); *Bobby Art International* (1996).
8. **Peaceful protest** (with Art. 19(1)(b)): *Ramlila Maidan* (2012); *MKSS* (2018).
9. **Freedom on the internet:** *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020).
Not included: the right to call a **bandh** (*CPI(M) v. Bharat Kumar*, 1998).

**Part II. Restrictions: Art. 19(2)**

The restriction must (i) be imposed by **law**, (ii) fall under one of the **eight grounds**, and (iii) be **reasonable**. The First Amendment (1951) added public order, friendly relations with foreign States and incitement to an offence, and inserted "reasonable"; the Sixteenth Amendment (1963) added the sovereignty and integrity of India.

| Ground | Leading cases and rule |
|---|---|
| **Sovereignty and integrity of India** | Aimed at secession. Section 124A IPC (sedition) was tested in *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* AIR 1962 SC 955: valid only if confined to acts with a **tendency to create disorder or incite violence**; mere criticism of the Government is not sedition. *Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab* (1995) 3 SCC 214: casual slogans without incitement are not sedition. The Supreme Court kept s. 124A in abeyance on 11 May 2022 (*S.G. Vombatkere*). The BNS, 2023 has no "sedition"; s. 152 punishes acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. |
| **Security of the State** | The narrowest circle: war, rebellion, serious threats. *Superintendent, Central Prison v. Ram Manohar Lohia* AIR 1960 SC 633: a speech urging non-payment of irrigation dues had no proximate link with public order, so the law was struck down. |
| **Public order** | *Dr Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar* AIR 1966 SC 740: three concentric circles: **law and order** (widest), **public order**, **security of the State** (narrowest). Only acts that disturb public order, not mere law and order, may be restricted. *Arun Ghosh v. State of W.B.* (1970) 1 SCC 98: the test is whether the act disturbs the current of life of the community. *S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram* (1989) 2 SCC 574: the danger must be real and imminent, "like a spark in a powder keg". |
| **Friendly relations with foreign States** | Rarely litigated; covers speech that jeopardises relations. |
| **Decency or morality (obscenity)** | The *Hicklin* test (*R v. Hicklin*, 1868) was applied in *Ranjit D. Udeshi v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1965 SC 881. *Aveek Sarkar v. State of W.B.* (2014) 4 SCC 257 replaced it with the **community-standards test**: the work is judged as a whole, by contemporary standards, from the standpoint of the average person. *Bobby Art International* (1996): *Bandit Queen* was not obscene. Statute: IPC s. 292 (now BNS s. 294). |
| **Contempt of court** | Contempt of Courts Act, 1971; Arts. 129 and 215. Truth is a defence since 2006 (s. 13). *E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.N. Nambiar* (1970) 2 SCC 325; *Arundhati Roy, In re* (2002) 3 SCC 343; *Prashant Bhushan, In re* (2021) 1 SCC 745 (fine of Re. 1). |
| **Defamation** | *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) 7 SCC 221 upheld criminal defamation (IPC ss. 499–500; now BNS s. 356). *R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N.* (1994) 6 SCC 632: public officials cannot sue over official conduct unless publication is false or reckless. |
| **Incitement to an offence** | *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) 5 SCC 1: only **incitement** can be restricted; **discussion** and **advocacy** are protected. Section 66A IT Act was struck down as vague and as having a chilling effect. |

**Hate speech** is not a separate ground. It is dealt with under public order and incitement, and in statutes (IPC ss. 153A and 295A; now BNS ss. 196 and 299). See *Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. Union of India* (2014) 11 SCC 477 and *Amish Devgan v. Union of India* (2021) 1 SCC 1, which look at the effect on the target group.

**Part III. Tests of reasonableness**
1. **Law:** an executive order is not a "law" (and in *Kaushal Kishor*, 2023, the Constitution Bench held that no ground outside Art. 19(2) can be added).
2. **Arbitrary or excessive restrictions are unreasonable:** *Chintaman Rao v. State of M.P.* AIR 1951 SC 118.
3. **Factors:** *State of Madras v. V.G. Row* AIR 1952 SC 196: nature of the right, purpose of the restriction, extent and urgency of the evil, disproportion, prevailing conditions; both substantive and procedural reasonableness count.
4. **Proportionality:** legitimate aim, rational nexus, necessity (least restrictive means), and balance (*Modern Dental College*, 2016; *Puttaswamy*, 2017; *Anuradha Bhasin*, 2020).
5. **Prior restraint** is disfavoured (*Brij Bhushan*; *Rajagopal*), but film pre-censorship was upheld (*Abbas*).

**Conclusion.** Free speech is the rule and restriction the exception. A restriction must come from a valid law, fit one of the eight grounds, and be proportionate. The Court's line from *Lohia* to *Shreya Singhal* is that only a real and proximate threat to public order, or incitement, justifies silencing a citizen.

## C-11 · Article 20: protection in respect of conviction for offences

**Asked as:** not asked in full in Dec 2022–2025, but each clause has been asked separately (see B-04, B-05, C-12). The standard long form is "Explain the safeguards in Art. 20."
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and text 3 · clause (1) 5 · clause (2) 5 · clause (3) 5 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 20 protects a person **accused of an offence** against three abuses of the criminal process: retrospective penal laws, double punishment, and compelled self-incrimination. It is available to **any person**, citizen or not, only in **criminal proceedings**, and it **cannot be suspended even in an Emergency** (Art. 359(1) proviso, inserted by the 44th Amendment, 1978).

**Clause (1): no ex post facto penal law.** "No person shall be convicted of any offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence, nor be subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time of the commission of the offence."
- It has **two limbs**: no conviction for an act that was not an offence when done; no penalty greater than the law then in force allowed.
- It bars retrospective **criminal** laws only. Civil and tax laws may be retrospective (they are tested under Art. 14, 19 or 300A).
- It does not bar changes of **procedure**, because no one has a vested right in a mode of trial: *Rao Shiv Bahadur Singh v. State of Vindhya Pradesh* AIR 1953 SC 394; *Kedar Nath Bajoria v. State of W.B.* AIR 1953 SC 404.
- A retrospective law that **reduces** punishment or benefits the accused is allowed: *Ratan Lal v. State of Punjab* AIR 1965 SC 444 (Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 applied to an earlier offence).
- Preventive detention is not "punishment for an offence", so clause (1) does not apply to it.
- **New criminal codes.** The BNS, BNSS and BSA came into force on 1 July 2024. Offences committed before that date continue to be tried under the IPC and CrPC; a new BNS offence (for example organised crime or mob lynching) cannot be used against pre-July 2024 acts. That is Art. 20(1) at work.

**Clause (2): no double jeopardy.** "No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once."
- The plea has four elements: (i) a **prosecution** before a court or judicial tribunal; (ii) **punishment** (a conviction); (iii) for the **same offence**; (iv) a second prosecution and punishment.
- *Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay* AIR 1953 SC 325: confiscation of gold by Customs authorities was not a prosecution before a judicial tribunal, so a later trial under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act was not barred.
- *S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India* AIR 1954 SC 375: a departmental inquiry is not a prosecution.
- *State of Bombay v. S.L. Apte* AIR 1961 SC 578: the bar applies only if the **ingredients** of the two offences are the same.
- Under the statute, CrPC s. 300 (now BNSS s. 337) also bars a second trial after **acquittal**, which Art. 20(2) by itself does not; s. 26 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 allows prosecution under either of two enactments but not punishment twice for the same offence.

**Clause (3): no compulsion to be a witness against oneself.** Three conditions: the person is **accused of an offence**; he is **compelled**; and he is made to be a **witness against himself**, that is, to give testimony from personal knowledge.
- *M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra* AIR 1954 SC 300 (eight judges): search and seizure is not compelled testimony; "witness" includes oral and documentary evidence.
- *State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* AIR 1961 SC 1808 (eleven judges): giving specimen handwriting, signatures and thumb impressions is not testimony; the clause covers statements of personal knowledge.
- *Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani* (1978) 2 SCC 424: protection extends to the **investigation stage**; a person may refuse to answer questions that would incriminate him; right to silence; lawyer's presence advisable.
- *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010) 7 SCC 263: **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping** without consent violate Art. 20(3) and Art. 21 (mental privacy).
- *Ritesh Sinha v. State of U.P.* (2019) 8 SCC 1: a magistrate may order a voice sample; like fingerprints, it is not testimony.
- *Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India* (2022): statements under s. 50 PMLA are not hit by Art. 20(3) because the person summoned is not yet an "accused"; the reasoning is criticised.

**Criticism.** Art. 20(2) is narrower than the US Fifth Amendment: it needs a prosecution **and punishment**, so protection against acquittals comes from statute. A narrow meaning of "accused" and "witness" leaves the privilege weak against physical evidence and agency investigations. Coerced statements in custody continue despite *Nandini Satpathy* and *D.K. Basu*.

**Conclusion.** Article 20 gives the accused three fixed protections that no Emergency can remove: certainty of the law at the time of the act, finality after punishment, and freedom from compelled self-incrimination. The case law shows that each is read according to its text, and the later extensions (right to silence, mental privacy) come mainly from Art. 21.

## C-12 · Short notes: (a) Protection against self-incrimination; (b) Doctrine of double jeopardy

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13 ("Write short notes on: (a) Protection against self incrimination (b) Doctrine of double jeopardy") · related: Dec 2022 Q8 (double jeopardy, 10 marks, answered at B-04)
**Marks:** 20 (two notes of 10 marks each, about 450 words each) · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** each note: text 2 · elements 2 · cases 4 · limits and conclusion 2

### Answer

#### (a) Protection against self-incrimination (Art. 20(3)) — 10 marks

**Text and source.** "No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself." The clause is modelled on the Fifth Amendment to the US Constitution. It rests on the principle that the prosecution must prove its case and may not use the accused as a source of proof.

**Three elements.**
1. The person is **accused of an offence**: a formal accusation (FIR or complaint) has been made.
2. He is **compelled** by physical or mental pressure, threat or inducement.
3. He is made **to be a witness against himself**: to give testimony, oral or written, based on his own knowledge that tends to incriminate him.

**Leading cases.**
- *M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra* (1954, eight judges): a search warrant does not compel the accused to be a witness; a document seized is not his testimony.
- *State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961, eleven judges): specimen handwriting, signatures and thumb impressions are material evidence, not testimony. A mere production of a document is not testimony unless the accused is made to state facts.
- *Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani* (1978): the protection applies from the stage of police interrogation; a suspect may keep silent on incriminating questions; the right to silence follows from Arts. 20(3) and 21.
- *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010): involuntary **narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping** violate Art. 20(3) and Art. 21; consent must be informed and voluntary.
- *Ritesh Sinha v. State of U.P.* (2019): a voice sample is like a fingerprint and does not offend the clause.

**Limits.** Fingerprints, blood, DNA, handwriting and voice samples are not covered; a witness who is not an accused is protected by statute instead; the privilege can be waived by choosing to testify. Statutory support: CrPC s. 161(2) (now BNSS s. 180(2)) lets a person decline to answer incriminating questions in police examination.

**Conclusion.** Art. 20(3) protects testimony, not the body. Its modern strength lies in the right to silence read into Arts. 20(3) and 21.

#### (b) Doctrine of double jeopardy (Art. 20(2)) — 10 marks

**Meaning and source.** "No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once." The principle comes from the common-law pleas of *autrefois acquit* and *autrefois convict* and the Fifth Amendment of the US Constitution: a person must not be vexed twice for the same offence.

**Elements.** A person may invoke the clause only if he has been (i) **prosecuted** before a court or a judicial tribunal, (ii) **punished**, (iii) for the **same offence**, and (iv) is again prosecuted and punished.

**Cases.**
- *Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay* AIR 1953 SC 325: Customs confiscation by an administrative authority is not a "prosecution" before a judicial tribunal; a later FERA trial was permitted.
- *S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India* AIR 1954 SC 375: a departmental inquiry followed by a criminal trial is not double jeopardy.
- *State of Bombay v. S.L. Apte* AIR 1961 SC 578: two offences are not the "same" if their ingredients differ (IPC s. 409 and the Insurance Act).
- *Sangeetaben Mahendrabhai Patel v. State of Gujarat* (2012) 7 SCC 621: proceedings under s. 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act and under IPC s. 420 for the same cheque can both go on because the ingredients differ.

**Statutory rule.** CrPC s. 300 (now **BNSS s. 337**) bars a second trial after **conviction or acquittal** on the same facts; the General Clauses Act, 1897, s. 26 allows prosecution under either of two enactments but not punishment twice for the same offence.

**Art. 20(2) and the statutory rule compared.** The Constitution protects only against **prosecution and punishment**; the statute also covers **acquittals**. Art. 20(2) is fundamental and cannot be suspended in an Emergency.

**Conclusion.** The Indian clause is narrower than the American doctrine: the departmental, customs and civil proceedings that are not criminal prosecutions fall outside it, and offences with different ingredients may be tried separately.

<a id="c-13"></a>
## C-13 · Expanding horizons of the right to life and personal liberty (Art. 21)

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q10 ("Discuss the Expanding Horizons of Right to Life and Personal Liberty as guaranteed under Indian Constitution. Refer to decided cases."); related Dec 2023 Q8 (B), Dec 2025 Q10 (C-14).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text and early law 4 · *Maneka Gandhi* 4 · heads of rights with cases 8 · limits and recent developments 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 21 says in one line: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." In 1950 it protected only against executive action without a law. Today it is the source of the widest range of unenumerated rights in the Constitution. It is available to **every person**, and it **cannot be suspended** even in an Emergency (44th Amendment).

**1. The narrow beginning: *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 27.** A preventive detainee argued that Arts. 19, 21 and 22 must be read together. The majority held that "procedure established by law" meant procedure laid down by a validly enacted law; "law" meant State-made law, not natural justice; and Art. 21 was a separate code from Art. 19. A law could be unfair so long as it was duly enacted. *Kharak Singh v. State of U.P.* AIR 1963 SC 1295 widened "life" (quoting *Munn v. Illinois*: more than animal existence) but denied a right to privacy. *Gobind v. State of M.P.* (1975) 2 SCC 148 assumed a limited privacy right.

**2. The turning point: *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) 1 SCC 248.** The passport of Maneka Gandhi was impounded under s. 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967 in the "public interest", without a hearing. A seven-judge bench held:
(i) Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are **interconnected** (the "golden triangle"): a law that deprives a person of personal liberty must satisfy each of them;
(ii) "procedure established by law" must be **fair, just and reasonable**, not arbitrary or oppressive;
(iii) natural justice (*audi alteram partem*) is part of fair procedure;
(iv) "personal liberty" has the widest amplitude.
This imported substantive due process by interpretation and effectively overruled *Gopalan*.

**3. Heads of rights read into Art. 21**

| Head | Leading cases |
|---|---|
| **Human dignity** | *Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Delhi* (1981) 1 SCC 608: the right to live with dignity and its basic necessities. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) 3 SCC 161: release and rehabilitation of bonded labour. |
| **Prisoners' rights; no torture** | *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494; *Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration* (1980) 3 SCC 526 (handcuffing). *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416 (arrest guidelines). |
| **Speedy trial and legal aid** | *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar* (1980) 1 SCC 81; *M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra* (1978) 3 SCC 544; *Khatri v. State of Bihar* (1981) 1 SCC 627. |
| **Compensation** | *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141; *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) 2 SCC 746. |
| **Livelihood and shelter** | *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) 3 SCC 545; *Chameli Singh v. State of U.P.* (1996) 2 SCC 549. |
| **Health** | *Parmanand Katara v. Union of India* (1989) 4 SCC 286; *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of W.B.* (1996) 4 SCC 37. |
| **Environment** | *Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar* (1991) 1 SCC 598; *M.C. Mehta* (1987) 1 SCC 395; *Vellore Citizens* (1996) 5 SCC 647 (see C-15). |
| **Workplace dignity of women** | *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241; now the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013. |
| **Privacy** | *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1 (see C-16). |
| **Identity and autonomy** | *NALSA v. Union of India* (2014) 5 SCC 438 (transgender persons); *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 (s. 377 read down); *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2019) 3 SCC 39 (adultery, IPC s. 497, struck down). |
| **Education** | *Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992) 3 SCC 666; *Unni Krishnan v. State of A.P.* (1993) 1 SCC 645; Art. 21A (86th Amendment, 2002). |
| **Death with dignity** | *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996) 2 SCC 648; *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1 (passive euthanasia and living will). |

**4. Death penalty and Art. 21.** *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) 2 SCC 684 upheld the death penalty but only in the "rarest of rare" cases; *Mithu v. State of Punjab* (1983) 2 SCC 277 struck down the mandatory death penalty (IPC s. 303); *Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014) 3 SCC 1 held that unexplained delay in deciding a mercy petition can justify commutation.

**5. Limits.** Deprivation is allowed by a **valid law with fair procedure**: arrest, imprisonment, preventive detention and the death penalty in the rarest of cases. *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) 2 SCC 521 (4:1, Khanna J. dissenting) held that no habeas corpus lay during the Emergency; the 44th Amendment (1978) made Arts. 20 and 21 non-suspendable, and *Puttaswamy* (2017) declared *ADM Jabalpur* wrongly decided.

**6. Recent developments.** *Puttaswamy* (2017); *Navtej* and *Common Cause* (2018); *M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (21 March 2024), which recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change (Arts. 14 and 21); the Court's directions on demolitions (13 November 2024); and *Harish Rana* (11 March 2026, as reported), which applied the *Common Cause* framework to allow withdrawal of life support.

**Criticism.** Critics say the Court has legislated by creating rights that legislatures should enact, and that rights such as shelter and health are weakly enforced. Preventive detention, UAPA and PMLA bail conditions still limit liberty, and undertrial detention remains common.

**Conclusion.** From *Gopalan* to *Maneka Gandhi*, Art. 21 changed from a shield against arbitrary executive action into a guarantee that every deprivation of life and liberty must be by a fair, just and reasonable law. Its horizon has widened so far that it is called the heart of the Fundamental Rights.

## C-14 · Right to life means life with human dignity

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q10 ("Right to life does not mean mere animal existence but it means right to live with human dignity. Discuss with the help of decided cases.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and source 4 · cases by head 10 · limits 2 · recent and criticism 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The statement comes from a line of cases that began with *Kharak Singh v. State of U.P.* (AIR 1963 SC 1295), where Subba Rao J., quoting *Munn v. Illinois*, said that "life" means more than animal existence: it includes all those limbs and faculties by which life is enjoyed. In *Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi* (1981) 1 SCC 608, Bhagwati J. held that the right to life includes the right to live with **human dignity** and the bare necessities of life: adequate nutrition, clothing and shelter, and facilities for reading, writing and expressing oneself in diverse forms.

**Source of dignity.** The Preamble speaks of "the dignity of the individual". *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) 1 SCC 248 made Art. 21 a guarantee of fair, just and reasonable procedure, which is the door through which dignity entered.

**Dignity in the cases**

| Aspect of dignity | Case and holding |
|---|---|
| **Freedom from torture and cruel treatment** | *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494: prisoners retain their fundamental rights; solitary confinement and bar fetters are inhuman. *Prem Shankar Shukla v. Delhi Administration* (1980) 3 SCC 526: handcuffing is allowed only for compelling reasons. *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416: arrest guidelines against custodial violence. |
| **Freedom from bondage** | *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) 3 SCC 161: release and rehabilitation of bonded labourers; the right to live with dignity includes protection of health and freedom from exploitation. |
| **Livelihood** | *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) 3 SCC 545: the right to life includes livelihood; pavement dwellers cannot be evicted without notice and a hearing, because deprivation of livelihood deprives life itself. |
| **Shelter** | *Chameli Singh v. State of U.P.* (1996) 2 SCC 549: shelter is part of the right to life. |
| **Health and medical aid** | *Parmanand Katara v. Union of India* (1989) 4 SCC 286: immediate medical aid to an injured person; *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity* (1996) 4 SCC 37: a government hospital cannot deny emergency treatment. |
| **Women's dignity at work** | *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241: sexual harassment violates Arts. 14, 15, 19 and 21; guidelines became the Act of 2013. |
| **Identity and sexuality** | *NALSA v. Union of India* (2014) 5 SCC 438; *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1: dignity includes self-identity and sexual orientation; constitutional morality prevails over social morality. |
| **Privacy and autonomy** | *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1: dignity is the core of privacy. |
| **Dignity in dying** | *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1: a person's right to die with dignity (passive euthanasia, living will) is part of Art. 21. *Gian Kaur* (1996) held the right to life does not include a right to die, but a dignified natural death is different. |
| **Dignity after death** | *Parmanand Katara* (1995) 3 SCC 248 and later cases: the right to dignity extends to a dead body; *Ashray Adhikar Abhiyan v. Union of India* (2002) 2 SCC 27 on shelters for the homeless. |
| **Environment and clean water** | *Subhash Kumar* (1991) 1 SCC 598: pollution-free air and water form part of the right to life. |

**Limits.** The right is not absolute. A person may be deprived of life or liberty by a valid law with fair procedure (arrest, detention, the death penalty in the rarest cases: *Bachan Singh*, 1980). Dignity is also a standard against which the State's treatment of the accused is tested, as in *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010), where involuntary narco-analysis was held to violate mental privacy and dignity.

**Recent developments.** *Puttaswamy* (2017) put dignity at the centre of the Constitution; *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024) recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change; the Court's demolition guidelines of 13 November 2024 tied the right to shelter to due process; *Harish Rana* (11 March 2026, as reported) addressed the dignity of a patient in a permanent vegetative state.

**Criticism.** The language of dignity is flexible and may appear without a clear test; many dignity rights remain declarations without enforcement (shelter, health, nutrition); the Court has sometimes preferred directions to the executive over a remedy for the individual.

**Conclusion.** The statement is correct. "Life" in Art. 21 is not mere survival but a life of dignity, with food, shelter, health, security and respect for autonomy and identity. The Court has turned a short clause into a guarantee of the conditions that make life worth living.

## C-15 · Environment protection and Article 21

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q10 and Dec 2022 Q9 (same words: "Environment protection structure is the result of extended interpretation of Article 21. Discuss.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** constitutional and statutory background 4 · cases by principle 10 · critical evaluation 3 · conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** The original Constitution did not mention the environment. Provisions were added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**: Art. 48A (State to protect and improve the environment) and Art. 51A(g) (citizens' duty to protect the natural environment). Both are non-justiciable. The Supreme Court made the environment enforceable by reading a **right to a healthy environment into Art. 21**, then built principles and institutions on that right. The statement in the question is therefore correct.

**1. From the statutes to the right.** The Water Act, 1974, Air Act, 1981 and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 (after the Bhopal gas leak of 1984) provided statutory control. The Court filled their gaps through Arts. 21 and 32.

**2. The right to a wholesome environment**
- *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P.* (1985) 2 SCC 431: the first case where the Court ordered closure of limestone quarries in the Doon Valley, weighing ecological balance against employment.
- *Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar* (1991) 1 SCC 598: the right to life includes the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air.
- *Hinch Lal Tiwari v. Kamala Devi* (2001) 6 SCC 496: protection of ponds and the environment as part of Art. 21.

**3. Principles evolved**

| Principle | Case |
|---|---|
| **Absolute liability** (an enterprise engaged in a hazardous activity is absolutely liable, without exceptions such as act of God) | *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (Oleum Gas Leak case) (1987) 1 SCC 395: the Court declined to apply the *Rylands v. Fletcher* exceptions in India. |
| **Precautionary principle** and **polluter pays** | *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) 5 SCC 647: both are part of Indian environmental law; sustainable development accepted. *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) 3 SCC 212 (Bichhri case): the polluter must pay for restoration. *A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu* (1999) 2 SCC 718: burden on the developer to show the activity is safe. |
| **Public trust doctrine** | *M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath* (1997) 1 SCC 388: natural resources are held by the State in trust for the public. |
| **Sustainable development; intergenerational equity** | *Narmada Bachao Andolan v. Union of India* (2000) 10 SCC 664 (the Court balanced development and displacement); *State of H.P. v. Ganesh Wood Products* (1995) 6 SCC 363. |
| **Monitoring through continuing mandamus** | *T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India* (1997) 2 SCC 267 (forests); *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1997) 2 SCC 353 (the Taj Trapezium). |
| **Noise pollution as an Art. 21 injury** | *In re Noise Pollution* (2005) 5 SCC 733: the right to sleep and to a peaceful life. |
| **Animals and nature** | *Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja* (2014) 7 SCC 547: animals have a right to live with dignity. |
| **Climate** | *M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (21 March 2024): a right against the adverse effects of climate change flows from Arts. 14 and 21. |

**4. Institutions.** The **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** created the National Green Tribunal, which applies the polluter-pays, precautionary and sustainable-development principles (s. 20). It followed repeated suggestions by the Supreme Court and the Law Commission (186th Report, 2003) for specialised environmental courts.

**5. Evaluation**
1. *Strengths:* a right to the environment that is enforceable without a statute; novel remedies (continuing mandamus, expert committees, compensation); principles now binding as law.
2. *Weaknesses:* orders on development and livelihood are made without a consistent test (compare *Doon Valley* with *Narmada*); judicial supervision may displace elected bodies; implementation by agencies remains poor; rights of the poor are sometimes overlooked in "clean-up" orders.

**Conclusion.** The statement is right: it was the extended interpretation of Art. 21, aided by the DPSP in Art. 48A and the duty in Art. 51A(g), that gave India its constitutional environmental law. Today the law rests on rights, principles (precaution, polluter pays, public trust) and a specialised tribunal, and the challenge lies in enforcement rather than doctrine.

## C-16 · The right to privacy

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025 as a stand-alone question. Included because it is the most important Art. 21 development after 2017 and is likely to be asked.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** early law 4 · *Puttaswamy* facts, issues and holdings 8 · test and applications 4 · statute and criticism 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Constitution does not use the word "privacy". Its status as a fundamental right was settled only on **24 August 2017** in *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1, by a nine-judge bench.

**1. Early law**
- *M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra* AIR 1954 SC 300 (eight judges): search and seizure; the Court declined to import a privacy right.
- *Kharak Singh v. State of U.P.* AIR 1963 SC 1295 (six judges): night domiciliary visits struck down, but the majority held that privacy was not a fundamental right; Subba Rao J. dissented.
- *Gobind v. State of M.P.* (1975) 2 SCC 148 (three judges): a limited privacy right assumed, subject to a compelling State interest.
- *R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N.* (1994) 6 SCC 632: the right to be left alone as part of Art. 21. *PUCL v. Union of India* (1997) 1 SCC 301: telephone tapping needs procedural safeguards.

**2. *Puttaswamy*: facts, issue and holding**
- **Facts.** A retired High Court judge, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy, challenged the Aadhaar scheme. The Union argued, relying on *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh*, that there is no fundamental right to privacy. The matter was referred to a nine-judge bench to decide that question.
- **Issue.** Is there a fundamental right to privacy under the Constitution, and what is its source?
- **Holding.** Unanimously, **yes**. Privacy is an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Art. 21 and of the freedoms in Part III as a whole (Arts. 14, 19 and 21). *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* were overruled to the extent they held otherwise, and *ADM Jabalpur* was declared wrongly decided. Privacy includes **bodily integrity, decisional autonomy and informational privacy**; it is not absolute.
- **Test for any intrusion** (from the opinions of Chandrachud and Kaul JJ.): (i) **legality**, a law must authorise it; (ii) a **legitimate aim**; (iii) **proportionality**, a rational nexus and the least intrusive means.

**3. Applications after 2017**
- *Puttaswamy (II)* (2019) 1 SCC 1 (26 September 2018): Aadhaar upheld for welfare and PAN linking; s. 57 (use by private entities) struck down; compulsory linking with bank accounts and mobile numbers rejected.
- *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 and *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2019) 3 SCC 39: privacy and autonomy support decriminalisation of consensual same-sex conduct and adultery.
- *Common Cause* (2018): bodily integrity in the decision to refuse treatment.
- *Selvi* (2010) is an earlier case on mental privacy.

**4. Statute.** The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** (assent 11 August 2023) and the DPDP Rules, 2025 regulate processing of digital personal data; their main obligations are being phased in (the dates reported are 13 November 2025, 12 November 2026 and 12 May 2027, which should be checked in the Gazette). Critics point to broad exemptions for the State and the change to the Right to Information Act (s. 44(3)).

**5. Criticism.** The right is defined in broad terms but its limits are being developed case by case; surveillance, Aadhaar linking and data sharing continue; "proportionality" is applied unevenly.

**Conclusion.** *Puttaswamy* is a landmark because it restored the dignity-based reading of Art. 21 and overruled *ADM Jabalpur*. Privacy now supports decisions on identity, marriage, health and data, and every State intrusion must meet the tests of law, legitimate aim and proportionality.

## C-17 · Right to education: Art. 21A and the RTE Act, 2009

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q13 ("Write short essay on Right to Education."); Dec 2024 Q4 (A, 4 marks)
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** constitutional development 6 · RTE Act features 5 · cases 5 · criticism and recent 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Education was originally only a **Directive Principle**: Art. 45 required the State to provide free and compulsory education to children until the age of fourteen within ten years of the Constitution. The Supreme Court first made it a right by interpretation; the **86th Amendment Act, 2002** made it a fundamental right.

**1. Judicial development**
- *Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka* (1992) 3 SCC 666: the right to education flows from the right to life; capitation fees held unconstitutional.
- *Unni Krishnan J.P. v. State of A.P.* (1993) 1 SCC 645 (five judges): the right to free education exists for every child until the age of fourteen; after that it is subject to the State's economic capacity (Arts. 41, 45). The scheme the Court framed for private professional colleges was overruled in *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka* (2002) 8 SCC 481.

**2. Constitutional provisions after 2002**
- **Art. 21A** (inserted by the 86th Amendment): "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the State may, by law, determine."
- **Art. 45** (substituted): early childhood care and education for children below six.
- **Art. 51A(k)** (inserted): a duty of a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child aged six to fourteen.
- **Art. 15(5)** (93rd Amendment, 2005): reservation in educational institutions, including private unaided ones (except minority institutions).

**3. Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009** (in force from 1 April 2010)
- Free and compulsory elementary education for children aged 6–14 in a **neighbourhood school** (s. 3).
- Duties on the appropriate Government and local authority (ss. 6, 8, 9).
- **25% reservation** of seats in Class I in private unaided schools for children from weaker and disadvantaged groups, with reimbursement by the State (s. 12(1)(c)).
- No capitation fee and no screening (s. 13); no expulsion or holding back till completion of elementary education (s. 16); the 2019 amendment lets States permit holding back in classes V and VIII after a re-examination.
- Norms for pupil–teacher ratio, teacher qualifications and infrastructure; School Management Committees (s. 21); monitoring by the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights.

**4. Case law on the Act**
- *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (2012) 6 SCC 1: the Act and the 25% quota upheld for non-minority unaided and aided schools; minority schools outside its scope.
- *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India* (2014) 8 SCC 1 (Constitution Bench): RTE Act does not apply to **minority** institutions (aided or unaided); Art. 15(5) upheld.
- In the order of 1 September 2025 in *Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust v. State of Maharashtra* (as reported), a two-judge bench doubted the blanket exclusion of minority schools laid down in *Pramati* and referred the question to a larger bench; its present status should be checked.

**5. Criticism.**
1. The right covers only children **aged 6 to 14**. Children below six and between fifteen and eighteen are outside Art. 21A.
2. Implementation gaps: teacher vacancies, poor learning outcomes, infrastructure.
3. Disputes over reimbursement and eligibility under the 25% quota.
4. Exclusion of minority institutions.
**Strengths:** the right is justiciable; the statute gives enforceable duties; and the National Education Policy, 2020 widens the focus on foundational learning.

**Conclusion.** India has moved from an unenforceable directive to a fundamental right and a detailed statute. The challenge is not law but quality and reach: the Court's decisions have secured the right in principle, and State action must make it real for every child.
