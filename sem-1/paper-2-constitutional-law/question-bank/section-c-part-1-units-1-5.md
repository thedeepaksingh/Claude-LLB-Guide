## C-01 · Federal structure of the Constitution and the form of government

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q6 (B, 10 marks: "What do you mean by Federalism? Is Indian Constitution federal? Discuss."); Dec 2024 Q6 (B: "neither federal nor unitary but quasi-federal. Do you agree?"); Dec 2022 Q2, Dec 2023 Q3, Dec 2024 Q2 (A). The syllabus line is "Federal Structure; Form of Government", so a 20-mark question is likely. The 10-mark versions are B-01 and B-02.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and thesis 3 · federal features 4 · unitary features 4 · cases 5 · form of government 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The word "federal" does not appear in the Constitution. Article 1(1) says "India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**". Dr Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly (4 November 1948) that the drafters chose "Union" because the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement between States and no State has a right to secede; the federation is a Union because it is indestructible.

**Meaning of federalism.** A federation divides powers between a general government and regional governments, each independent in its own sphere, under a written constitution interpreted by an independent judiciary (K.C. Wheare; Dicey). Wheare called the Indian Constitution **quasi-federal**: a unitary State with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal State with subsidiary unitary features. Granville Austin called it **cooperative federalism**.

**Federal features**
1. **Written and supreme Constitution** (Arts. 13, 245).
2. **Dual polity**: the Union (Part V) and the States (Part VI).
3. **Division of powers**: Art. 246 and the Seventh Schedule (Union, State and Concurrent Lists); executive power divided by Arts. 73 and 162; finances shared (Arts. 268–293, Finance Commission, Art. 280).
4. **Rigid amendment of federal provisions**: ratification by half the States under the proviso to Art. 368(2).
5. **Independent judiciary** with power to decide Union–State disputes (Art. 131) and judicial review.
6. **Bicameral Parliament** with a Council of States (Art. 80).
7. **Institutions of cooperation**: Inter-State Council (Art. 263), GST Council (Art. 279A, 101st Amendment), water-disputes machinery (Art. 262).

**Unitary (centralising) features**
1. **Parliament can alter the area, boundaries and name of any State** by ordinary law (Art. 3): "an indestructible Union of destructible States" (*Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1960 SC 51).
2. **Strong Centre in legislation**: the Union List prevails (Art. 246(1)); **residuary power** with Parliament (Art. 248); Union law prevails on the Concurrent List (Art. 254); Parliament may legislate on State subjects in the national interest (Art. 249), during an Emergency (Art. 250), by agreement (Art. 252) and to implement treaties (Art. 253).
3. **Emergency provisions** (Arts. 352, 356, 360) turn the system into a unitary one.
4. **Governor is appointed by the President** (Arts. 153–156) and may reserve Bills for the President (Art. 200).
5. **Union directions to States** (Arts. 256, 257); **All-India Services** (Art. 312); **single citizenship**; integrated judiciary; a single Election Commission and a single Comptroller and Auditor-General.
6. **Financial dependence** of States on the Centre (grants-in-aid, Art. 275).
7. **Unequal representation** of States in the Rajya Sabha.

**What the courts have said**

| Case | Principle |
|---|---|
| *State of W.B. v. Union of India* AIR 1963 SC 1241 | India is **not a federation in the strict sense**; States are not sovereign; Parliament may affect their territory; quasi-federal. |
| *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) 4 SCC 225 | The **federal character** is part of the basic structure. |
| *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) 3 SCC 1 (nine judges) | **Federalism is part of the basic structure**; States are not mere appendages of the Centre; a proclamation under Art. 356 is **justiciable** (review for relevant material and mala fides); the Assembly must not be dissolved before Parliament approves; the majority is tested **on the floor of the House**; the Court may restore a dismissed government. |
| *Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006) 2 SCC 1 | Dissolution of the Bihar Assembly (2005) unconstitutional. |
| *Govt. of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India* (2018) 8 SCC 501 and (2023) 9 SCC 1 | Federalism is **cooperative**; the elected government of Delhi has control over "services" within its legislative field; the Lieutenant-Governor is bound by aid and advice except in matters outside the government's competence. |
| *Union of India v. Mohit Minerals* (2022) 10 SCC 700 | GST Council recommendations are **persuasive, not binding**; Union and States have simultaneous power over GST. |
| *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu* (8 April 2025) | A Governor cannot hold Bills back indefinitely; the Court treated ten re-passed Bills as assented to (Art. 142). |
| Presidential Reference (Art. 143), opinion of 20 November 2025 (as reported) | The Court cannot fix timelines for Governors and the President or declare "deemed assent", but Governors may not sit on Bills indefinitely. |

**Asymmetric federalism.** Arts. 371 to 371J give special provisions to States such as Nagaland, Mizoram, Assam, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Karnataka. In *In re Article 370* (11 December 2023) a five-judge bench upheld the abrogation of Art. 370 and accepted that the Constitution allows asymmetric federalism.

**Form of government.** The Constitution adopts the **parliamentary system** at both levels. The President is the constitutional head (Arts. 52–53); real power lies with the Council of Ministers under the Prime Minister (Art. 74(1): the President acts on aid and advice, with one right of reconsideration after the 44th Amendment); the Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)). In the States the Governor is the nominal head (Art. 163). *Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (AIR 1955 SC 549) and *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab* (1974) 2 SCC 831 hold that the President and Governors act on ministerial advice except where the Constitution gives discretion. Separation of powers is functional rather than rigid, and is part of the basic structure (*Kesavananda*; *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975).

**Commissions.** The Sarkaria Commission (1988) advised a strong Centre with Art. 356 as a last resort and a permanent Inter-State Council; the Punchhi Commission (2010) made proposals on Governors and Art. 356.

**Conclusion.** The Constitution is **federal in structure but unitary in spirit**: a federation with a strong Centre which, in an Emergency, works as a unitary State. Since *Bommai* the Court treats federalism as a basic feature, and recent decisions on Delhi's services, GST and Governors show that it enforces limits on the Centre, though not in the rigid American sense. The answer to the question is therefore "quasi-federal, with federalism as an enforceable basic feature".

## C-02 · The Preamble

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q6 (B: "Preamble gives direction and purpose to the Constitution"); Dec 2023 Q5, Dec 2024 Q1, Dec 2025 Q1 (A). A 20-mark question on the Preamble is the standard form.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text and background 4 · meaning of key words 6 · part of Constitution and amendability with cases 6 · conclusion 4 (uses and criticism)

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Preamble is the opening statement of the Constitution. It declares who made it ("We, the People of India"), what the State is, and what it aims to secure. It is based on the **Objectives Resolution** moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947.

**Text (as amended).** "We, the People of India, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic** and to secure to all its citizens: **Justice**, social, economic and political; **Liberty** of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; **Equality** of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all **Fraternity** assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation; in our Constituent Assembly this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do hereby adopt, enact and give to ourselves this Constitution."

**Amendment.** Only once: the **42nd Amendment (1976)** added "Socialist", "Secular" and "integrity".

**Meaning of key words**
1. **We, the People:** the source of authority is the people, not Parliament or any outside power. (The Assembly was indirectly elected, so the phrase is also a statement of principle.)
2. **Sovereign:** free of external control; internally, power flows from the Constitution.
3. **Socialist:** Indian socialism is democratic and welfare-oriented: reducing inequality (Arts. 38, 39), not nationalisation of all means of production. *D.S. Nakara v. Union of India* (1983) 1 SCC 305 used it to strike down an arbitrary pensioner classification. *Dr Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024) held that "socialism" means economic and social justice and a welfare State, not hostility to private enterprise.
4. **Secular:** the State has no religion and treats all faiths equally; Indian secularism allows State regulation and reform of religion. It is part of the basic structure (*Kesavananda*; *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) 3 SCC 1; *Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India* (1994) 6 SCC 360).
5. **Democratic:** government by the will of the people through free and fair elections (*Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975; *Union of India v. ADR*, 2002).
6. **Republic:** the head of State is elected, not hereditary (Arts. 54–55).
7. **Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity:** implemented through Arts. 14–18, 19–22, 25–28, 38–39. Fraternity assures **the dignity of the individual** and the **unity and integrity** of the nation.

**Is the Preamble a part of the Constitution?**
- *In re Berubari Union* AIR 1960 SC 845: **not a part**; it is a key to the minds of the framers but is not a source of power or a limitation on power.
- *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) 4 SCC 225: **it is a part** of the Constitution; *Berubari* was not followed. It contains the basic features and helps interpret ambiguous provisions.
- *LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995) 5 SCC 482: the Preamble is an **integral part** of the Constitution.
- *Dr Balram Singh v. Union of India* (25 November 2024): Parliament's power under Art. 368 **extends to the Preamble**; the date "26 November 1949" does not bar amendment; a challenge to the 1976 insertion, brought some 44 years later, was dismissed.

**Settled position.** The Preamble is a part of the Constitution. It is not directly enforceable and cannot override an express provision (*Berubari*); it may be amended under Art. 368 without altering the basic structure (*Kesavananda*; *Balram Singh*).

**Uses.** It is an aid to interpretation where words are ambiguous; it is a source of the basic structure; it declares the source of authority and the objects of the State.

**Criticism.** The Preamble has no legal force of its own. The words "socialist" and "secular" were added during the Emergency and were debated; K.T. Shah's amendments to insert them were rejected in 1948. The Court has held, however, that the values were already in the original text (Arts. 25–28, 38–39).

**Conclusion.** The Preamble is the philosophy of the Constitution in short. Its ideals of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity guide interpretation, and through *Kesavananda* they have become limits on the amending power. A question asking "Does the Preamble give direction and purpose?" is answered yes: it does, but only through the provisions that carry its ideals into law.

## C-03 · Against whom Fundamental Rights are enforceable; the remedies available

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q13 ("Against whom can the fundamental rights be enforced? What remedies are available to a person to enforce fundamental rights? Discuss."); related Dec 2025 Q3 (A: "Against whom Fundamental Rights are available?")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** State under Art. 12 and tests 7 · private persons 3 · remedies (Arts. 32, 226, writs) 7 · conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Fundamental Rights in Part III are chiefly **restraints on the State**. Article 12 says who is "the State"; Arts. 32 and 226 say how rights are enforced.

**Part I. Against whom?**

**(1) The State (Art. 12).** The term "State" includes: the Government and Parliament of India; the Government and Legislature of each State; and **all local or other authorities** within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.
- **Local authorities:** municipalities, panchayats, port trusts and similar bodies.
- **"Other authorities":** the courts first limited it by *ejusdem generis* (governmental functions only). *Rajasthan SEB v. Mohan Lal* AIR 1967 SC 1857 rejected that limit and included authorities with power to make rules and regulations having the force of law. *Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram* (1975) 1 SCC 421 held that statutory corporations (ONGC, LIC, IFC) are "State". *R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority* (1979) 3 SCC 489 and *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib* (1981) 1 SCC 722 developed the **instrumentality test**: State holding of share capital, financial assistance meeting almost all expenditure, monopoly status, deep and pervasive control, public-importance functions, transfer of a government department. *Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. IICB* (2002) 5 SCC 111 (seven judges) overruled *Sabhajit Tewary* (1975) and held that the test is whether the body is **financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of the Government**.
- **Not "State":** a private body such as the BCCI (*Zee Telefilms*, 2005) although it may be amenable to Art. 226 for public functions.
- **Judiciary:** its administrative functions are State action; a judicial order is not "law" and cannot be challenged under Art. 32 (*Naresh Mirajkar*, AIR 1967 SC 1), but the **curative petition** is available (*Rupa Ashok Hurra*, (2002) 4 SCC 388).

**(2) Private persons.** In principle rights are enforceable only against the State (**vertical** operation). The Constitution itself binds private persons under **Art. 15(2)** (access to shops and public places), **Art. 17** (untouchability), **Art. 23** (forced labour and traffic in human beings: *PUDR v. Union of India*, (1982) 3 SCC 235) and **Art. 24** (child labour). *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241 laid down binding guidelines for all workplaces. In *Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P.* (3 January 2023) a Constitution Bench held that Arts. 19 and 21 can be enforced against **non-State actors** also, and that the State has a positive duty to protect those rights. Art. 226 lets a High Court issue writs to "any person or authority" and a private body performing a public function can be reached (*Andi Mukta Sadguru v. V.R. Rudani*, (1989) 2 SCC 691).

**Part II. Remedies**

**(1) Article 32: the right to constitutional remedies.** The right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Part III rights is itself a fundamental right. Dr Ambedkar called it "the heart and soul" of the Constitution. Under Art. 32(2) the Court may issue **directions, orders or writs**, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto. Art. 32(3) lets Parliament empower other courts; Art. 32(4) bars suspension except as provided in the Constitution (Art. 359).
- *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 124: the Court is the protector of fundamental rights and cannot refuse relief.
- *Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner* AIR 1963 SC 996: a rule requiring security for filing a petition under Art. 32 was struck down.
- *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 and *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) 2 SCC 746: **compensation** under public law for violation of Art. 21.
- *Daryao v. State of U.P.* AIR 1961 SC 1457: res judicata applies to Art. 32 petitions after a decision on merits by a High Court under Art. 226.

**(2) Article 226: High Courts.** A High Court may issue writs for enforcement of fundamental rights **and for any other purpose**. The jurisdiction is wider than Art. 32. Judicial review by High Courts is part of the basic structure (*L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261).

**(3) The five writs.** *Habeas corpus* (production of a person unlawfully detained); *mandamus* (command to perform a public duty); *prohibition* (forbids an inferior court from exceeding jurisdiction); *certiorari* (quashes an order of an inferior body); *quo warranto* (tests title to a public office).

**(4) Other remedies.** Art. 13 (laws inconsistent with Part III are void); Public Interest Litigation (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981); statutory remedies against private persons (Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955; Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976); the National and State Human Rights Commissions (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993); ordinary civil and criminal remedies.

**Limits.** Art. 359 allows the President to suspend the right to move courts for rights he specifies during an Emergency; since the 44th Amendment Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be so suspended. Art. 33 restricts the rights of the armed forces; Art. 34 applies during martial law.

**Conclusion.** Fundamental Rights run mainly against the State in its widest sense, extended by the instrumentality test and, in a few areas, against private persons. The remedies are strong: the Supreme Court and High Courts can issue writs, award compensation and, through PIL, give relief on behalf of those who cannot approach the Court.

## C-04 · Doctrines of eclipse and severability (Article 13)

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q10 ("Explain with the help of decided cases, the 'doctrine of eclipse' and the 'doctrine of severability'.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Art. 13 text and background 4 · eclipse with cases 7 · severability with cases 7 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 13 makes Part III supreme over ordinary law. **Art. 13(1)** declares all pre-Constitution laws void **to the extent of** their inconsistency with fundamental rights. **Art. 13(2)** forbids the State from making any law that takes away or abridges those rights, and such law is void to the extent of the contravention. **Art. 13(3)** defines "law" widely (ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, custom or usage having the force of law). **Art. 13(4)** (24th Amendment, 1971) excludes constitutional amendments from Art. 13.

The words "to the extent of the inconsistency" are the root of both doctrines.

**1. Doctrine of eclipse.**
*Meaning.* A law that is inconsistent with a fundamental right is not **dead** but **overshadowed**. It is unenforceable while the inconsistency lasts; if the inconsistency is removed, the law **revives** without re-enactment, as the shadow lifts.

*Cases.*
- *Keshav Madhav Menon v. State of Bombay* AIR 1951 SC 128: a pre-Constitution law that violated Art. 19 was not void for acts done **before 26 January 1950**; Art. 13(1) operates prospectively and the law remains in the statute book for past transactions.
- *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of M.P.* AIR 1955 SC 781: the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947 of C.P. and Berar became inoperative on 26 January 1950 because it conflicted with Art. 19(1)(g). The **First Amendment (1951)** removed the conflict; the law **revived** without re-enactment. It was "eclipsed", not void *ab initio*.
- *Deep Chand v. State of U.P.* AIR 1959 SC 648: a **post-Constitution** law that contravenes Part III is **stillborn** (void *ab initio*) and cannot revive.
- *Mahendra Lal Jaini v. State of U.P.* AIR 1963 SC 1019: the Court held that eclipse also applies to a post-Constitution law that infringes a right which only citizens enjoy (Art. 19); such a law is void only to the extent of the conflict and is not wiped out.
- *State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills* (1974) 4 SCC 656: a law violating only citizens' rights (Art. 19) is not void for **non-citizens**, who cannot claim Art. 19. It is eclipsed only as against citizens.

*Result.* Eclipse applies to **pre-Constitution laws** (Art. 13(1)) and, for rights that only citizens enjoy, to post-Constitution laws too (*Jaini*; *Ambica Mills*). A post-Constitution law that violates a right enjoyed by every person is **stillborn** (*Deep Chand*).

**2. Doctrine of severability (separability).**
*Meaning.* When only a **part** of a law is inconsistent with Part III, only that part is void if it can be **separated** from the rest. If the valid and invalid parts are inseparable, the whole law falls.

*Cases and tests.*
- *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 27: s. 14 of the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 was void, but the remainder of the Act stood.
- *State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara* AIR 1951 SC 318: parts of the Bombay Prohibition Act that violated Art. 19(1)(f) were severed.
- *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v. Union of India* AIR 1957 SC 628: the tests: (i) the **intention of the legislature**: would it have enacted the valid part without the invalid? (ii) whether the valid and invalid parts are so mixed up that they cannot be separated; (iii) whether, after severance, the rest forms a **complete code** and gives effect to the object of the Act; (iv) whether severance leaves a law different in character from the one intended. The Court also held that a statute may be read down.
- The rule also applies to **constitutional amendments**: *Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu* (1992) Supp (2) SCC 651 struck down para 7 of the Tenth Schedule (ouster of judicial review) as invalid for want of ratification but severed it and upheld the rest.

**3. Related doctrine: waiver.** A fundamental right cannot be waived by a person, because rights rest on public policy (*Basheshar Nath v. CIT*, AIR 1959 SC 149; *Olga Tellis*, (1985) 3 SCC 545: there is no estoppel against the Constitution).

**4. Eclipse and severability compared**

| Basis | Eclipse | Severability |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Is the law dead or merely dormant? | Which part of the law is bad? |
| Effect | The whole inconsistent law is unenforceable until the conflict is removed | Only the offending part is void; the rest survives |
| Cure | Removing the inconsistency (e.g. amendment) revives the law | Not needed; the valid part always stood |
| Main cases | *Keshav Madhav Menon*; *Bhikaji Narain*; *Deep Chand*; *Ambica Mills* | *Gopalan*; *Balsara*; *Chamarbaugwalla* |

**Conclusion.** Both doctrines flow from the words "to the extent of the inconsistency" in Art. 13. Eclipse limits the loss to the period of the conflict, and severability limits the loss to the offending part. They save as much legislation as the Constitution permits, while the supremacy of Part III remains untouched.

## C-05 · Can Fundamental Rights be amended? The basic structure doctrine

**Asked as:** not asked in full in Dec 2022–2025 (it is the unit's standard question); *Kesavananda* and the basic structure feature in many answers. Related: Dec 2022 Q1 (A: "fundamental law"), Dec 2023 Q1 (A).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** problem and text 3 · chain of cases 9 · basic structure content 4 · later applications 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 13(2) bars the State from making any "law" that takes away or abridges fundamental rights; Art. 368 gives Parliament power to amend the Constitution. The question is whether a constitutional amendment is a "law" under Art. 13(2). The answer has changed in four stages.

**1. Parliament can amend Part III: *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965).**
*Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India* AIR 1951 SC 458: the First Amendment (which curtailed the right to property) was upheld; "law" in Art. 13(2) means ordinary law made in exercise of legislative power and not a constitutional amendment under Art. 368. *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* AIR 1965 SC 845 (3:2) followed it, though Hidayatullah and Mudholkar JJ. doubted it.

**2. Parliament cannot amend Part III: *Golaknath* (1967).**
*I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* AIR 1967 SC 1643 (eleven judges, 6:5): an amendment **is** "law" under Art. 13(2); Parliament has no power to take away or abridge fundamental rights. The Court applied **prospective overruling**, so earlier amendments stayed valid.

**3. Parliament's response: 24th and 25th Amendments (1971).** The 24th Amendment inserted **Art. 13(4)** (Art. 13 does not apply to Art. 368 amendments) and amended Art. 368. The 25th inserted **Art. 31C** (laws for Art. 39(b) and (c) immune from Arts. 14, 19 and 31).

**4. The settlement: *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) 4 SCC 225.** A bench of thirteen judges (the largest in the Court's history) decided by 7:6:
- *Golaknath* was **overruled**; Parliament can amend any provision of the Constitution, including Part III;
- but it **cannot alter the basic structure or framework** of the Constitution;
- the first part of Art. 31C was upheld and the second part (excluding judicial review) was struck down.
The Court did not list the basic features exhaustively. Sikri CJ named the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form, the secular character, the separation of powers and the federal character; other judges added further features. The view of Khanna J., that an amendment may change but not destroy the identity of the Constitution, decided the balance.

**5. Content of the basic structure (as recognised by courts).** Supremacy of the Constitution; republican and democratic form; secularism; federalism; separation of powers; unity and integrity; sovereignty; rule of law; judicial review; free and fair elections; independence of the judiciary; the essence of fundamental rights; the balance between Parts III and IV; the limited nature of the amending power. The list is open.

**6. Application after *Kesavananda***
- *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) Supp SCC 1: clause (4) of Art. 329A (inserted by the 39th Amendment to validate the Prime Minister's election) was struck down; free and fair elections, rule of law and separation of powers are basic.
- *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (1980) 3 SCC 625: sections 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment were struck down; **limited amending power is itself basic**, and the harmony of Parts III and IV is part of the basic structure.
- *Waman Rao v. Union of India* (1981) 2 SCC 362: laws placed in the **Ninth Schedule** before 24 April 1973 are protected; those after that date are open to challenge.
- *I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu* (2007) 2 SCC 1 (nine judges): a Ninth Schedule law enacted after 24 April 1973 can be struck down if it violates the essence of rights that form part of the basic structure (Arts. 14, 15, 19, 21).
- *S.R. Bommai* (1994): federalism and secularism are basic features.
- *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997) 3 SCC 261: judicial review by the High Courts and the Supreme Court is basic.
- *Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India* (2016) 5 SCC 1: the 99th Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 were struck down as violating the independence of the judiciary.

**7. Criticism and defence.** *Critics:* the doctrine is judge-made and gives the Court the last word over the elected Parliament; its content is uncertain. *Defenders:* a constituent power that can alter the identity of the Constitution would let a temporary majority destroy the democratic order; the doctrine has protected the Constitution from the 1970s Emergency amendments (39th, 42nd).

**Conclusion.** The line from *Shankari Prasad* to *Kesavananda* shows the Court moving from textual literalism to protection of constitutional identity. Today Fundamental Rights can be amended, but only so far as the basic structure and the essence of the rights survive.

## C-06 · Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of the laws

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 ("Discuss with illustrations the right of equality before law."); Dec 2025 Q7 (B: "Doctrine of reasonableness and doctrine of non-arbitrariness are the guiding principles of equality clause"; answered at B-09).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text and meaning 3 · reasonable classification with cases 6 · arbitrariness with cases 6 · other facets and exceptions 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Article 14: "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India." It applies to **any person**, including non-citizens and corporations, and binds the State (Art. 12). With Arts. 19 and 21 it forms the "golden triangle" (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978).

**1. Two phrases.** *Equality before the law* is a British concept (Dicey's rule of law): negative; no one is above the ordinary law, and no special privilege is allowed. *Equal protection of the laws* is American (14th Amendment): positive; equals must be treated equally in like circumstances. The Constitution takes both.

**2. Rule of law.** It is part of the basic structure (*Kesavananda*; *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain*, 1975). Exceptions: **Art. 361** (the President and Governors are not answerable to any court for official acts; no criminal proceedings during term; civil proceedings after two months' notice); parliamentary privileges (Arts. 105, 194); diplomatic immunity; Art. 31C.

**3. Reasonable classification: the traditional test.** Art. 14 forbids **class legislation** but permits **reasonable classification**, which must satisfy two conditions: (i) an **intelligible differentia** distinguishing the group from others left out, and (ii) a **rational nexus** between that differentia and the object of the law.
- *Shri Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar* AIR 1958 SC 538: the principles: a single individual may be a class; presumption of constitutionality; the legislature may address the problem where it is most felt; the Court may consider matters of common knowledge and history.
- *Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri v. Union of India* AIR 1951 SC 41: a law for one company upheld.
- *State of W.B. v. Anwar Ali Sarkar* AIR 1952 SC 75: the West Bengal Special Courts Act, 1950 was struck down as it gave no guiding principle for sending cases to special courts.
- *Budhan Choudhry v. State of Bihar* AIR 1955 SC 191: the two-part test was restated.
- *D.S. Nakara v. Union of India* (1983) 1 SCC 305: dividing pensioners by their date of retirement had no rational nexus with the object of pension; struck down.
- *Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI* (2014) 8 SCC 682: s. 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act (prior sanction for inquiry against senior officers) struck down as classification without nexus to the object.

**4. The new concept: non-arbitrariness.**
- *E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1974) 4 SCC 3 (Bhagwati J.): equality is a "dynamic concept"; "equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies". Any arbitrary State action violates Art. 14.
- *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) 1 SCC 248: a procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, not arbitrary.
- *R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority* (1979) 3 SCC 489 and *Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib* (1981) 1 SCC 722: government contracts and admissions must be non-arbitrary.
- *Shrilekha Vidyarthi v. State of U.P.* (1991) 1 SCC 212: mass termination of government counsel on a change of government was arbitrary.
- *State of A.P. v. McDowell & Co.* (1996) 3 SCC 709: a **law** could not be struck down merely because it is arbitrary.
- *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) 9 SCC 1: instant triple talaq struck down; the opinion of Nariman and Lalit JJ. applied **"manifest arbitrariness"** to legislation (a law that is capricious, irrational or without adequate determining principle).
- *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018) and *Joseph Shine* (2019): IPC ss. 377 (as applied to consenting adults) and 497 struck down; both held arbitrary.

**5. Other facets**
- **Natural justice and fairness:** *A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India* (1969) 2 SCC 262.
- **Promissory estoppel** against the Government: *Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P.* (1979) 2 SCC 409.
- **Legitimate expectation:** *Union of India v. Hindustan Development Corporation* (1993) 3 SCC 499.
- **Equal pay for equal work:** *Randhir Singh v. Union of India* (1982) 1 SCC 618; *State of Punjab v. Jagjit Singh* (2017) 1 SCC 148.
- **No equality in illegality:** *Chandigarh Administration v. Jagjit Singh* (1995) 1 SCC 745.
- **Special provisions** under Art. 15(3)–(6) and Art. 16(4) are consistent with Art. 14 as measures for substantive equality.

**Illustrations (for "discuss with illustrations").** (a) A tax law that taxes only the urban rich is a reasonable classification; a law that taxes only persons of one religion is not. (b) Two employees doing the same work cannot be paid differently without a rational basis. (c) A minister who arbitrarily cancels one person's licence while granting an identical one to another violates Art. 14.

**Criticism.** "Arbitrariness" is vague and gives judges wide discretion; *McDowell* cautioned against it; "manifest arbitrariness" is debated. The presumption of constitutionality makes challenges difficult. Art. 14 alone may preserve existing inequality, which is why Arts. 15(3)–(6), 16(4), 17, 38 and 39 exist.

**Conclusion.** Art. 14 now has two arms: classification and non-arbitrariness. Together they prevent class legislation and capricious State action, and they carry the burden of Art. 21 and Art. 19 in the "golden triangle".

## C-07 · Reservation policy

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q9 ("Critically examine the reservation policy as guaranteed under Indian Constitution. Refer to decided cases.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** constitutional provisions 4 · leading cases in sequence 8 · issues (creamy layer, 50%, promotion, EWS, sub-classification) 4 · critical evaluation 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Equality in Art. 14 allows the State to make special provision for disadvantaged groups. The Constitution makes reservation an instrument of **substantive equality** for Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST) and other backward classes, and since 2019 for economically weaker sections (EWS).

**1. Constitutional provisions**
- **Art. 15(3):** women and children. **Art. 15(4):** socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs (inserted by the First Amendment, 1951). **Art. 15(5):** admission to educational institutions, including private unaided ones except minority institutions (93rd Amendment, 2005). **Art. 15(6):** EWS, up to 10% (103rd Amendment, 2019).
- **Art. 16(4):** backward classes not adequately represented in the services; **16(4A):** reservation in promotion for SC/ST (77th Amendment, 1995); **16(4B):** carry-forward of vacancies (81st Amendment, 2000); **16(6):** EWS.
- **Art. 335:** claims of SC/ST consistent with efficiency of administration. **Arts. 330 and 332:** reserved seats in the House of the People and State Assemblies (extended to 2030 by the 104th Amendment). **Arts. 340–342:** commissions and lists. **Art. 46:** DPSP on education and economic interests of weaker sections.

**2. Leading cases in sequence**
- *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* AIR 1951 SC 226: a communal Government Order for medical seats violated Arts. 15(1) and 29(2). The First Amendment responded by adding **Art. 15(4)**.
- *M.R. Balaji v. State of Mysore* AIR 1963 SC 649: 68% reservation was excessive; caste cannot be the sole test; reservation should be less than 50%.
- *State of Kerala v. N.M. Thomas* (1976) 2 SCC 310: Art. 16(4) is not an exception to Art. 16(1) but an application of equality.
- ***Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217 (nine judges).** On the Mandal Commission (OM of 13 August 1990 for 27% OBC reservation and OM of 1991 for 10% economic reservation), the Court held: (i) 27% reservation for OBCs valid; backwardness may be identified primarily by caste together with other criteria; (ii) the **creamy layer** must be excluded; (iii) total reservation should **not exceed 50%** save in extraordinary situations; (iv) no reservation in **promotions** under Art. 16(4) (answered by the 77th Amendment); (v) the 10% economic reservation of 1991 was **struck down**; (vi) permanent bodies to examine inclusion and exclusion (the National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993).
- *M. Nagaraj v. Union of India* (2006) 8 SCC 212: the Court upheld the amendments (77th, 81st, 82nd, 85th) but required the State to show, with quantifiable data, **backwardness**, **inadequate representation** and **overall efficiency**.
- *Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India* (2008) 6 SCC 1: the 93rd Amendment and the 27% OBC quota in central institutions upheld; creamy layer excluded.
- *Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta* (2018) 10 SCC 396: data on backwardness need not be shown for SC/ST in promotion, but inadequacy of representation must be; the creamy layer principle applies to SC/ST in promotions.
- *Mukesh Kumar v. State of Uttarakhand* (2020) 3 SCC 1: there is **no fundamental right** to reservation in promotion; Arts. 16(4) and 16(4A) are enabling.
- *Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, Maharashtra* (2021) 8 SCC 1: the Maratha reservation was struck down; the 50% ceiling stood; *Indra Sawhney* was not referred to a larger bench. The 105th Amendment (2021) restored the States' power to maintain their own OBC lists.
- *Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (7 November 2022; 3:2): the **103rd Amendment** (EWS 10%) held valid; economic criterion alone is a valid classification and does not violate the basic structure; the dissent (Lalit CJ and Bhat J.) objected to the exclusion of SC/ST/OBC poor and to breaching the 50% ceiling.
- *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024; seven judges, 6:1): **sub-classification within SC/ST** is permissible on quantifiable data of backwardness and inadequate representation; *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2005) was overruled.

**3. Critical evaluation**

*In favour.* (i) Historic exclusion needs a remedy; formal equality cannot overcome centuries of caste disadvantage; (ii) the Court has built safeguards: creamy layer, data requirements, 50% ceiling, efficiency (Art. 335); (iii) sub-classification (*Davinder Singh*) helps the most disadvantaged within a category.

*Against.* (i) The **50% ceiling** is judge-made and is breached by state laws (Tamil Nadu 69% through the Ninth Schedule) and by the EWS quota; (ii) **caste as a proxy** for backwardness is contested; (iii) the **creamy layer** is applied unevenly; (iv) reservation in **promotion** has produced conflicting judgments (*Nagaraj*, *Jarnail Singh*, *Mukesh Kumar*); (v) the EWS quota excludes the poor among SC/ST/OBC and was upheld only 3:2; (vi) benefits go to a few within each group; (vii) the public sector shrinks and private-sector jobs remain outside Art. 16.

**Conclusion.** Reservation is constitutionally accepted and judicially regulated. The policy needs continuing review on the basis of data: who is still excluded, whether benefits reach the most backward, and whether the ceiling and the creamy layer are applied consistently. The Court's approach from *Indra Sawhney* to *Davinder Singh* is to keep reservation as a tool of equality while insisting on evidence and limits.
