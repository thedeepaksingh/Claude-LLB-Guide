<a id="a-38"></a>
**A-38 · Equality before law and equal protection of the laws**

*Equality before the law* (British, negative) means that no one is above the ordinary law and no special privilege is allowed. *Equal protection of the laws* (American, positive) means that persons in like circumstances must be treated alike. Art. 14 uses both and applies to any person, including a company. Exceptions include Art. 361 (President and Governors) and Art. 31C.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-39"></a>
**A-39 · Reasonable classification**

Art. 14 allows classification that rests on (i) an **intelligible differentia** distinguishing the group from others and (ii) a **rational nexus** with the object of the law (*Budhan Choudhry*, 1955; *Ram Krishna Dalmia*, 1958). The law is presumed constitutional. *Anwar Ali Sarkar* (1952) struck down a law with no guiding principle; *D.S. Nakara* (1983) struck down a pension classification by date of retirement.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-40"></a>
**A-40 · Non-arbitrariness under Article 14**

*E.P. Royappa* (1974): equality is a dynamic concept; "equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies", so arbitrary State action violates Art. 14. *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) required fair, just and reasonable procedure. In *Shayara Bano* (2017) two judges applied "manifest arbitrariness" to strike down a law; *McDowell* (1996) had cautioned against striking down legislation merely as arbitrary.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-41"></a>
**A-41 · Equal pay for equal work**

Art. 39(d) directs equal pay for equal work for men and women. *Randhir Singh v. Union of India* (1982) read it with Arts. 14 and 16 to make it enforceable, holding that unequal pay for equal work is arbitrary. *State of Punjab v. Jagjit Singh* (2017) applied it to temporary employees doing the same work as regular staff. The Code on Wages, 2019 bars gender discrimination in wages.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-42"></a>
**A-42 · Exceptions to equality before law**

Art. 361: the President and Governors are not answerable to any court for official acts; no criminal proceedings lie against them during office; civil proceedings need two months' notice. Members of Parliament and State Legislatures have privileges (Arts. 105, 194). Foreign sovereigns and diplomats have immunity. Art. 31C protects some laws from challenge under Art. 14.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-43"></a>
**A-43 · Article 15(3): women and children**

Art. 15(3) allows special provision for **women and children**; it supports maternity benefits and seats for women in local bodies. The Court has held that it cannot perpetuate stereotypes: *Anuj Garg* (2008) struck down a ban on women working in bars; *Joseph Shine* (2019) held that s. 497 IPC could not be saved by Art. 15(3); *Babita Puniya* (2020) granted permanent commission to women officers.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-44"></a>
**A-44 · Article 15(4) and 15(5)**

Art. 15(4), inserted by the First Amendment (1951) after *Champakam Dorairajan*, permits special provision for socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs. Art. 15(5), inserted by the 93rd Amendment (2005), extends this to admission in educational institutions, including private unaided ones but not minority institutions. *Ashoka Kumar Thakur* (2008) upheld the 27% OBC quota; *Pramati* (2014) upheld Art. 15(5).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-45"></a>
**A-45 · Article 16(4): reservation in public employment**

Art. 16(4) allows reservation of appointments or posts for backward classes not adequately represented in State services. Art. 16(4A) (77th Amendment, 1995) covers promotion for SCs and STs; 16(4B) allows carry-forward of vacancies. *Indra Sawhney* (1992) upheld 27% for OBCs, excluded the creamy layer and fixed a 50% ceiling. *Mukesh Kumar* (2020): reservation in promotion is not a fundamental right.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-46"></a>
**A-46 · Creamy layer**

The "creamy layer" means the socially and economically advanced members of a backward class, who are excluded from reservation. *Indra Sawhney* (1992) required its exclusion for OBCs; *Ashoka Kumar Thakur* (2008) applied it to the 27% quota in higher education; *Jarnail Singh* (2018) applied the principle to SCs and STs in promotion. The Government fixes the criteria and revises them from time to time.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-47"></a>
**A-47 · EWS reservation (103rd Amendment)**

The 103rd Amendment (2019) inserted Arts. 15(6) and 16(6): up to 10% reservation for economically weaker sections (other than SCs, STs and backward classes) in education and public employment. *Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India* (7 November 2022) upheld it by 3:2; the minority (Lalit CJI and Bhat J.) objected that it excluded poor SCs, STs and OBCs and crossed the 50% ceiling.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-48"></a>
**A-48 · The 50% ceiling on reservation**

*M.R. Balaji* (1963) said that reservation should be less than 50%. *Indra Sawhney* (1992) held that total reservation should not exceed 50% save in extraordinary situations. *Jaishri Patil* (2021) struck down the Maratha quota that crossed it. The EWS quota also goes beyond 50% and was upheld in *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022). Tamil Nadu's 69% is protected through the Ninth Schedule.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-49"></a>
**A-49 · Sub-classification of Scheduled Castes and Tribes**

*State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (1 August 2024, seven judges, 6:1) held that States may sub-classify SCs and STs for reservation if they have quantifiable data on backwardness and inadequate representation, so that the most disadvantaged receive a share. It overruled *E.V. Chinnaiah* (2005). Sub-classification must not be arbitrary or politically motivated.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-50"></a>
**A-50 · Article 17: abolition of untouchability**

Art. 17 abolishes "untouchability" and forbids its practice in any form; enforcing any disability arising from it is an offence. It binds the State and private persons. The Constitution does not define the word. The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 give effect to it.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-51"></a>
**A-51 · Article 18: abolition of titles**

The State may not confer titles (except military and academic distinctions); a citizen may not accept a title from a foreign State; office-holders need the President's consent for gifts or offices from a foreign State. In *Balaji Raghavan* (1996) the Supreme Court held that the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not titles, but must not be used as prefixes or suffixes.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-52"></a>
**A-52 · Right to know**

The right to know is read into Art. 19(1)(a). *State of U.P. v. Raj Narain* (1975): in a democracy there can be few secrets. *S.P. Gupta* (1981) spoke of open government; *Union of India v. ADR* (2002) allowed voters to know a candidate's criminal record, assets and education; electoral bonds were struck down in 2024. The Right to Information Act, 2005 gives statutory form.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-53"></a>
**A-53 · Public order**

"Public order" was added to Art. 19(2) by the First Amendment (1951). *Ram Manohar Lohia* (1966) described three circles: law and order (widest), public order, and security of the State (narrowest). Only acts that disturb public order may be restricted, not those that merely disturb law and order. *Rangarajan* (1989): the threat must be real and proximate, "like a spark in a powder keg".

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-54"></a>
**A-54 · Prior restraint**

Prior restraint means stopping speech before it is published, for example by censorship. It is disfavoured: *Brij Bhushan* (1950) struck down pre-censorship of a newspaper, and *R. Rajagopal* (1994) held that the press may publish matters of public record without prior restraint. Film pre-censorship was upheld in *K.A. Abbas* (1970) because of the special impact of films.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-55"></a>
**A-55 · Commercial speech**

Commercial speech such as advertising is protected by Art. 19(1)(a) because it informs the public. *Tata Press v. MTNL* (1995) held that an advertisement that gives information to the public falls within the freedom, as the public has a right to receive it. False or misleading advertisements may be restricted under Art. 19(2) or consumer law.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-56"></a>
**A-56 · Freedom of speech on the internet**

*Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) held that freedom of speech and expression over the internet, and the freedom to carry on trade or business through it, are protected by Arts. 19(1)(a) and (g). An indefinite internet shutdown is impermissible; orders must be published, necessary, proportionate and temporary, and are open to judicial review.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-57"></a>
**A-57 · Obscenity**

Obscenity is restricted under "decency or morality" in Art. 19(2). The old *Hicklin* test (applied in *Ranjit Udeshi*, 1965) judged a work by its effect on the most susceptible. *Aveek Sarkar* (2014) adopted the **community-standards test**: the work is judged as a whole, by contemporary standards, from the viewpoint of the average person. *Bobby Art International* (1996): *Bandit Queen* was not obscene.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-58"></a>
**A-58 · Contempt of court**

Contempt of court is a ground in Art. 19(2). The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 defines civil and criminal contempt; Arts. 129 and 215 empower the Supreme Court and High Courts to punish. Truth is a defence if bona fide and in the public interest (s. 13, since 2006). In *Prashant Bhushan, In re* (2020) the Court imposed a fine of one rupee.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-59"></a>
**A-59 · Defamation**

Defamation is a ground in Art. 19(2). *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) upheld criminal defamation (IPC ss. 499–500; now BNS s. 356), holding that reputation is part of Art. 21 and that the restriction is reasonable. *R. Rajagopal* (1994): a public official cannot sue over official conduct unless the publication is false and made with reckless disregard of the truth.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-60"></a>
**A-60 · *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015)**

The Court struck down s. 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 as vague and overbroad, with a chilling effect on speech not covered by Art. 19(2). It distinguished **discussion**, **advocacy** and **incitement**: only incitement may be restricted. It read down s. 79 (intermediary liability) and upheld s. 69A (blocking) with safeguards.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-61"></a>
**A-61 · Protection against self-incrimination (Art. 20(3))**

No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. The elements are: an accused, compulsion, and testimony against himself. Specimen handwriting and fingerprints are not testimony (*Kathi Kalu Oghad*, 1961). The protection applies from police interrogation (*Nandini Satpathy*, 1978). *Selvi* (2010) barred involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and brain mapping.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-62"></a>
**A-62 · Double jeopardy (Art. 20(2))**

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. It applies only where there was a prosecution before a court or judicial tribunal, a punishment, and the same offence (*Maqbool Hussain*, 1953). A departmental inquiry is not a prosecution (*Venkataraman*, 1954). BNSS s. 337 also bars a second trial after acquittal.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-63"></a>
**A-63 · Narco-analysis: *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010)**

The Court held that involuntary administration of narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping tests violates Art. 20(3) (protection against self-incrimination) and Art. 21 (mental privacy and personal liberty). Consent must be voluntary and informed, and voluntary tests are allowed only with safeguards. Compelled results cannot be used against the accused.

*Word count: 48*

<a id="a-64"></a>
**A-64 · "Procedure established by law" and "due process"**

Art. 21 uses "procedure established by law" (from the Japanese Constitution), unlike the American "due process of law". *Gopalan* (1950) read it as any procedure laid down by a valid law. *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable, so substantive due process has been introduced by interpretation.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-65"></a>
**A-65 · The golden triangle (Arts. 14, 19 and 21)**

*Maneka Gandhi* (1978) held that Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive: a law that deprives a person of personal liberty must satisfy Art. 21's requirement of fair procedure, Art. 14's test of non-arbitrariness and, where it applies, Art. 19. This overruled the separate-code approach of *Gopalan* (1950).

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-66"></a>
**A-66 · Right to livelihood**

*Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) held that the right to life includes the right to livelihood, because a person cannot live without the means of living. Pavement dwellers could not be evicted without notice and a hearing, though they could be removed after a reasonable opportunity. The Court relied on Arts. 39(a) and 41. There is no right to encroach on public land.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-67"></a>
**A-67 · Right to privacy**

*K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (24 August 2017, nine judges) unanimously held that privacy is a fundamental right under Art. 21 and Part III. It covers bodily integrity, decisional autonomy and informational privacy. Any intrusion must satisfy legality, a legitimate aim and proportionality. *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* were overruled in part, and *ADM Jabalpur* was overruled.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-68"></a>
**A-68 · Speedy trial and free legal aid**

*Hussainara Khatoon* (1979–80) held that a speedy trial is part of Art. 21 and that free legal aid is an essential element of a fair procedure; undertrials held beyond the maximum sentence were released. *M.H. Hoskot* (1978): an indigent prisoner is entitled to a lawyer at State cost. Art. 39A and the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 give effect.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-69"></a>
**A-69 · Right to shelter**

The right to shelter is part of the right to life. *Chameli Singh v. State of U.P.* (1996) held that shelter is a necessary part of a life with dignity. *Olga Tellis* (1985) required notice and a hearing before eviction. The Court's directions of 13 November 2024 on demolitions require notice and due process before a property is pulled down.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-70"></a>
**A-70 · Right to health and medical aid**

The right to health is part of Art. 21. *Parmanand Katara* (1989): every doctor must give immediate medical aid to an injured person. *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity* (1996): failure of a government hospital to provide emergency treatment violated Art. 21 and compensation was awarded. Arts. 39(e), 41 and 47 support the right.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-71"></a>
**A-71 · Right to die**

*Gian Kaur* (1996) held that the right to life does not include the right to die, overruling *P. Rathinam* (1994). *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011) allowed passive euthanasia under court supervision. *Common Cause* (9 March 2018) held that the right to die with dignity is part of Art. 21 and permitted passive euthanasia and advance directives (living wills) under guidelines.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-72"></a>
**A-72 · *Unni Krishnan v. State of A.P.* (1993)**

A five-judge bench held that every child has a fundamental right to free education until the age of fourteen, under Art. 21 read with Arts. 41 and 45; beyond that, the right depends on the State's economic capacity. Capitation fees were unconstitutional. Its scheme for private professional colleges was overruled in *T.M.A. Pai* (2002). Art. 21A followed in 2002.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-73"></a>
**A-73 · The 25% quota under the RTE Act**

Section 12(1)(c) of the RTE Act, 2009 requires specified private unaided schools to admit at least 25% of Class I children from weaker and disadvantaged groups, with the State reimbursing the cost. *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan* (2012) upheld the quota for non-minority schools; *Pramati* (2014) exempted minority institutions.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-74"></a>
**A-74 · Death penalty and Article 21**

*Bachan Singh* (1980, 4:1) upheld the death penalty but only in the "rarest of rare" cases, after weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances. *Mithu* (1983) struck down the mandatory death sentence in s. 303 IPC. *Shatrughan Chauhan* (2014) held that unexplained delay in deciding a mercy petition can justify commutation under Art. 21.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-75"></a>
**A-75 · Right to a clean environment**

The Supreme Court has read a right to a healthy environment into Art. 21: *Rural Litigation Kendra* (1985) (Doon Valley quarrying) and *Subhash Kumar* (1991) (pollution-free water and air). Art. 48A (State) and Art. 51A(g) (citizens) support it. The Court has developed the precautionary principle, the polluter-pays principle and the public trust doctrine.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-76"></a>
**A-76 · Absolute liability**

In *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (Oleum gas leak, 1987) the Court held that an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity is **absolutely liable** for harm, without the exceptions of *Rylands v. Fletcher* such as act of God, and that compensation should relate to the enterprise's size and capacity. It followed the Bhopal gas leak of 1984.

*Word count: 61*
