## C-18 · Right against exploitation: Articles 23 and 24

**Asked as:** not asked as a 20-mark question in Dec 2022–2025. Included because Arts. 23–24 are a named syllabus line ("Right against exploitation") and the standard long question is "Discuss the constitutional protection against trafficking, begar, forced labour and child labour."
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Art. 23 text and terms 4 · Art. 23 cases 6 · Art. 24 text and cases 5 · statutes 2 · criticism and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** Articles 23 and 24 form the "Right against Exploitation" in Part III. They protect those who are weakest in the market: the poor labourer, the trafficked woman and the working child. The framers had in mind slavery, serfdom, *begar* (unpaid compulsory service extracted by landlords or the State) and the *devadasi* custom. The two Articles give constitutional force to the idea of human dignity in the Preamble and to Art. 39(e) and (f) of the Directive Principles.

**Article 23.**
> (1) Traffic in human beings and *begar* and other similar forms of forced labour are prohibited and any contravention shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. (2) Nothing in this article shall prevent the State from imposing compulsory service for public purposes, and in imposing such service the State shall not make any discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class or any of them.

Three points make Art. 23 unusual.
1. It protects **every person**, citizen or not.
2. It operates against the **State and private persons** alike. Most Fundamental Rights are claimed against the State; Art. 23 is one of the few with direct horizontal effect (*People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India*, (1982) 3 SCC 235).
3. By Art. 35(a)(ii) only **Parliament** may make the law that punishes the offence, so that the penal law is uniform across India.

**Meaning of the terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Traffic in human beings | Buying and selling of human beings as chattels, including trafficking of women and children for prostitution or labour (*Vishal Jeet v. Union of India*, (1990) 3 SCC 318) |
| *Begar* | Labour or service without payment which a person is compelled to perform |
| Other similar forms of forced labour | Any labour obtained by **force**, and "force" includes **economic compulsion** (see *PUDR* below) |

**Leading cases on Article 23**

| Case | What it decided |
|---|---|
| *PUDR v. Union of India* (Asiad Workers case), (1982) 3 SCC 235 | Labourers on the Asian Games construction projects in Delhi were paid less than the minimum wage through contractors. Bhagwati J. held that Art. 23 is enforceable against **private contractors** as well as the State; "force" is not only physical or legal compulsion but also the compulsion of hunger and poverty; **payment below the minimum wage is forced labour**; the State must see that labour laws are enforced. The petition came from a civil-liberties organisation, which the Court held entitled to sue on behalf of the workers. |
| *Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan*, (1983) 1 SCC 525 | Famine-relief workers paid less than the minimum wage: the State cannot take advantage of the helplessness of the starving; a Rajasthan law exempting such works from minimum wage law was bad to that extent. |
| *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India*, (1984) 3 SCC 161 | Bonded labourers in stone quarries near Delhi: a letter was treated as a petition; the Court ordered inspection, release and rehabilitation, and linked the matter to the right to live with dignity in Art. 21. The Court said that where a worker is shown to be working in bondage, the burden lies on the employer to disprove it. |
| *Neerja Chaudhary v. State of M.P.*, (1984) 3 SCC 243 | Release is not enough: **rehabilitation** of freed bonded labourers is a constitutional duty of the State. |
| *Deena v. Union of India*, (1983) 4 SCC 645 | Prisoners made to work without wages in jail are subject to forced labour; they should be paid reasonable wages. Labour as part of rigorous imprisonment is not by itself forbidden. |
| *Vishal Jeet* (1990) and *Gaurav Jain v. Union of India*, (1997) 8 SCC 114 | Directions to rescue and rehabilitate girls forced into prostitution and the *devadasi* practice; care and education of the children of sex workers. |
| *Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India*, (2011) 5 SCC 1 | Children trafficked for circuses: guidelines for rescue, rehabilitation and prevention. |

**Compulsory service for public purposes (Art. 23(2)).** The State may require conscription, national service or jury duty, but cannot discriminate on grounds *only* of religion, race, caste or class. Note that sex and place of birth are not mentioned.

**Statutes giving effect to Article 23.** The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** abolished the system, wiped out bonded debts, created Vigilance Committees and made the practice an offence. The **Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956** deals with commercial sexual exploitation. Trafficking is punished by **ss. 143–144 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023** (earlier IPC ss. 370 and 370A). Minimum-wage protection, which *PUDR* treated as the content of Art. 23, now rests on the Code on Wages, 2019, which replaced the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976.

**Article 24.**
> No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment.

On its face Art. 24 is narrow: it bars child work only in **factories, mines and other hazardous employment**. It is self-executing and applies to the State and to private employers. Its meaning is widened by Art. 39(e)–(f), Art. 21A and Art. 45.

| Case | What it decided |
|---|---|
| *PUDR* (1982) | **Construction work is hazardous employment**; a child under 14 cannot be employed in it, although the Employment of Children Act, 1938 did not then list it. Art. 24 applies directly. |
| *M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu*, (1996) 6 SCC 756 | Children in the **Sivakasi** match and fireworks factories. Hazardous work is forbidden. The Court directed that an offending employer pay **Rs. 20,000 per child** into a Child Labour Rehabilitation-Cum-Welfare Fund, that the State secure alternative employment for the family or contribute to the fund, and that working hours and education for non-hazardous work be regulated. |
| *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India*, (1997) 10 SCC 549 | Child workers in the carpet industry: directions for education, health and monitoring. |

**Statutory framework for Art. 24.** The **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986** was amended in **2016** (in force 1 September 2016; renamed the *Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act*). It now (i) prohibits employment of a **child below 14 in any occupation**, except helping in a family enterprise after school hours or during vacations in non-hazardous work and working as a child artist in audio-visual entertainment under safeguards; (ii) prohibits adolescents of 14–18 from hazardous occupations and processes; and (iii) raises the penalty (six months to two years' imprisonment and/or a fine of Rs. 20,000–50,000 for employing a child). The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 supports it by keeping children in school.

**Criticism.**
1. Bonded labour and child labour continue in brick kilns, agriculture, domestic work and construction; convictions and rehabilitation payments are slow.
2. The text of Art. 24 does not forbid non-hazardous child work, and the 2016 exception for family enterprises is said to let agricultural and home-based child work continue.
3. Adolescents of 14–18 are protected only from hazardous work.
4. Trafficking is dealt with by scattered provisions; the Trafficking of Persons (Prevention, Protection and Rehabilitation) Bill, 2018 lapsed.
5. Informal and platform work raise the same "economic compulsion" question that *PUDR* answered in 1982.

**Conclusion.** *PUDR* made Art. 23 a living guarantee by recognising that poverty can compel as surely as a whip, and *M.C. Mehta* gave Art. 24 practical remedies. The next task is enforcement: identification, prosecution, rehabilitation and school enrolment.

## C-19 · Article 25: religious freedom of the individual

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q11 ("With the help of decided cases discuss Religious Freedom as provided to individuals under Article 25 of Indian Constitution."). Related: the Art. 25/26 distinction and "essential religious practices" are the usual short forms (B-level and A-level versions follow in the Section B file).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** secular background and text 4 · elements and limits 5 · cases (including essential religious practices) 8 · recent developments and criticism 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** India is a secular State in the sense that it has no State religion and treats all religions equally, but it does not keep religion out of public life. The word "secular" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but the Supreme Court had long found secularism in Arts. 14–16 and 25–30, and in *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, (1994) 3 SCC 1, held it to be a basic feature. Article 25 is the individual's religious freedom. Article 26 gives the corresponding collective right to a religious denomination.

**Text.** Art. 25(1): "Subject to public order, morality and health and to the other provisions of this Part, all persons are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion."
Art. 25(2) saves laws (a) regulating or restricting any economic, financial, political or other **secular activity** associated with religious practice and (b) providing for **social welfare and reform** or throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. **Explanation I** says the wearing and carrying of **kirpans** is included in the profession of the Sikh religion. **Explanation II** says that "Hindus" in clause (2)(b) includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.

**Features.**
1. It is available to **all persons**, citizens and foreigners alike.
2. It protects **belief and practice**: conscience (including the right to hold no belief), profession (open declaration), practice (worship, rites, observances) and propagation (exposition and spread of one's teachings).
3. It is a right against the **State**.
4. It is **not absolute.** It is subject to public order, morality and health; to the **other provisions of Part III** (so that a religious practice cannot override Arts. 14, 15 or 17); and to State regulation under Art. 25(2).

**Propagation and conversion.** In *Rev. Stainislaus v. State of M.P.*, (1977) 1 SCC 677, the Court upheld the Orissa and Madhya Pradesh Freedom of Religion Acts: the right to propagate means to transmit or spread one's religion by exposition of its tenets, and it does **not include a right to convert** another person, since forced or induced conversion disturbs public order. Several States have since enacted anti-conversion laws, and challenges to the newer, marriage-linked laws are before the courts. The right of an adult to marry a person of her choice is protected under Art. 21 (*Shafin Jahan v. Asokan K.M.*, (2018) 16 SCC 368).

**Religion is more than belief: the doctrine of essential religious practices (ERP).** In *Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Shirur Mutt*, AIR 1954 SC 282, Mukherjea J. held that religion includes rituals and observances and not only doctrine, and that the Constitution protects what is **essential** to the religion, while the State may regulate secular matters such as the administration of property. Later cases applied this test.

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Ratilal Panachand Gandhi v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1954 SC 388 | Trusts and property may be regulated, religious practice may not; no right to maladminister. |
| *Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar*, AIR 1958 SC 731 | Sacrificing a cow on Bakr-Id is not an essential part of Islam; the ban on cow slaughter stands. |
| *Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali*, AIR 1961 SC 1402 | Only practices that are **essential and integral** are protected; superstitious accretions are not. |
| *Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore*, AIR 1958 SC 255 | Temple-entry law (Art. 25(2)(b)) against the right of a denominational temple to exclude outsiders (Art. 26(b)): the Court harmonised the two, allowing exclusion only for specific ceremonies. |
| *Acharya Jagdishwaranand Avadhuta v. Commissioner of Police*, (1983) 4 SCC 522 | The *tandava* dance in public processions is not an essential rite of the Ananda Marga faith, so public order prevails. |
| *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala*, (1986) 3 SCC 615 | Jehovah's Witness children who stood respectfully but did not sing the national anthem could not be expelled: no one can be forced to act against conscience (Arts. 19(1)(a), 25). |
| *M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India*, (1994) 6 SCC 360 | A mosque is not essential to the practice of Islam, so the State may acquire a place of worship under law. |
| *Church of God (Full Gospel) v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Association*, (2000) 7 SCC 282 | Loudspeakers are not an essential part of any religion; noise can be regulated. |
| *Shayara Bano v. Union of India*, (2017) 9 SCC 1 | Instant triple talaq set aside by a 3:2 majority; two judges held it manifestly arbitrary under Art. 14, one held it contrary to the Quran so not an essential practice. Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019. |
| *Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala* (Sabarimala), (2019) 11 SCC 1 | The exclusion of women aged 10–50 was held, by 4:1, to violate Arts. 14, 15 and 25(1); Ayyappa devotees were not a separate denomination; the practice was not essential. Indu Malhotra J. dissented, saying that courts should not decide what is essential to a faith. |

**Secular activity and social reform (Art. 25(2)).** Under clause (2)(a) the State can regulate the management of religious property, fairs and employment. Under clause (2)(b) the State has abolished practices such as *devadasi* dedication and temple exclusion and has opened Hindu temples to all (the temple-entry laws). *Seshammal v. State of Tamil Nadu*, (1972) 2 SCC 11, held that the appointment of a temple priest is a secular act that the State can regulate, subject to the ritual requirements of the *Agamas*.

**Criticism of the ERP doctrine.** The word "essential" does not appear in the Constitution; Art. 26(b) speaks of "matters of religion". Judges have to decide questions of theology, and the test can disfavour minority and oral traditions. Indu Malhotra J. and many scholars say the Court should ask only whether a practice is harmful or discriminatory. Art. 25(2)(b) deals only with Hindu institutions, and critics call it asymmetric; its defenders point to the history of caste exclusion.

**Recent developments.**
1. The **Sabarimala review petitions** were kept pending by the order of 14 November 2019, which referred the wider questions (the scope of Arts. 25 and 26, ERP, "constitutional morality", and similar practices in other faiths) to a larger Bench. A **nine-judge Bench** heard the reference in 2026 and reserved judgment in May 2026; check whether it has been pronounced.
2. The **hijab** case: the Karnataka High Court (15 March 2022) held the hijab not an essential practice, and the Supreme Court delivered a **split verdict** on 13 October 2022; the matter was referred to a larger Bench.
3. The **Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025** is under challenge under Arts. 14, 25 and 26; by an interim order of 15 September 2025 the Court declined to stay the whole Act but stayed a few provisions.

**Conclusion.** Article 25 protects what a person believes and does in the name of religion, but not what disturbs public order, morality or health, and not what violates equality and dignity. The Court has moved from asking only "is the practice essential?" towards asking also "is it consistent with Part III?"; the nine-judge reference may settle the point.

## C-20 · Cultural and educational rights of minorities: Articles 29 and 30

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q7 (B, 10 marks: "Examine the Cultural and Educational Rights of Minorities as prescribed under Articles 29 and 30. Refer to decided cases.") and Dec 2025 Q8 (B: "Write a critical note on Cultural and Educational Rights of Minorities…"). This 20-mark version is the full form; the 10-mark answer is B-10.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Art. 29 text and cases 5 · who is a minority 3 · Art. 30 and limits with cases 7 · AMU, recent developments 2 · criticism and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** After Partition the Constituent Assembly wanted to reassure religious and linguistic minorities that they could keep their identity in a democracy. Articles 29 and 30, under "Cultural and Educational Rights", give that assurance.

**Article 29.**
- **Art. 29(1):** "Any section of the citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script or culture of its own shall have the right to conserve the same." The holder is any **section of citizens**, so a majority in one State may also claim it. It includes the right to agitate by lawful means for the protection of the language (*Jagdev Singh Sidhanti v. Pratap Singh Daulta*, AIR 1965 SC 183). In *D.A.V. College, Bhatinda v. State of Punjab*, (1971) 2 SCC 269, the Court struck down the rule making Punjabi in Gurmukhi script the compulsory medium for affiliated colleges because it violated Arts. 29(1) and 30(1). In *State of Karnataka v. Associated Management of (Govt. Recognised, Unaided, English Medium) Primary and Secondary Schools*, (2014) 9 SCC 485, a Constitution Bench held that the State cannot impose the mother tongue as the medium at the primary stage.
- **Art. 29(2):** no citizen shall be denied admission to an institution **maintained by the State or receiving State aid** on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, language or any of them. In *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan*, AIR 1951 SC 226, the communal Government Order reserving medical and engineering seats by caste and religion was struck down; the First Amendment (1951) then inserted Art. 15(4). *State of Bombay v. Bombay Education Society*, AIR 1954 SC 561, held that a rule barring children whose mother tongue was not English from English-medium schools denied admission on the ground of language.

**Article 30.**
- **30(1):** all minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to **establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.**
- **30(1A)** (44th Amendment, 1978): on compulsory acquisition of a minority institution's property, the amount must not restrict or abrogate the right.
- **30(2):** the State shall not, in granting aid, discriminate against an institution because it is under the management of a minority.

**Who is a minority?** The Constitution does not define "minority". *In re Kerala Education Bill, 1957*, AIR 1958 SC 956, treated a community as a minority if it is numerically less than half of the population. Whether the unit is the nation or the State was settled in *T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka*, (2002) 8 SCC 481 (eleven judges): the **State is the unit** for both linguistic and religious minorities. The Central Government has notified Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists and Parsis, and later Jains, as minority communities under the National Commission for Minorities Act, 1992. The National Commission for Minority Educational Institutions Act, 2004 lets the Commission decide disputes about minority status.

**Scope: administer, not maladminister.**

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *In re Kerala Education Bill, 1957* AIR 1958 SC 956 | The right is not absolute; regulation of educational standards is allowed, but not what destroys the minority character. |
| *Rev. Sidhajbhai Sabhai v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1963 SC 540 | Under the guise of regulation the State cannot reduce the management to a cipher. |
| *Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society v. State of Gujarat*, (1974) 1 SCC 717 (nine judges) | The right to administer is not the right to maladminister. Regulations on affiliation, standards and discipline are valid, but a rule forcing the college to refer staff disciplinary disputes to a University Tribunal violated Art. 30(1). |
| *St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi*, (1992) 1 SCC 558 | An aided minority college may reserve seats for its own community (up to half) but must admit others on merit. |
| *T.M.A. Pai Foundation*, (2002) 8 SCC 481 | State is the unit; education is an occupation under Art. 19(1)(g); unaided institutions have more autonomy over admissions and fees but may not charge capitation fees or profiteer; aided institutions can be regulated more; minority institutions may admit non-minority students. |
| *P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra*, (2005) 6 SCC 537 (seven judges) | The State cannot impose reservation on unaided private institutions, including minority ones. |
| *Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India*, (2014) 8 SCC 1 | The RTE Act, 2009 and Art. 15(5) do not apply to minority institutions, aided or unaided. |

**Art. 30(2).** Once the State aids educational institutions, it cannot refuse aid because the institution is run by a minority; but aid can be made subject to reasonable regulations, such as qualifications of teachers and merit-based admission of non-minority students.

**The AMU case.** *S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India*, AIR 1968 SC 662, held that Aligarh Muslim University, being established by an Act of Parliament, was not "established" by the minority and so could not claim Art. 30(1). On **8 November 2024** a seven-judge Bench in *Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal* by a **4:3** majority **overruled *Azeez Basha*** on that point. The majority (Chandrachud CJI writing for himself and three others) said that incorporation by statute does not itself rule out minority status and that the Court must look at the origin of the institution (the idea, the funds, the land and the approvals), its purpose and its administration. Whether AMU is in fact a minority institution was left to a regular Bench to decide. Three judges dissented.

**Other recent developments.**
1. *Anjuman Ishaat-e-Taleem Trust v. State of Maharashtra* (1 September 2025): a two-judge Bench doubted the blanket exemption of minority institutions from the RTE Act in *Pramati* and referred the question to a larger Bench *(verify the present stage)*.
2. *Anjum Kadari v. Union of India* (5 November 2024) upheld the Uttar Pradesh Board of Madarsa Education Act, 2004 as regulatory and consistent with Art. 30, but held the provisions conferring degrees (Kamil and Fazil) to be inconsistent with the UGC Act.
3. The three-language formula and mother-tongue instruction under the National Education Policy, 2020 continue to touch Arts. 29(1), 30(1) and 350A.

**Art. 29 and Art. 30 compared.** Article 29 is available to any section of citizens and protects language, script and culture; Art. 29(2) is an individual right of admission. Article 30 is available only to minorities and protects the establishment and administration of educational institutions. Article 29 is wide in who may claim but narrow in what it gives; Art. 30 is narrow in who but wide in what.

**Criticism.**
1. "Minority" is undefined, and the State-as-unit test produces anomalies.
2. The exemption of minority institutions from reservation and from the RTE quota leaves their students without those safeguards.
3. Claims of minority status are sometimes used to avoid regulation of fees and admissions.
4. The line between regulation and interference is drawn case by case.
5. On the positive side, the rights protect pluralism and let communities preserve culture through education, and *T.M.A. Pai* balances autonomy with accountability.

**Conclusion.** The courts have read Art. 30 as a right to autonomy within the law: a minority may run its own institutions, but the State may regulate for standards, and no one may use the right to avoid the Constitution's wider promise of equality. The AMU judgment and the *Pramati* reference are the points to watch.

## C-21 · Public interest litigation

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q11 ("Public Interest Litigation is a prominent tool to get constitutional remedies now a days. Discuss with the help of decided cases."); Dec 2024 Q12 ("Public Interest Litigation has opened a floodgate as a constitutional remedy. Explain with the help of decided cases."); Dec 2023 Q11 ("What do you understand by public interest litigation? What are the powers of Supreme Court and High Courts in this regard?"). **The one topic asked in three consecutive years.**
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and origin 3 · locus standi and features 4 · leading cases 5 · powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts 3 · abuse and guidelines 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** In an ordinary case only the person whose own right is violated can approach the court (the rule of *locus standi*). Millions of people are too poor, illiterate or powerless to do so. **Public interest litigation (PIL)** relaxes that rule: any public-spirited person or group, acting bona fide, may approach the court on behalf of those who cannot, or to protect rights shared by the public as a whole (environment, consumers, good governance). PIL grew in the late 1970s and 1980s, led by Justices V.R. Krishna Iyer and P.N. Bhagwati, as the Court sought to restore its image after the Emergency (*ADM Jabalpur*, 1976) and to make the Constitution's promises real for the poor.

**Constitutional basis.** There is no mention of PIL in the Constitution. It rests on Art. 32 (the Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights), Art. 226 (High Courts, for Fundamental Rights and "any other purpose"), Art. 142 (the Supreme Court's power to do complete justice), Art. 21 (read with Arts. 39A and 41) and the Court's duty as guardian of Part III.

**Locus standi.** In *S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981 Supp SCC 87 (the Judges' Transfer case), the Court held that any member of the public with sufficient interest may seek redress for a legal wrong or injury caused to a person or a class who, by reason of poverty, disability or social or economic disadvantage, cannot approach the court.

**Features of PIL.**
1. **Relaxed locus standi.**
2. **Epistolary jurisdiction.** A letter or a newspaper report can be treated as a writ petition, and the Court can act *suo motu* (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration*, (1978) 4 SCC 494, where a prisoner's letter was treated as a petition; *Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra*, (1983) 2 SCC 96, on a journalist's letter on women prisoners).
3. **Non-adversarial procedure.** The Court acts as a facilitator, appoints Commissioners, expert committees and *amici curiae* to find facts.
4. **Creative remedies.** Guidelines, continuing mandamus, compensation, monitoring committees.
5. **Low cost,** with court fees and formalities relaxed.

**Leading cases**

| Case | Contribution |
|---|---|
| *Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar*, (1980) 1 SCC 81 | Newspaper reports exposed thousands of undertrial prisoners in Bihar jails held for longer than the maximum sentence. The Court held speedy trial a part of Art. 21 and free legal aid a Fundamental Right, and ordered their release. |
| *S.P. Gupta* (1981) | Liberalised standing (above). |
| *PUDR v. Union of India*, (1982) 3 SCC 235 | The Asiad workers case: minimum wages and the abolition of forced labour under Art. 23. |
| *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India*, (1984) 3 SCC 161 | Bonded labour in quarries: a letter treated as a writ; fact-finding by Commissioners. |
| *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P.*, (1985) 2 SCC 431 | The Doon Valley case: illegal limestone quarrying stopped to protect the ecology. |
| *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*, (1987) 1 SCC 395 and later orders | Oleum gas leak: the doctrine of **absolute liability** for hazardous industry; the Ganga and Taj Mahal cases. |
| *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan*, (1997) 6 SCC 241 | In the absence of law, the Court laid down guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace; later enacted as the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013. |
| *Vineet Narain v. Union of India*, (1998) 1 SCC 226 | The Hawala case: continuing mandamus to secure the independence of the CBI and the Central Vigilance Commission. |
| *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India*, (2020) 3 SCC 637 | Internet shutdowns reviewed on proportionality. |
| *M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India* (2024) | Protection of the Great Indian Bustard led the Court to recognise a right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change as part of Arts. 14 and 21. |

**Powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts in PIL**

| | Supreme Court | High Court |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Art. 32 (and Art. 142) | Art. 226 (and the supervisory power in Art. 227) |
| Subject matter | Enforcement of Fundamental Rights only | Fundamental Rights and "any other purpose", so a wider range including statutory duties |
| Relief | Writs of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto, **directions and orders**; compensation; guidelines | The same, and the power is wider as to persons ("any person or authority") |
| Special | Art. 141 makes its guidelines binding law until Parliament legislates (*Vishaka*); complete justice under Art. 142 | Local, quick fact-finding; often the better forum for fact-heavy disputes |

Both courts can appoint commissions and committees, call for reports, order interim protection, impose costs and monitor compliance through continuing mandamus. In *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261, the Court held that judicial review by the Supreme Court and High Courts under Arts. 32, 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure.

**Abuse and limits.** The Court itself has criticised PIL as "publicity interest litigation" (*Dattaraj Nathuji Thaware v. State of Maharashtra*, (2005) 1 SCC 590, where costs were imposed). In *Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar*, (1991) 1 SCC 598, the Court said PIL cannot be used for personal grudge or private motive; in *Janata Dal v. H.S. Chowdhary*, (1992) 4 SCC 305, it said that strangers cannot ordinarily interfere in a criminal prosecution. *State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal*, (2010) 3 SCC 402, laid down detailed guidelines: courts should encourage genuine PIL and discourage the rest; verify the petitioner's credentials and the correctness of the information; ensure that the matter is of real public interest and not for private gain or political motive; and impose exemplary costs on frivolous petitions. PIL should not be used for service matters, or where a private remedy is adequate.

**Criticism.**
1. Judicial overreach: courts have entered policy, budgets and administration (see *Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Club v. Chander Hass*, (2008) 1 SCC 683, a call for judicial restraint).
2. Weak follow-up: monitoring orders can outlive the case and the Court lacks the means to enforce them.
3. Floodgates: PILs add to a heavy docket, and the genuinely poor may still not be heard.
4. Lack of accountability of the petitioner for the claims made on behalf of others.

**Recent trend.** The Court has used PIL for electoral reform (*ADR*, electoral bonds, 2024), the environment (*M.K. Ranjitsinh*, 2024) and digital rights, while insisting on costs for abuse and on the separation of powers.

**Conclusion.** PIL has democratised access to justice and produced doctrines (absolute liability, the right to a speedy trial, the right to a healthy environment, workplace guidelines) that ordinary litigation would never have produced. Its future depends on the discipline set out in *Balwant Singh Chaufal*: a genuine petitioner, a real public cause, and a court that acts as a guardian and not as a ruler.

<a id="c-22"></a>
## C-22 · "Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right"

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q12 ("Article 32 is itself a fundamental right. Discuss with decided cases."). Related: Dec 2022 Q13 (remedies, answered at C-03), and PIL (C-21).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** text and meaning 3 · why it is itself a right (consequences) 5 · writs with cases 5 · compensation and guidelines 3 · limits and Art. 226 comparison 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** A right without a remedy is empty (*ubi jus ibi remedium*). The framers therefore placed the remedy in Part III itself. Dr Ambedkar told the Constituent Assembly that if he were asked to name the most important Article, without which the Constitution would be a nullity, he would name this one; "it is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it." Other rights in Part III are rights against the State; Art. 32 is the right to **enforce** them.

**Text.**
(1) The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III is **guaranteed.**
(2) The Supreme Court may issue **directions, orders or writs**, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari.
(3) Parliament may empower any other court to exercise these powers within its local limits.
(4) The right guaranteed by this Article shall **not be suspended** except as otherwise provided by the Constitution.

**Why it is "itself a Fundamental Right".** It stands in Part III under the heading "Right to Constitutional Remedies" (Arts. 32–35). Four consequences follow.
1. **The Supreme Court is bound to entertain the petition.** In *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras*, AIR 1950 SC 124, Patanjali Sastri J. said that Art. 32 provides a "guaranteed" remedy and that the Court is thus the protector and guarantor of Fundamental Rights; it cannot decline to enforce them. In *Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner, U.P.*, AIR 1963 SC 996, the Court struck down its own rule requiring a security deposit for filing an Art. 32 petition, because a procedural hurdle that makes the remedy costly impairs the guarantee.
2. **Suspension only by the Constitution itself.** Art. 359 allows the President in an Emergency to suspend the right to move the court for the rights named in the order, but not for Arts. 20 and 21 (after the 44th Amendment, 1978). In *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla*, (1976) 2 SCC 521, the majority had held that even Art. 21 could be suspended; that decision was repudiated in *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, (2017) 10 SCC 1.
3. **It cannot be abridged by amendment or ordinary law.** Judicial review by the Supreme Court and High Courts is part of the basic structure (*L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*, (1997) 3 SCC 261).
4. **The Court can mould the remedy.** "Directions, orders or writs" are wide enough to allow compensation, guidelines and continuing supervision.

**The five writs.** The Constitution adopts the English prerogative writs, but the Court is not bound by their technical rules.

| Writ | Meaning | Use | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | "You may have the body" | To release a person unlawfully detained; any person may file on the detenu's behalf | *Kanu Sanyal v. District Magistrate, Darjeeling*, (1973) 2 SCC 674; *Sunil Batra* (1978); *Sebastian M. Hongray v. Union of India*, (1984) 1 SCC 339 (disappeared persons) |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | To compel a public authority to perform a **public duty**; not against the President or a Governor personally (Art. 361), not for a private contract, not to compel a legislature to enact a law | *Lekhraj Sathramdas Lalvani v. N.M. Shah*, AIR 1966 SC 334; *Praga Tools Corporation v. C.A. Imanual*, (1969) 1 SCC 585 |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | Issued to an inferior court or tribunal **while proceedings are pending**, to stop it from exceeding jurisdiction or violating natural justice | *East India Commercial Co. v. Collector of Customs*, AIR 1962 SC 1893 |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | To **quash** an order already made by an inferior body for want or excess of jurisdiction, error of law apparent on the face of the record, or breach of natural justice | *T.C. Basappa v. T. Nagappa*, AIR 1954 SC 440; *Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Ahmad Ishaque*, AIR 1955 SC 233 |
| **Quo warranto** | "By what authority" | To test the title of a person holding a **public office** created by statute or the Constitution; any person may apply | *University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao*, AIR 1965 SC 491; *Kumar Padma Prasad v. Union of India*, (1992) 2 SCC 428 |

Prohibition is preventive and certiorari is curative; both rest on jurisdictional error and natural justice.

**Compensation, guidelines and continuing mandamus.** In *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*, (1983) 4 SCC 141, a man was kept in jail for years after his acquittal and the Court awarded compensation under Art. 32. *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K*, (1985) 4 SCC 677, followed for an illegal detention of a legislator. In *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa*, (1993) 2 SCC 746, for a custodial death, the Court held that compensation is a public-law remedy against the State for breach of Art. 21 and that sovereign immunity is no defence. The right to compensation under Art. 32 does not bar a civil suit or a prosecution. In *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.*, (1997) 1 SCC 416, the Court laid down arrest and detention guidelines, and in *Vishaka* (1997) and *Vineet Narain* (1998) it issued guidelines and kept the case under supervision.

**Limits.**
1. Art. 32 lies only for Fundamental Rights; for ordinary legal rights one goes to the High Court under Art. 226.
2. **Res judicata.** If a High Court has dismissed an Art. 226 petition on merits after a reasoned order, an Art. 32 petition on the same facts is barred (*Daryao v. State of U.P.*, AIR 1961 SC 1457); dismissal without reasons or for delay does not bar it. This rule is not applied to habeas corpus.
3. **Delay and alternative remedy.** Not an absolute bar under Art. 32, but the Court often asks a petitioner to go first to the High Court where the dispute turns on facts.
4. **Against whom.** The State (Art. 12). Private persons only where the right is enforceable against them (Arts. 17, 23, 24) or, following *Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P.*, (2023) 4 SCC 1, where the State has failed in its positive duty to protect the right.

**Art. 32 and Art. 226 compared.** Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and lies only for Fundamental Rights; the Court must entertain it. Article 226 is a constitutional power, not a Fundamental Right; it lies for Fundamental Rights and "any other purpose", and relief is discretionary. The writ jurisdiction of the High Court is wider in territory-bound subject matter and in the persons against whom it lies, though narrower in territory.

**Criticism.** Art. 32 is sometimes used for matters better suited to the High Court; PIL has widened access but the cost and delay of reaching Delhi remain; and compensation is often small. But the guarantee has produced the best of the Court's rights jurisprudence (*Maneka Gandhi*, *Vishaka*, *Puttaswamy*).

**Conclusion.** Article 32 is more than a procedural provision. By making the remedy a Fundamental Right, the Constitution has made the Supreme Court the sentinel of every other right in Part III. A right that can be refused by the very court that must enforce it would be no right at all.

## C-23 · Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q12 (20 marks: "With the help of decided cases discuss the relationship between Directive Principles of State Policy and Fundamental Rights."); Dec 2024 Q8 and Dec 2023 Q7 (B, 10 marks). **Asked in all three of the last three years; the most reliable question in the paper.**
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** nature of each Part and differences 4 · evolution of the relationship with cases 9 · aid to interpretation and implementation 3 · recent developments 2 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Part III (Arts. 12–35) guarantees justiciable rights that secure **political democracy**; Part IV (Arts. 36–51) lists directives to the State that aim at **social and economic democracy** and a welfare State. Granville Austin called the two Parts "the conscience of the Constitution". The Constitution does not say which prevails when they conflict, and the answer has changed over seventy years.

**Nature and differences.** Under Art. 37 the Directive Principles "shall not be enforceable by any court" but are "nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country" and it is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

| Basis | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Mostly negative limits on the State | Positive duties of the State |
| Enforcement | Justiciable (Arts. 32, 226); a law that violates them is void (Art. 13) | Non-justiciable (Art. 37); a law that ignores them is not void |
| Aim | Political democracy, individual liberty | Social and economic democracy, welfare State |
| Beneficiary | Individuals | Society |
| Source | US Bill of Rights | Irish Constitution (1937); Instrument of Instructions under the Government of India Act, 1935 |

**Evolution of the relationship**

*Stage 1: Fundamental Rights prevail (1951).* In *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan*, AIR 1951 SC 226, the Court struck down the communal Government Order and held that the Directive Principles, being non-enforceable, **must conform to and run as subsidiary to the chapter on Fundamental Rights.** The First Amendment inserted Art. 15(4) in response.

*Stage 2: harmonious construction (1958).* In *In re Kerala Education Bill, 1957*, AIR 1958 SC 956, the Court said that the Directive Principles cannot override Fundamental Rights, but the Court should try to **harmonise** the two Parts, and the Directives may be used to decide the extent of a Fundamental Right. *State of Kerala v. N.M. Thomas*, (1976) 2 SCC 310, treated reservation under Art. 16(4) as a facet of equality supported by Art. 46, and spoke of the Parts as supplementary.

*Stage 3: Golaknath, Art. 31C and Kesavananda (1967–1973).* *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1967 SC 1643, held Fundamental Rights "transcendental" and beyond amendment. Parliament responded with the 25th Amendment (1971), which inserted **Art. 31C**: a law giving effect to the policy of Art. 39(b) or (c) cannot be challenged for violating Arts. 14, 19 or 31, and a law containing a declaration to that effect cannot be questioned in court. In *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*, (1973) 4 SCC 225, the Court overruled *Golaknath*, upheld the first part of Art. 31C, but struck down the part that barred judicial review; and said that Parts III and IV together are fundamental and the Constitution must be read as an integrated whole.

*Stage 4: the 42nd Amendment and* Minerva Mills *(1976–1980).* The 42nd Amendment extended Art. 31C to **all** Directive Principles, giving Part IV primacy over Arts. 14 and 19. In *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India*, (1980) 3 SCC 625, a Constitution Bench struck down that extension. Chandrachud CJ said: "The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV. To give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution." The **balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the basic structure.** Art. 31C survives only to the extent upheld in *Kesavananda*. *Waman Rao v. Union of India*, (1981) 2 SCC 362, confirmed that the 1971 Art. 31C was valid, and *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.*, (1983) 1 SCC 147, upheld the nationalisation of coking-coal mines under it.

*Stage 5: Directive Principles as an aid to Fundamental Rights (1980 onwards).* The Court has used the Directives to give content to Art. 21 and Art. 14.

| Directive | Right | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Art. 39(d) equal pay for equal work | Arts. 14, 16 | *Randhir Singh v. Union of India*, (1982) 1 SCC 618; *State of Punjab v. Jagjit Singh*, (2017) 1 SCC 148 |
| Art. 39A free legal aid | Art. 21 | *M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra* (1978); *Hussainara Khatoon* (1980) |
| Arts. 41 and 45 education | Art. 21 | *Mohini Jain* (1992); *Unni Krishnan* (1993), then Art. 21A |
| Arts. 41 and 43 livelihood | Art. 21 | *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation*, (1985) 3 SCC 545 |
| Art. 47 health | Art. 21 | *Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of W.B.*, (1996) 4 SCC 37 |
| Art. 48A environment | Art. 21 | *M.C. Mehta* series; *Subhash Kumar* (1991) |
| Art. 46 weaker sections | Arts. 15(4), 16(4) | *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992) |

*Stage 6: Art. 39(b) and private property (2024).* In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024; nine judges; 7:1:1), the Court held that **not every privately owned resource** is a "material resource of the community" under Art. 39(b); the State may treat a private resource as such only after considering its nature, scarcity, impact on the public and concentration. The wide view of Krishna Iyer J. in *State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy*, (1977) 4 SCC 471, which *Sanjeev Coke* had followed, was not accepted.

**Implementation of Directives.** Art. 40: panchayats (73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992); Art. 39A: Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987; Art. 41: MGNREGA, 2005; Art. 42: Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (26 weeks after the 2017 amendment); Art. 45 and Art. 21A: Right to Education Act, 2009; Art. 47: National Food Security Act, 2013; Art. 48A: Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; Art. 39(d) and Art. 43: now the Code on Wages, 2019. Art. 44, the Uniform Civil Code, has been given effect in one State: the Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act, 2024 (in force 27 January 2025), with Bills passed in Gujarat and Assam in 2026 *(check assent)*.

**Criticism of Part IV.** Some called them pious aspirations without sanction; the arrangement lacks priority and some directives (prohibition, cow protection) are contested; resource constraints and weak political will slow implementation. Defenders reply that the Directives are the yardstick by which voters judge governments, and that the Court has made many of them indirectly enforceable.

**Present position.**
1. In a direct conflict, a Fundamental Right prevails over a Directive Principle, except where Art. 31C (as upheld in *Kesavananda*) applies to a law furthering Art. 39(b) or (c).
2. The Court tries first to **harmonise** the Parts.
3. The Directives are used to interpret and expand Fundamental Rights, to uphold laws as reasonable restrictions, and to guide legislation.
4. The balance between the Parts is a basic feature.

**Conclusion.** The relationship has moved from the supremacy of rights (*Champakam*), through harmony (*Kerala Education Bill*), to a doctrine of balance (*Minerva Mills*). Fundamental Rights are the means and the Directives are the goal: the Constitution wants individual liberty and social justice together.

## C-24 · Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q7 (B: "Write a short note on 'fundamental duties'"); Dec 2023 Q4, Dec 2024 Q5, Dec 2025 Q2 (A: "four fundamental duties"). A 20-mark question has not been asked, but the topic appears **every year** at the lower tiers.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 25 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** origin 3 · the eleven duties 4 · nature and enforceability 4 · use by courts and statutes 5 · criticism and importance 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Constitution as adopted in 1950 listed rights, and the only duties it spoke of were the duties of the State in Part IV. During the Emergency the Government appointed the **Swaran Singh Committee** (1976) to study constitutional amendments. Following its report the **42nd Amendment, 1976** inserted **Part IVA** with a single Article, **Art. 51A**, listing ten duties of citizens. The **86th Amendment, 2002** added a duty of parents and guardians as clause (k), making **eleven**. The idea is often traced to the UDHR (Art. 29), to the Soviet and Japanese constitutions, and to Gandhi's teaching that rights flow from the performance of duties.

**The eleven duties.** It is the duty of every **citizen** of India:

| Clause | Duty |
|---|---|
| (a) | to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem |
| (b) | to cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired the national struggle for freedom |
| (c) | to uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India |
| (d) | to defend the country and render national service when called upon |
| (e) | to promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people, transcending religious, linguistic, regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women |
| (f) | to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture |
| (g) | to protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures |
| (h) | to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform |
| (i) | to safeguard public property and to abjure violence |
| (j) | to strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity |
| (k) | who is a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to his child or ward between six and fourteen years (86th Amendment, 2002) |

**Nature.**
1. They are addressed to **citizens only**, not to the State or to non-citizens.
2. They are **non-justiciable**: the text has no sanction and no court can issue a writ to enforce them. Parliament can give them effect through ordinary legislation.
3. Some are vague or aspirational ("scientific temper", "excellence"); others can be legislated for (public property, environment, the flag, women's dignity).
4. Failure to perform a duty does not take away a Fundamental Right.
5. They are the **counterpart** of rights, but unlike Part III they do not bind the State, and unlike Part IV they bind the citizen.

**How the courts use Art. 51A.** The duties are not enforceable by writ, but the courts use them to interpret rights and statutes, to uphold laws that give effect to a duty as reasonable restrictions, and to issue directions to the State.

| Case | Use |
|---|---|
| *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of U.P.*, (1985) 2 SCC 431 | Art. 48A and Art. 51A(g) invoked to stop unlawful limestone quarrying in the Doon Valley. |
| *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India*, (1988) 1 SCC 471 | Art. 51A(g) cited to direct tanneries to treat their effluent before it reached the Ganga. |
| *Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala*, (1986) 3 SCC 615 | The duty to respect the National Anthem (Art. 51A(a)) does not compel anyone to sing it; Arts. 19(1)(a) and 25 protect respectful silence. |
| *A.I.I.M.S. Students' Union v. A.I.I.M.S.*, (2002) 1 SCC 428 | Fundamental Duties, though not enforceable by writ, are a valuable guide and aid to interpreting constitutional and legal issues; in a case of doubt they can tilt the balance. The State may enforce them by law. |
| *Union of India v. Naveen Jindal*, (2004) 2 SCC 510 | The right to fly the National Flag is part of Art. 19(1)(a), subject to the Flag Code and the duty of respect. |
| *State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat*, (2005) 8 SCC 534 | Art. 51A(g) (compassion for living creatures) read with Art. 48 to uphold a ban on slaughter of cows and their progeny. |
| *Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja*, (2014) 7 SCC 547 | Jallikattu and bullock-cart races were prohibited, relying on the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act and Art. 51A(g). On 18 May 2023 a Constitution Bench upheld the Tamil Nadu amendment legalising Jallikattu. |
| *Shyam Narayan Chouksey v. Union of India*, order of 30 November 2016 | Playing of the National Anthem in cinemas made compulsory by interim order, relying on Art. 51A(a); modified in 2017–2018 so that it is not mandatory. |

**Statutes that give effect to the duties.** Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 and the Flag Code, 2002 (clause (a)); Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 and BNS s. 152 (clause (c)); Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955, Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961, the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013 (clause (e)); Ancient Monuments Act, 1958 (clause (f)); Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960 (clause (g)); Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984 (clause (i)); RTE Act, 2009 (clause (k)).

**The Verma Committee (1999).** The Justice J.S. Verma Committee on Fundamental Duties identified existing legal provisions that give effect to some duties and recommended that duties be taught in schools and publicised. No constitutional amendment followed.

**Fundamental Duties and Directive Principles.** Both are non-enforceable, but the Directives are addressed to the State and the Duties to the citizen; the Directives were in the original Constitution and the Duties were added in 1976; the Duties have no Article numbered in Part IV but a separate Part, IVA.

**Criticism.**
1. No sanction: they are moral exhortations.
2. Vague terms such as "composite culture", "scientific temper" and "excellence".
3. They were added during the Emergency and were thought by some to be a counterweight to rights.
4. They are not exhaustive: paying taxes, voting and respecting the rights of others are not listed.
5. They are placed after the Directive Principles, outside Part III.

**Importance.**
1. A reminder that rights go with civic responsibilities.
2. An aid to interpretation, and a ground for restricting rights and upholding laws.
3. A basis for legislation and for curriculum content (NEP 2020 recommends including constitutional values).
4. A caution against destruction of public property and anti-national acts.

**Conclusion.** The Fundamental Duties complete the triangle of Parts III, IV and IVA. They do not create enforceable obligations by themselves, but they inform the Court's reading of rights and give Parliament a basis for legislation. As the Court said in the AIIMS case, though a writ will not lie, the duties are a valuable guide to interpretation.
