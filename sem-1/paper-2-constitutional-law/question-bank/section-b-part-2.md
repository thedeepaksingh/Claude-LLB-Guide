## B-17 · Doctrines of eclipse and severability

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q10 (20 marks: C-04).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 13 root 1 · eclipse with cases 4 · severability with cases 4 · difference 1

### Answer

Art. 13 makes laws inconsistent with Part III void "to the extent of" the contravention. Both doctrines grow from these words.

**Eclipse.** An inconsistent law is not dead but overshadowed; it cannot be enforced while the conflict lasts and revives, without re-enactment, when the conflict ends.
- *Keshav Madhav Menon* (1951): Art. 13(1) is prospective.
- *Bhikaji Narain Dhakras* (1955): a Motor Vehicles law inconsistent with Art. 19(1)(g) lay dormant from 26 January 1950 and revived when the First Amendment (1951) removed the conflict.
- *Deep Chand* (1959): a post-Constitution law that violates a right is stillborn.
- *Ambica Mills* (1974): a law violating only citizens' rights remains valid against non-citizens.

**Severability.** If only part of a law is void, only that part falls, provided it can be separated.
- *A.K. Gopalan* (1950): s. 14 of the Preventive Detention Act void, the rest upheld.
- *R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla* (1957) tests: would the legislature have enacted the rest? Are the parts separable? Does the rest form a workable whole?
- *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) severed para 7 of the Tenth Schedule.

**Difference.** Eclipse concerns time and revival; severability concerns parts.

**Conclusion.** Both save as much legislation as the Constitution permits.

## B-18 · Suspension of Fundamental Rights (Arts. 358 and 359)

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q5 and Dec 2024 Q3 (A, 4 marks). Related: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 352 1 · Art. 358 2 · Art. 359 3 · cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

Fundamental Rights are not absolute. In a national Emergency they may be affected in two ways.

**Art. 352.** An Emergency may be declared on the ground of war, external aggression or armed rebellion (the 44th Amendment, 1978, replaced "internal disturbance").

**Art. 358.** When an Emergency on the ground of **war or external aggression** is in force, the freedoms of **Art. 19 are automatically suspended**, with no order needed. An armed-rebellion Emergency does not have this effect.

**Art. 359.** The President may by order suspend the **right to move any court** for enforcing the Part III rights named in the order. The rights themselves survive; only enforcement is suspended. Since the 44th Amendment, **Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended**.

**Cases.**
- *Makhan Singh Tarsikka* (1964): the order suspends the right to move court for the named rights, but a challenge on grounds such as mala fides or action outside the law is still open.
- *ADM Jabalpur* (1976, 4:1; Khanna J. dissenting): no habeas corpus during the 1975 Emergency; overruled in *Puttaswamy* (2017).

**Other limits:** Art. 33 (armed forces), Art. 34 (martial law).

**Conclusion.** After 1978 the core of life and liberty can no longer be suspended.

## B-19 · The basic structure doctrine

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q1 and Dec 2023 Q1 (A: "fundamental law" and related). 20-mark form: C-05.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 1 · development 4 · basic features 2 · applications 2 · criticism 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** Parliament's power under Art. 368 does not include the power to destroy the basic structure or framework of the Constitution.

**Development.** *Shankari Prasad* (1951) and *Sajjan Singh* (1965): Part III can be amended. *Golaknath* (1967): it cannot. ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)**, thirteen judges, 7:6: *Golaknath* overruled; Parliament may amend any provision but not the basic structure. The first part of Art. 31C was upheld and its bar on judicial review struck down.

**Basic features** (an open list): supremacy of the Constitution; republican and democratic form; secularism; federalism; separation of powers; judicial review; unity and integrity; free and fair elections; rule of law; essence of the fundamental rights; balance between Parts III and IV.

**Applications.** *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975): Art. 329A(4) struck down. *Minerva Mills* (1980): the limited amending power is itself basic. *Waman Rao* (1981) and *Coelho* (2007): Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed. *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997): judicial review is basic. The NJAC case (2015) struck down the 99th Amendment.

**Criticism.** Judge-made; uncertain list; the last word rests with unelected judges. **Defence:** it stops a temporary majority from changing the Constitution's identity.

*Word count: 190*

## B-20 · Who can claim Fundamental Rights?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q3 (A: "Against whom Fundamental Rights are available?") is the companion question. The syllabus line is "who can claim Fundamental Rights".
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** citizens/persons 3 · non-citizens with cases 3 · corporations 3 · others 1

### Answer

**Citizens only:** Art. 15 (non-discrimination), Art. 16 (public employment), Art. 19 (six freedoms), Art. 29 (cultural rights), and Art. 30 (minorities) as usually classified.

**All persons, including foreigners:** Art. 14, Art. 20, Arts. 21 and 21A, Art. 22 (enemy aliens excepted from preventive-detention safeguards), Arts. 23–24, Arts. 25–28 and the remedy under Art. 32.

**Non-citizens.** *Anwar v. State of J&K* (1971) and *NHRC v. State of Arunachal Pradesh* (1996): Arts. 14 and 21 protect non-citizens; the State had to protect the life and liberty of Chakma refugees. *Hans Muller* (1955): a foreigner has no fundamental right to reside in India.

**Corporations.** A company is a "person" for Art. 14 but not a citizen for Art. 19 (*State Trading Corporation*, 1963; *Tata Engineering*, 1965). In *Bennett Coleman* (1972) the Court looked at the real effect on shareholders: where a law hits their own rights through the company, they may sue.

**Others.** Art. 33 lets Parliament restrict the rights of the armed forces and police. Through PIL anyone may sue for those who cannot (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981).

**Conclusion.** Rights for "citizens" are narrower than rights for "persons".

*Word count: 185*

## B-21 · Scheme of Fundamental Rights (Part III)

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Scheme of Fundamental Rights"); not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** groups 5 · features 3 · classification 2

### Answer

Part III (Arts. 12–35) lists the Fundamental Rights.

**Groups.**
1. **Definition and supremacy:** Art. 12 ("State"); Art. 13 (laws inconsistent with Part III are void).
2. **Equality:** Arts. 14–18 (equality before law; no discrimination; equal opportunity in public employment; untouchability abolished; titles abolished).
3. **Freedom:** Art. 19 (six freedoms), Art. 20 (conviction), Art. 21 (life and liberty), Art. 21A (education), Art. 22 (arrest and detention).
4. **Against exploitation:** Arts. 23–24.
5. **Religion:** Arts. 25–28.
6. **Cultural and educational rights:** Arts. 29–30.
7. **Remedies:** Art. 32.
8. **Savings and limits:** Arts. 31A–31C; Arts. 33–35.

The right to property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and is now in Art. 300A.

**Features.**
- They are **justiciable** (Arts. 32, 226).
- They are **superior**: a law that violates them is void (Art. 13).
- They are **not absolute**: reasonable restrictions apply.
- They can be amended, but not so as to alter the basic structure.

**Classification.** Some rights belong to citizens only (Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29, 30), others to all persons. Some are negative limits on the State (Art. 19); some need positive action (Art. 21A). Arts. 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 bind private persons too.

*Word count: 191*

## B-22 · The test of reasonable classification under Article 14

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q7 (B-09) and Dec 2022 Q11 (C-06) cover the same ground; this is the classification half as a stand-alone note.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** rule 3 · principles 3 · cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

Art. 14 forbids **class legislation** but allows **reasonable classification**. Two conditions must be met: (i) the classification must rest on an **intelligible differentia** that separates those grouped from those left out; (ii) the differentia must have a **rational nexus** with the object of the law (*Budhan Choudhry*, 1955).

**Principles** (*Ram Krishna Dalmia*, 1958): a law is presumed constitutional and the challenger bears the burden; a single person or company may be a class; the legislature may address the problem where it is most felt; the Court may notice history and common knowledge.

**Cases.**
- *Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri* (1951): a law concerning one company upheld.
- *Anwar Ali Sarkar* (1952): the Special Courts Act struck down; it gave no principle for choosing cases.
- *D.S. Nakara* (1983): classifying pensioners by date of retirement had no nexus with the object of pension; struck down.
- *Subramanian Swamy v. Director, CBI* (2014): s. 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act (prior sanction for inquiry against senior officers) struck down.

**Limit.** After *Royappa* (1974) State action must also be non-arbitrary.

**Conclusion.** Equality means equal treatment of those similarly placed.

*Word count: 182*

## B-23 · Article 15: prohibition of discrimination and special provisions

**Asked as:** not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025; the reservation clauses are covered at C-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text 3 · special provisions with amendments 3 · cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Art. 15(1):** the State shall not discriminate against any citizen on grounds **only** of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. **15(2):** no citizen shall suffer disability regarding access to shops, restaurants, hotels, places of entertainment, wells, tanks, roads and places of public resort; this binds private persons too.

**Special provisions.** 15(3): women and children. 15(4): socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs (First Amendment, 1951, after *Champakam Dorairajan*). 15(5): admission to educational institutions, including private unaided ones but not minority institutions (93rd Amendment, 2005). 15(6): economically weaker sections, up to 10% (103rd Amendment, 2019).

**Cases.**
- *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951): the communal Government Order violated Arts. 15(1) and 29(2).
- *M.R. Balaji* (1963): caste not the sole test; reservation about 50% at most.
- *C.B. Muthamma* (1979) and *Anuj Garg* (2008): rules based on stereotypes about women struck down.
- *Ashoka Kumar Thakur* (2008): Art. 15(5) and the OBC quota upheld, creamy layer excluded.
- *Pramati* (2014): Art. 15(5) does not apply to minority institutions.
- *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022, 3:2): the EWS amendment upheld.

**Meaning of "only".** If another ground also operates, Art. 15(1) is not breached.

**Conclusion.** Art. 15 permits special provision as a means to substantive equality.

*Word count: 195*

## B-24 · *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992)

**Asked as:** not asked as a stand-alone question in Dec 2022–2025; the central case in C-07 (reservation, Dec 2024 Q9).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** facts 2 · holdings 5 · later developments 3

### Answer

**Facts.** The V.P. Singh Government accepted the Mandal Commission report and, by an Office Memorandum of 13 August 1990, reserved 27% of central posts for backward classes; a 1991 memorandum added 10% for economically poorer sections. A nine-judge bench heard the challenge.

**Holdings.**
1. 27% reservation for backward classes under Art. 16(4) is valid; backwardness may be identified mainly by caste together with other criteria.
2. The **creamy layer** must be excluded.
3. Total reservation should **not exceed 50%** except in extraordinary situations.
4. Reservation applies to **initial appointment** and not to promotion (a position later changed by the 77th Amendment).
5. The 10% economic reservation was **struck down**: Art. 16(4) concerns backward classes, not poverty alone.
6. Permanent bodies should examine claims of inclusion and exclusion (the National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993).

**Later developments.** *M. Nagaraj* (2006): amendments upheld, but the State must show data on backwardness, inadequate representation and efficiency. *Jaishri Patil* (2021): Maratha quota struck down; the 50% limit stood. *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022): the 103rd Amendment (EWS) upheld, 3:2. *Davinder Singh* (2024): sub-classification within SCs and STs permitted.

**Conclusion.** *Indra Sawhney* is the foundation of the present law of reservation.

*Word count: 195*

## B-25 · Articles 17 and 18: untouchability and titles

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; syllabus line "Right to Equality (Articles 14 to 18)".
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 17 text and law 5 · Art. 18 text and case 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Art. 17.** "Untouchability" is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of it is an offence punishable by law.
- It binds the State **and private persons**.
- Undefined; it means the social practice of treating certain castes as unclean, not literal touching.
- **Statutes:** the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** in 1976; and the **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**.
- *Subhash Kashinath Mahajan* (2018) limited automatic arrest under the 1989 Act; Parliament restored it (s. 18A, 2018), upheld in *Prathvi Raj Chauhan* (2020).

**Art. 18.**
1. The State shall not confer any title (except military or academic).
2. A citizen shall not accept a foreign title.
3. A non-citizen holding an office of profit under the State needs the President's consent for a foreign title.
4. No office-holder may accept a gift or office from a foreign State without the President's consent.

*Balaji Raghavan* (1996, five judges): the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are **not "titles"** but must not be used as prefixes or suffixes.

**Purpose.** Both keep a republic of equals.

## B-26 · Restrictions on freedom of speech under Article 19(2)

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q9 and Dec 2022 Q12 (20 marks: C-10).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** conditions 2 · grounds with cases 6 · tests and conclusion 2

### Answer

A restriction on Art. 19(1)(a) must be (i) imposed by **law**, (ii) within one of the **eight grounds**, and (iii) **reasonable**. The grounds are exhaustive (*Kaushal Kishor*, 2023).

**Grounds.**
1. **Sovereignty and integrity of India** (16th Amendment, 1963). *Kedar Nath Singh* (1962) confined sedition to incitement to violence.
2. **Security of the State**: serious threats such as rebellion.
3. **Friendly relations with foreign States.**
4. **Public order** (First Amendment, 1951): *Lohia* (1966) distinguished law and order, public order and security of the State; *Rangarajan* (1989): the threat must be proximate, "like a spark in a powder keg".
5. **Decency or morality**: the *Hicklin* test gave way to community standards in *Aveek Sarkar* (2014).
6. **Contempt of court**: Contempt of Courts Act, 1971; truth is a defence since 2006.
7. **Defamation**: criminal defamation upheld in *Subramanian Swamy* (2016).
8. **Incitement to an offence**: *Shreya Singhal* (2015) struck down s. 66A IT Act; mere discussion and advocacy are protected.

**Reasonableness.** *Chintaman Rao* (1951): not arbitrary or excessive; *V.G. Row* (1952): extent of the evil and proportion; now proportionality (*Modern Dental College*, 2016; *Anuradha Bhasin*, 2020).

**Conclusion.** Speech is the rule; restriction is the exception.

*Word count: 192*

## B-27 · Sedition: from Kedar Nath Singh to BNS section 152

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; a topical sub-topic of Art. 19(2) (C-08, C-10).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** old law 2 · cases 4 · BNS s. 152 2 · criticism and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Old law.** Section 124A IPC punished bringing into hatred or contempt, or exciting disaffection towards, the Government established by law.

**Cases.**
- *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* (1962): s. 124A is valid only if confined to acts that tend to create public disorder or incite violence; criticism of the Government, however strong, is not sedition.
- *Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab* (1995): casually shouting slogans, without incitement, is not sedition.
- *S.G. Vombatkere* (11 May 2022): s. 124A kept in abeyance; no new FIRs, pending cases on hold, until the Government re-examined it.

**BNS, 2023** (in force 1 July 2024). The word "sedition" is dropped. **Section 152** punishes acts that endanger the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, committed by words, signs, electronic communication or financial means, with intent or knowledge that they will encourage secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities or separatist feelings.

**Criticism.** Critics say s. 152 is as broad as the old section and may chill dissent; the Government says it targets secession, not criticism. Its validity has been questioned; check the present position.

**Conclusion.** The test remains *Kedar Nath*: violence or incitement, not dissent.

## B-28 · Freedom of the press

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q13 (20 marks: C-09).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** basis 2 · cases 5 · limits and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Basis.** The Constitution does not mention the press. Freedom of the press is **derived from Art. 19(1)(a)**; it is no higher than a citizen's right and is subject to Art. 19(2). Dr Ambedkar explained that an editor exercises the citizen's right.

**Cases.**
- *Romesh Thappar* (1950): a ban on circulation of a journal violated the freedom; circulation is part of it.
- *Brij Bhushan* (1950): pre-censorship held void.
- *Sakal Papers* (1962): a newspaper price-and-page order, which cut circulation, struck down.
- *Bennett Coleman* (1972): newsprint quotas struck down; the Court looks to the **direct effect** of a law.
- *Indian Express* (1985): no immunity from taxes, but arbitrary levies can be challenged.
- *R. Rajagopal* (1994): the press may publish matters of public record; no prior restraint on public officials' conduct.
- *Madhyamam Broadcasting* (2023): refusing a channel's licence on undisclosed security material was disproportionate.
- *Anuradha Bhasin* (2020): press freedom extends to the internet.

**Limits.** Art. 19(2) grounds: defamation, contempt, public order, decency and others. *Sahara* (2012) allows postponement of publication to protect a fair trial.

**Conclusion.** The press has no special constitutional status, but the Court protects it against prior restraint and indirect economic control.

*Word count: 190*

## B-29 · Protection against self-incrimination (Article 20(3))

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13(a) (short note; 20-mark form: C-12).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text and elements 3 · cases 4 · limits and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Text.** "No person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself." The clause is modelled on the Fifth Amendment of the US Constitution.

**Elements.** (i) The person is **accused of an offence**; (ii) he is **compelled**; (iii) he is made **a witness against himself**, that is, to give testimony based on his own knowledge.

**Cases.**
- *M.P. Sharma* (1954, eight judges): search and seizure is not compelled testimony.
- *State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad* (1961, eleven judges): specimen handwriting, signatures and thumb impressions are not testimony.
- *Nandini Satpathy* (1978): the protection applies from the stage of police interrogation, and a person may stay silent on incriminating questions.
- *Selvi v. State of Karnataka* (2010): involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph and brain mapping violate Arts. 20(3) and 21.
- *Ritesh Sinha* (2019): a voice sample is not testimony.

**Limits.** Fingerprints, DNA and handwriting are outside it; the privilege can be waived by testifying. CrPC s. 161(2), now BNSS s. 180(2), lets a person decline to answer incriminating questions.

**Conclusion.** Art. 20(3) protects testimony, not the body. Its modern strength is the right to silence under Arts. 20(3) and 21.

*Word count: 189*

## B-30 · *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978)

**Asked as:** not asked as a stand-alone question; the central case in C-13 and B-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** facts 2 · issues 2 · holdings 4 · significance 2

### Answer

**Facts.** The passport of Maneka Gandhi, a journalist, was impounded in 1977 under s. 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967 "in the interests of the general public", without giving reasons or a hearing.

**Issues.** (i) Does "personal liberty" in Art. 21 include the right to travel abroad? (ii) Must the "procedure established by law" be fair? (iii) Are Arts. 14, 19 and 21 connected?

**Holding** (seven judges).
1. The right to go abroad is part of personal liberty.
2. The procedure must be **fair, just and reasonable**, not arbitrary or oppressive.
3. Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are **interconnected**, the "golden triangle": a law depriving personal liberty must satisfy each.
4. Natural justice (*audi alteram partem*) is part of fair procedure; the Government gave an undertaking to hear her.
5. Art. 14 forbids arbitrariness (*Royappa* applied).

**Significance.** It effectively overruled *A.K. Gopalan* (1950) and brought substantive due process into Art. 21. It opened the way to rights of dignity, health, shelter, a clean environment and privacy.

**Conclusion.** *Maneka Gandhi* turned Art. 21 from a guard against executive action into a test of the fairness of all laws.

*Word count: 187*

## B-31 · The right to privacy: *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017)

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025 (20 marks: C-16).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** early law 2 · facts and issue 2 · holding and test 4 · later developments 2

### Answer

**Early law.** *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962) denied that privacy is a fundamental right. *Gobind* (1975) assumed a limited right.

**Facts and issue.** A retired High Court judge, Justice K.S. Puttaswamy, challenged the Aadhaar scheme. The Union argued that privacy is not a fundamental right. A nine-judge bench decided the question on **24 August 2017**.

**Holding.** Unanimously, privacy is an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under **Art. 21** and of the freedoms in Part III. It includes **bodily integrity, decisional autonomy and informational privacy**. *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* were overruled to the extent of the contrary view, and *ADM Jabalpur* was declared wrongly decided.

**Test for any intrusion:** (i) **legality**, a law must authorise it; (ii) a **legitimate aim**; (iii) **proportionality**, with a rational nexus and the least intrusive means.

**Later developments.**
- *Puttaswamy (II)* (2018): Aadhaar upheld for welfare schemes and PAN linking; s. 57 struck down.
- *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018) and *Joseph Shine* (2019) rested on privacy and autonomy.
- The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the Rules of 2025 regulate digital data.

**Conclusion.** Privacy is now a core fundamental right.

*Word count: 191*

## B-32 · Right to die and euthanasia

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; a standard Art. 21 topic (C-13, C-14).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** early law 3 · *Common Cause* 4 · recent and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Right to die?** *P. Rathinam* (1994) struck down s. 309 IPC (attempted suicide). *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996, five judges) overruled it: the right to life does not include a right to die, so abetment of suicide remains an offence; but the natural process of dying in a terminally ill patient is different.

**Passive euthanasia.** *Aruna Shanbaug v. Union of India* (2011): passive euthanasia (withdrawal of life support) is allowed under court supervision.

***Common Cause v. Union of India* (9 March 2018, Constitution Bench).** The right to die with dignity is part of Art. 21. Passive euthanasia is lawful, and a person may make an **advance directive (living will)** refusing treatment if terminally ill or in a persistent vegetative state. Guidelines followed, simplified on 24 January 2023.

**Active euthanasia** remains unlawful. Under the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (s. 115) a person who attempts suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and is not punished.

**Recent.** *Harish Rana* (March 2026, as reported) applied this framework to a patient in a persistent vegetative state.

**Conclusion.** Dignity extends to the end of life, but a right to die is not recognised.

*Word count: 191*
