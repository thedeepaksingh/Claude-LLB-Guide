## B-33 · Right to education: Article 21A and the RTE Act, 2009

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q13 (20 marks: "Write short essay on Right to Education", C-17); Dec 2024 Q4 (A, 4 marks).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** development 3 · Art. 21A 2 · RTE features 3 · cases and criticism 2

### Answer

**Development.** Education was first a Directive Principle (Art. 45). *Mohini Jain* (1992) read it into Art. 21 and struck down capitation fees. *Unni Krishnan* (1993): free education until fourteen is a fundamental right; beyond that it depends on the State's means. The **86th Amendment (2002)** inserted **Art. 21A** (free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen, as the State may by law determine), substituted Art. 45 (early childhood care) and added Art. 51A(k) (parents' duty).

**RTE Act, 2009** (in force 1 April 2010): free and compulsory elementary education in a neighbourhood school; **25% reservation** in Class I in private unaided schools for weaker and disadvantaged children, with State reimbursement (s. 12(1)(c)); no capitation fee or screening; no expulsion or detention till elementary education is complete (the 2019 amendment lets States permit detention after re-examination); norms for teachers; School Management Committees.

**Cases.** *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan* (2012): Act and quota upheld. *Pramati* (2014): not applicable to minority institutions; a 2025 referral doubts this.

**Criticism.** Only ages 6–14 are covered; teacher shortages; poor learning outcomes.

**Conclusion.** The right is now justiciable; quality is the challenge.

## B-34 · Article 23: traffic in human beings and forced labour

**Asked as:** not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025 (20 marks: C-18).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text and terms 3 · cases 4 · statutes 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Text.** Art. 23(1) prohibits traffic in human beings, *begar* and other similar forms of forced labour, and makes contravention an offence. Art. 23(2) allows compulsory service for public purposes without discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class.

**Features.** It protects every person; it operates against the **State and private persons** alike; only Parliament may punish the offence (Art. 35).

**Cases.**
- ***PUDR v. Union of India*** (Asiad Workers, 1982): "force" includes **economic compulsion**; payment below the minimum wage is forced labour; Art. 23 binds private contractors.
- *Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan* (1983): famine-relief workers cannot be paid less than the minimum wage.
- *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984): a letter was treated as a writ; release and rehabilitation of bonded labourers; the right to dignity under Art. 21.
- *Neerja Chaudhary* (1984): rehabilitation is a State duty.
- *Deena v. Union of India* (1983): prisoners must be paid reasonable wages.
- *Vishal Jeet* (1990): trafficking and *devadasi* practice.

**Statutes.** Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976; Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956; BNS ss. 143–144 (trafficking); Code on Wages, 2019.

**Conclusion.** *PUDR* made Art. 23 a real guarantee against exploitation by the powerful.

## B-35 · Article 24: prohibition of child labour

**Asked as:** not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025 (20 marks: C-18).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text 2 · cases 4 · statute 3 · criticism and conclusion 1

### Answer

**Text.** "No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment." It is self-executing and binds the State and private employers. Arts. 39(e)–(f), 21A and 45 support it.

**Cases.**
- ***PUDR*** (1982): construction work is hazardous employment; a child under fourteen cannot be employed in it.
- ***M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu*** (1996): in the Sivakasi match and fireworks industry, children may not do hazardous work. Offending employers must pay **Rs. 20,000 per child** into a rehabilitation fund; alternative employment or a State contribution; education for working children.
- *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1997): carpet-industry children.

**Statute.** The **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**, as amended in **2016**, now the *Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act*: it bars employment of children below fourteen in any occupation, except helping a family enterprise after school hours and as a child artist; bars adolescents (14–18) from hazardous work; raises penalties (six months to two years and/or a fine of Rs. 20,000–50,000).

**Criticism.** The family-enterprise exception; weak enforcement.

**Conclusion.** Art. 24 is narrow; the Court and Parliament widened it.

## B-36 · The doctrine of essential religious practices

**Asked as:** not asked as a stand-alone question in Dec 2022–2025; the central test in C-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning and origin 3 · cases 4 · criticism and present position 3

### Answer

**Meaning.** The Court protects under Arts. 25 and 26 only those practices that are **essential or integral** to a religion; secular, peripheral or superstitious practices can be regulated. Test: would removing the practice fundamentally alter the religion?

**Cases.**
- ***Shirur Mutt*** (1954): religion includes rituals, but the State may regulate secular matters.
- *Durgah Committee* (1961): superstitious accretions are not protected.
- *Hanif Quareshi* (1958): cow sacrifice is not essential to Islam.
- *Acharya Jagdishwaranand* (1983): *tandava* dance not essential to Ananda Marga.
- *Ismail Faruqui* (1994): a mosque is not essential.
- *Shayara Bano* (2017): instant triple talaq set aside.
- ***Sabarimala*** (2018, 4:1): exclusion of women aged 10–50 not essential and contrary to Arts. 14, 15, 25; Indu Malhotra J. dissented: courts should not decide theology.

**Criticism.** "Essential" is not in the Constitution; judges decide theology; minority and oral traditions are at risk; an alternative test asks whether a practice is harmful or discriminatory.

**Present position.** A **nine-judge Bench** in the *Sabarimala* reference reserved judgment on ERP in May 2026; check whether it has been pronounced. The hijab case (split verdict, 13 October 2022) is also pending.

**Conclusion.** ERP remains the working test until the nine-judge Bench rules.

*Word count: 193*

## B-37 · Distinguish between Article 25 and Article 26

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; the standard distinction question for Unit 10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** table of differences 6 · cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

| Basis | Art. 25 | Art. 26 |
|---|---|---|
| **Holder** | Every **person**, an individual right | A **religious denomination** or section, a collective right |
| **Content** | Freedom of conscience; to profess, practise and propagate religion | To establish institutions; manage its affairs in matters of religion; own and acquire property; administer property in accordance with law |
| **Subject to** | Public order, morality, health **and other provisions of Part III** | Public order, morality, health **only** |
| **State regulation** | Art. 25(2): secular activity; social welfare and reform; throwing open Hindu temples | Administration of property "in accordance with law" |
| **Cases** | *Stainislaus* (1977); *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986); *Shayara Bano* (2017) | *Shirur Mutt* (1954); *Syedna Saifuddin* (1962); *Tilkayat* (1963); *S.P. Mittal* (1983) |

**Denomination:** a group with a common faith, organisation and distinctive name (*Shirur Mutt*). Ayyappa devotees (*Sabarimala*) and Auroville (*S.P. Mittal*) were held not to be one.

**Conflict.** In *Venkataramana Devaru* (1958) the Court harmonised the temple-entry law (Art. 25(2)(b)) with a denominational temple's right to exclude outsiders from specific ceremonies (Art. 26(b)).

**Limits.** The State may regulate administration of property but not take the right away (*Ratilal*; *Tilkayat*).

**Conclusion.** Art. 25 protects the believer; Art. 26 protects the community's organisation.

## B-38 · Articles 27 and 28

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; the two remaining Articles of the "Freedom of Religion" line.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 27 4 · Art. 28 5 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Art. 27.** No person shall be compelled to pay any **tax**, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or denomination.
- It forbids a **tax**, not a **fee**. A tax is a compulsory exaction for general public purposes; a fee is payment for a specific service (*Shirur Mutt*, 1954).
- General revenue may fund religious facilities such as security at fairs.
- Haj subsidy: *Rafique Shaikh Bhikan* (2012) directed it be phased out in ten years; it ended in 2018.

**Art. 28.**
1. **28(1):** no religious instruction in any educational institution **wholly maintained out of State funds**.
2. **28(2):** this does not apply to an institution **administered by the State** but established under an **endowment or trust** requiring religious instruction.
3. **28(3):** in a **recognised or aided** institution no one can be made to attend religious instruction or worship without consent (the guardian's, for a minor).
Private unaided, unrecognised institutions are outside it.

**Cases.** *Aruna Roy* (2002): teaching about religions and values in the National Curriculum Framework is not religious instruction. *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986): no compulsion to join in worship against conscience.

**Conclusion.** Both keep the State neutral.

## B-39 · Who is a minority? The AMU case (2024)

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; part of C-20.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning of minority 3 · AMU facts and holding 5 · present position and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Minority.** The Constitution does not define the word. *Kerala Education Bill* (1958): a community numerically less than half. *D.A.V. College* (1971) and *T.M.A. Pai* (2002, eleven judges): the **State is the unit** for linguistic and religious minorities. The Centre has notified six communities, including Jains.

**AMU.** *S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India* (1967): AMU was established by an Act of Parliament (1920) and not by the Muslim community, so it could not claim Art. 30(1). The 1981 amendment (establishment by the Muslim community) was struck down by the Allahabad High Court in 2006.

***Aligarh Muslim University v. Naresh Agarwal*** (8 November 2024, seven judges, **4:3**). The majority (Chandrachud CJI) **overruled *Azeez Basha***: statutory incorporation does not by itself rule out minority status; look at the **origin** (idea, funds, land, approvals), purpose and administration (the "indicia" test). Whether AMU is a minority institution was left to a regular Bench. Three judges dissented.

**Present position.** The regular Bench's decision on AMU is pending; check the stage.

**Conclusion.** The 2024 judgment shifts the test from the mode of creation to the substance of establishment and administration.

*Word count: 184*

## B-40 · The five writs

**Asked as:** syllabus line "Constitutional remedies (Article 32)"; not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025. Fuller treatment: C-22.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each writ 1.5 + introduction/conclusion 2.5

### Answer

Art. 32(2) empowers the Supreme Court, and Art. 226 the High Courts, to issue writs in the nature of five English prerogative writs.

1. **Habeas corpus** ("you may have the body"): releases a person unlawfully detained; anyone may apply for the detenu (*Kanu Sanyal*, 1973; *Sebastian Hongray*, 1984).
2. **Mandamus** ("we command"): compels a public authority to perform a **public duty**. It does not lie against the President or a Governor personally (Art. 361) or to compel legislation (*Lekhraj Sathramdas*, 1966; *Praga Tools*, 1969).
3. **Prohibition** ("to forbid"): stops an inferior court or tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction **while proceedings are pending** (*East India Commercial Co.*, 1962); preventive, where certiorari is curative.
4. **Certiorari** ("to be certified"): **quashes** an order already made for lack or excess of jurisdiction, error of law apparent on the face of the record, or breach of natural justice (*T.C. Basappa*, 1954; *Hari Vishnu Kamath*, 1955).
5. **Quo warranto** ("by what authority"): tests the title to a **public office** created by statute or the Constitution; any person may apply (*University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao*, 1965; *Kumar Padma Prasad*, 1992).

**Conclusion.** The writs check illegal detention and abuse of power.

## B-41 · Article 32 and Article 226

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q12 (20 marks, "Article 32 is itself a fundamental right", C-22); the distinction is a standard question.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** table 7 · cases 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

| Basis | Art. 32 | Art. 226 |
|---|---|---|
| **Court** | Supreme Court | High Courts |
| **Nature** | Itself a **Fundamental Right** in Part III | A constitutional **power**, not a Fundamental Right |
| **Purpose** | Only for **Fundamental Rights** | Fundamental Rights **and "any other purpose"** (legal rights) |
| **Duty** | The Court **must** entertain the petition | **Discretionary**; alternative remedy and delay are usual grounds to refuse |
| **Against** | The State (Art. 12) | "Any person or authority", including the Government |
| **Territory** | Whole of India | The High Court's territory, or where the cause of action arises |
| **Suspension** | Only as the Constitution provides (Art. 32(4); Art. 359) | Not covered by Art. 32(4); judicial review is basic structure |

**Cases.** *Romesh Thappar* (1950): the Supreme Court is "protector and guarantor" of rights. *Prem Chand Garg* (1963): a security deposit rule struck down. *Daryao* (1961): res judicata bars an Art. 32 petition after an Art. 226 petition has been decided on merits. *L. Chandra Kumar* (1997): judicial review is basic structure.

**Trend.** Where facts are disputed, the Supreme Court often sends petitioners to the High Court.

**Conclusion.** Art. 32 is narrower in subject but a guaranteed right; Art. 226 is wider but discretionary.

*Word count: 185*

## B-42 · Public interest litigation: abuse, limits and guidelines

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q11, Dec 2024 Q12, Dec 2023 Q11 (20 marks: C-21). This is the "abuse and guidelines" half as a 10-mark note.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning and features 3 · abuse 3 · guidelines with cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Meaning.** PIL relaxes the rule of *locus standi*: a public-spirited person may approach the court for those who cannot (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981). Features: letters treated as petitions (epistolary jurisdiction), Commissioners and *amici*, continuing mandamus. Leading cases: *Hussainara Khatoon* (1980), *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984), *M.C. Mehta*, *Vishaka* (1997), *Vineet Narain* (1998).

**Abuse.** PIL has been used for publicity, political ends or private grudge. *Dattaraj Nathuji Thaware* (2005) spoke of "publicity interest litigation" and imposed costs. *Subhash Kumar* (1991): PIL cannot serve a private motive. *Janata Dal v. H.S. Chowdhary* (1992): strangers cannot ordinarily interfere in criminal prosecutions.

**Guidelines** (*State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal*, 2010):
1. Encourage genuine PIL and discourage the rest.
2. Verify the petitioner's credentials and the correctness of the information.
3. Ensure the matter is of real public interest and not private gain or political motive.
4. Impose exemplary costs on frivolous petitions.
5. High Courts should frame rules to regulate PIL.

**Limits.** PIL is unsuitable for service matters and where a private remedy exists. Courts are cautioned against policy and administration (*Aravali Golf Club*, 2008).

**Conclusion.** PIL is valuable if confined to genuine public causes.

## B-43 · Compensation for violation of Fundamental Rights under Article 32

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; part of C-22.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** principle 2 · cases 5 · limits 3

### Answer

**Principle.** Art. 32 empowers the Court to issue "directions, orders or writs". From the 1980s the Court has awarded **compensation** as a public-law remedy for violation of Art. 21; sovereign immunity is no defence.

**Cases.**
- ***Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*** (1983): a prisoner was kept in jail for years after his acquittal; the Court awarded compensation, the first such award.
- *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985): compensation for the illegal detention of a legislator.
- ***Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa*** (1993): compensation for a custodial death; it is a remedy in public law against the State and is distinct from a tort remedy.
- *D.K. Basu* (1997): arrest guidelines against custodial violence.

**Limits.** It is **without prejudice** to a civil suit or prosecution of the wrongdoer. The Court uses it where facts are clear and undisputed. It is paid by the State, not damages for a private wrong.

**Conclusion.** Compensation makes the remedy real: a right violated by the State should lead to relief and not only a declaration.

## B-44 · Directive Principles: classification and importance

**Asked as:** not asked at B level in Dec 2022–2025; the relationship with Fundamental Rights is B-07 and C-23.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** nature 2 · classification 4 · importance 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Nature.** Part IV (Arts. 36–51) is borrowed from the Irish Constitution. Under Art. 37 the directives are **not enforceable by any court** but are "fundamental in the governance of the country", and it is the State's duty to apply them in making laws.

**Classification** (an academic device):
- **Socialistic:** Arts. 38, 39 (including 39(d) equal pay for equal work), 39A (free legal aid), 41 (work, education), 42 (humane conditions, maternity relief), 43 (living wage), 43A (workers' participation), 47 (nutrition and public health).
- **Gandhian:** Art. 40 (village panchayats), 43 (cottage industries), 43B (co-operatives), 46 (SCs, STs and weaker sections), 47 (prohibition), 48 (agriculture; cattle).
- **Liberal-intellectual:** Art. 44 (uniform civil code), 45 (early childhood care), 48A (environment), 49 (monuments), 50 (separation of judiciary from executive), 51 (international peace).

**Importance.**
1. A guide to legislation and administration: panchayati raj (Art. 40), MGNREGA (Art. 41), the National Food Security Act, 2013 (Art. 47).
2. A yardstick of governments.
3. An aid to interpreting rights: *Randhir Singh* (1982), *Olga Tellis* (1985).
4. Complementary to rights: "conscience of the Constitution" (*Minerva Mills*).

**Criticism.** Non-justiciable, no priority among them, resource limits.

**Conclusion.** They set the goal of a welfare State.

## B-45 · The Uniform Civil Code (Article 44)

**Asked as:** not asked in Dec 2022–2025; a recurring current topic.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text and debate 2 · cases 3 · developments 3 · arguments 2

### Answer

**Text.** Art. 44: "The State shall endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India." It is a directive, not enforceable. Some Assembly members opposed it; Ambedkar saw it as desirable but not to be forced.

**Cases.**
- *Shah Bano* (1985): maintenance for a divorced Muslim wife under the criminal law; Art. 44 called "a dead letter". Parliament responded with the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986.
- *Sarla Mudgal* (1995): conversion to Islam does not dissolve a first marriage; the Court urged a UCC.
- *John Vallamattom* (2003): s. 118 of the Indian Succession Act struck down; a UCC urged again.

**Developments.** The 21st Law Commission (2018) said a UCC was "neither necessary nor desirable at this stage". The 22nd Law Commission invited views in June 2023. The Goa Civil Code is an example. The **Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act, 2024** came into force on 27 January 2025; Bills were passed in Gujarat and Assam in 2026 (check assent).

**For:** national integration; gender justice. **Against:** religious freedom (Art. 25), diversity, lack of consensus.

**Conclusion.** The Court has urged a UCC, but it remains a policy for the legislatures.

## B-46 · Article 31C and *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980)

**Asked as:** not asked as a stand-alone question in Dec 2022–2025; the key case in B-07 and C-23.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Art. 31C 3 · *Minerva Mills* facts and holding 5 · significance 2

### Answer

**Art. 31C.** Inserted by the 25th Amendment (1971): a law giving effect to the policy in Art. 39(b) or (c) is not void for violating Arts. 14, 19 or 31, and a law with a declaration to that effect cannot be questioned in court. *Kesavananda* (1973) upheld the first part and struck down the bar on judicial review. The **42nd Amendment (1976)** extended Art. 31C to **all** Directive Principles and added Art. 368(4)–(5).

**Facts.** Minerva Mills, a textile company, was taken over under the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974; it challenged ss. 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment.

**Holding** (Chandrachud CJ, 4:1; Bhagwati J. dissenting).
1. Section 55 (Art. 368(4)–(5)) struck down: **limited power cannot be enlarged into absolute power**.
2. Section 4 (extension of Art. 31C) struck down: the Constitution "is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Parts III and IV".
3. The **balance of Parts III and IV is basic structure.**
4. Art. 31C survives only as upheld in *Kesavananda*, for Art. 39(b) and (c).

**Later.** *Sanjeev Coke* (1983) upheld nationalisation under Art. 31C.

**Conclusion.** The case secures judicial review of amendments and the harmony of rights and directives.

*Word count: 195*

## B-47 · Environment protection and Article 21

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q10 and Dec 2022 Q9 (20 marks: C-15).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** constitutional basis 2 · right to environment 3 · principles 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Basis.** The 42nd Amendment (1976) added Art. 48A (State to protect the environment) and Art. 51A(g) (citizens' duty). Both are non-justiciable, so the Court read a **right to a healthy environment into Art. 21**.

**Right to environment.** *Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra* (1985): the Doon Valley limestone quarrying was stopped. *Subhash Kumar* (1991): the right to life includes pollution-free water and air.

**Principles evolved.**
- **Absolute liability:** *M.C. Mehta* (Oleum Gas Leak, 1987) rejected the *Rylands v. Fletcher* exceptions.
- **Precautionary principle and polluter pays:** *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum* (1996); *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action* (1996).
- **Public trust doctrine:** *M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath* (1997).
- **Sustainable development:** *Narmada Bachao Andolan* (2000).
- **Continuing mandamus:** *T.N. Godavarman* (1997) on forests; the Taj Trapezium case.
- **Climate:** *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024) recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change.

**Institutions.** Water Act, 1974; Air Act, 1981; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; the **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010**.

**Evaluation.** A right enforceable without a statute, but implementation is weak and orders on development vary.

**Conclusion.** The statement is right: the environment has constitutional protection through Art. 21.

*Word count: 180*
