## 1. Background: secularism and the Indian model

- The Constitution did not originally use the word "secular"; the **42nd Amendment (1976)** added it to the Preamble, but the idea was always present in Arts. 14–16, 25–30. The Supreme Court held **secularism a basic feature** in *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994) 3 SCC 1.
- **Meaning:** the State has **no religion of its own** and treats all religions **equally** (*sarva dharma sama bhava*), while retaining the power to regulate secular activities connected with religion and to carry out **social reform**.

| Model | Approach to religion |
|---|---|
| **India** | Positive secularism: **principled distance**: equal respect, State may regulate/reform (Art. 25(2)), provides for minority educational rights (Art. 30) |
| **USA** | Strict "wall of separation" (First Amendment: Establishment and Free Exercise clauses) |
| **France** | *Laïcité*: strict exclusion of religion from public institutions |

- **Constituent Assembly:** K.M. Munshi, Ambedkar, Patel and others debated "propagate" (the Christian minority sought it), the kirpan explanation, and the Hindu temple-entry provision.

## 2. Article 25: freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion

### 2.1 Text

> **Art. 25.** *(1) Subject to **public order, morality and health** and to the **other provisions of this Part**, all persons are **equally entitled to freedom of conscience** and the right freely to **profess, practise and propagate** religion.*
> *(2) Nothing in this article shall affect the operation of any existing law or prevent the State from making any law (a) **regulating or restricting any economic, financial, political or other secular activity** which may be associated with religious practice; (b) providing for **social welfare and reform** or the **throwing open of Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus**.*
> *Explanation I: The wearing and carrying of **kirpans** shall be deemed to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion.*
> *Explanation II: In sub-clause (b) of clause (2), the reference to Hindus shall be construed as including a reference to persons professing the **Sikh, Jaina or Buddhist** religion, and the reference to Hindu religious institutions shall be construed accordingly.*

### 2.2 Features
- **Available to all persons** (citizens and non-citizens); guarantees both **individual conscience** and **outward practice**.
- **Subject to** (i) public order, morality, health; (ii) the other provisions of Part III (e.g., Art. 14, 15, 17, 21); (iii) State regulation of secular activity and social reform (Art. 25(2)).
- **Includes** freedom of belief, worship, rituals, observances and, for the believer, **atheism**: freedom of conscience includes the right not to believe.
- **Against the State only** (unlike Art. 23).

### 2.3 Elements explained

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Freedom of conscience** | Inner freedom to form one's own beliefs or none |
| **Profess** | Open declaration of faith |
| **Practise** | Performance of rituals, worship, ceremonies and observances |
| **Propagate** | To transmit or spread one's religion by exposition of its tenets; **does not include a right to convert** another person (*Rev. Stainislaus v. State of M.P.*, (1977) 1 SCC 677) |
| **Secular activity** (Art. 25(2)(a)) | Economic, financial, political activities linked with religion (e.g., management of property, fairs, offerings, appointment of employees) |
| **Social welfare and reform** (Art. 25(2)(b)) | Abolition of evil customs: devadasi, sati, untouchability in temples, polygamy-related reforms; temple-entry laws |

### 2.4 Key cases on Art. 25

| Case | Citation | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| **Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt** | AIR 1954 SC 282 (Mukherjea J.) | **Religion** is not merely belief; it includes **rituals and observances**; Art. 25 protects practices that are **essential/integral** to the religion, to be determined by the **doctrines of that religion**; the State may regulate **secular** aspects (administration of property) but may not take over **religious** affairs; also drew the **tax v. fee** distinction for Art. 27 |
| **Ratilal Panachand Gandhi v. State of Bombay** | AIR 1954 SC 388 | The State can regulate secular matters such as trusts and property management; the State cannot interfere with religious practices; no right to maladminister property |
| **Durgah Committee, Ajmer v. Syed Hussain Ali** | AIR 1961 SC 1402 | Only practices **essential and integral** are protected; **superstitious beliefs and accretions** are not; the Court looks at the antiquity and the importance of the practice |
| **Sardar Syedna Taher Saifuddin Saheb v. State of Bombay** | AIR 1962 SC 853 (Constitution Bench) | The **Bombay Prevention of Excommunication Act, 1949** was struck down: excommunication by the Dawoodi Bohra head (Dai) on religious grounds is a matter of religion protected by Art. 26(b) |
| **Tilkayat Shri Govindlalji Maharaj v. State of Rajasthan** | AIR 1963 SC 1638 | Practices must be **integral to the religion**, not merely traditional; the Court separated the religious affairs of the Nathdwara denomination from the **secular management** of the temple and its property, which the State may regulate (read the report for which sections of the Nathdwara Temple Act, 1959 survived) |
| **Seshammal v. State of Tamil Nadu** | (1972) 2 SCC 11 | The **appointment of archakas** is secular and can be regulated, but the *Agamas* governing ritual must be respected |
| **Acharya Jagdishwaranand Avadhuta v. Commissioner of Police, Calcutta** | (1983) 4 SCC 522 | **Tandava dance** in processions is not an essential practice of the Ananda Marga faith; public order prevails |
| **S.P. Mittal v. Union of India** | (1983) 1 SCC 51 | **Auroville** is not a "religious denomination" for Art. 26; the Sri Aurobindo Society's teachings are a philosophy, so that the Act taking over its management is valid |
| **Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala** | (1986) 3 SCC 615 | Jehovah's Witness children who respectfully stood but did not sing the **national anthem** could not be expelled: Arts. 19(1)(a) and 25 |
| **Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar** | AIR 1958 SC 731 | Ban on cow slaughter is valid; sacrifice of a cow on Bakr-Id is **not an essential** part of Islam |
| **M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India** | (1994) 6 SCC 360 | A **mosque is not essential** to the practice of Islam; the State may acquire a place of worship (subject to law); the right to worship is not tied to a particular place |
| **Church of God (Full Gospel) in India v. K.K.R. Majestic Colony Welfare Association** | (2000) 7 SCC 282 | **Loudspeakers** are not an essential part of religion; noise pollution can be regulated |
| **Rev. Stainislaus v. State of M.P.** | (1977) 1 SCC 677 | Right to **propagate** does not include **conversion**; the Orissa and Madhya Pradesh Freedom of Religion Acts upheld (forced or induced conversion disturbs public order) |

## 3. Essential religious practices (ERP) doctrine

- **Origin:** *Shirur Mutt* (1954) used the word "essential" to separate **core** practices (protected) from **secular or peripheral** ones (regulable). The Court holds that the **religion itself** decides, but in practice the **Court has decided** what is essential, using the test: **would removal of the practice fundamentally alter the religion?**
- **Developments:** *Durgah Committee* (1961: not superstitious accretions) → *Tilkayat* (1963) → *Acharya Jagdishwaranand* (1983) → *Ismail Faruqui* (1994) → *Shayara Bano* (2017) → *Sabarimala* (2018; Indu Malhotra dissent: courts should not be arbiters of religion).
- **Criticism:**
  1. Courts are asked to adjudicate **theology**; judges are ill-equipped and risk interfering with belief.
  2. The doctrine is **not found in the Constitution**: the text speaks of "matters of religion" (Art. 26(b)), not "essential" practices.
  3. **Protection depends on proof** of essentiality; minority practices are most at risk.
  4. Competing approach: **constitutional morality** and individual rights (Arts. 14, 15, 17, 21) should limit religious practice (Sabarimala majority).
  5. Alternative: a **rights-based test** (is the practice harmful, discriminatory?) rather than an essentiality test; several academics and members of the Sabarimala bench have suggested this.
- **Hijab:** the Karnataka High Court (15 March 2022) held that wearing the hijab is **not an essential religious practice**; the Supreme Court gave a **split verdict** on 13 Oct 2022 (Gupta J. upheld the Government order; Dhulia J. set it aside, saying essential practices should not decide the case), and the matter went to the Chief Justice for a larger bench *(verify present status)*.

## 4. Article 26: freedom to manage religious affairs

### 4.1 Text

> **Art. 26.** *Subject to **public order, morality and health**, every **religious denomination or any section thereof** shall have the right (a) to **establish and maintain institutions** for religious and charitable purposes; (b) to **manage its own affairs in matters of religion**; (c) to **own and acquire movable and immovable property**; and (d) to **administer such property in accordance with law**.*

### 4.2 Notes
- **Collective right** of a **religious denomination** (a group with common faith and organisation, a distinctive name and a common spiritual organisation: *Shirur Mutt*; *S.P. Mittal*).
- **Subject only to public order, morality and health**, not to "other provisions of Part III": hence broader than Art. 25 in this respect, but see *Sabarimala*, where the Court read Art. 26 with Art. 25 and Art. 14.
- **Matters of religion** v. **administration of property:** the denomination controls the former; the State may regulate the latter (clause (d): "in accordance with law"): a law cannot **take away** the right to administer property altogether (*Shirur Mutt*; *Ratilal*; *Tilkayat*).

### 4.3 Art. 25 v. Art. 26

| Basis | Art. 25 | Art. 26 |
|---|---|---|
| Holder | **Individual** (any person) | **Religious denomination** or section |
| Content | Conscience; profess, practise, propagate | Establish institutions; manage religious affairs; own and administer property |
| Subject to | Public order, morality, health **and other provisions of Part III** | Public order, morality, health **only** |
| State regulation | Art. 25(2): secular activities, social welfare and reform, temple entry | "In accordance with law" for property |
| Key cases | *Stainislaus*, *Bijoe Emmanuel*, *Shayara Bano*, *Sabarimala* | *Shirur Mutt*, *Syedna Saifuddin*, *Tilkayat*, *S.P. Mittal* |

### 4.4 When Arts. 25(2)(b) and 26(b) conflict
***Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore*** AIR 1958 SC 255: the Madras Temple Entry Authorization Act (throwing open Hindu temples to all) v. the right of a **denominational temple** to exclude outsiders. The Court harmonised the two: the temple was a public temple, so Art. 25(2)(b) prevailed in general, but the denomination's right to exclude non-members during **specified religious ceremonies** was preserved under Art. 26(b). The decision is a standard example of **harmonious construction**.

### 4.5 Waqf and other statutory regulation
- ***Waqf (Amendment) Act, 2025*** (renaming the 1995 Act the *Unified Waqf Management, Empowerment, Efficiency and Development Act*: verify the title): challenged under Arts. 14, 25, 26, 29, 30. By an **interim order of 15 September 2025** (a bench of CJI B.R. Gavai and A.G. Masih J.) the Supreme Court **declined to stay the whole Act** but **stayed** (i) the requirement that a waqif must have practised Islam for five years (pending rules), (ii) part of the provision on the determination of disputed government property, and (iii) limited the number of non-Muslim members to **four** in the Central Waqf Council and **three** in State Waqf Boards; and, as reported, indicated that the Board's ex-officio chief executive should as far as practicable be a Muslim (reports differ on the exact designation). **Final adjudication pending** *(secondary-source summary; verify the order and the present stage)*.
- **Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991:** freezes the religious character of places of worship as on 15 Aug 1947; upheld as a "non-retrogression" safeguard in *M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das* (the Ayodhya judgment, (2020) 1 SCC 1, 9 Nov 2019); its validity is under challenge in the Supreme Court *(verify the interim directions)*.

## 5. Article 27: no tax for promotion of a religion

> **Art. 27.** *No person shall be compelled to pay any taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.*

- **Prohibits taxes**, not **fees**: a **tax** is a compulsory exaction for general public purposes; a **fee** is a charge for a specific service (*Shirur Mutt* (1954) held the s. 76 levy under the Madras Act to be a **tax**, not a fee, because it had no quid pro quo with services; it was void because the State legislature lacked competence to impose it, but it did not offend Art. 27, since its object was to administer religious institutions, not to promote a religion. *Prafull Goradia* (2011): Art. 27 is breached only if a substantial part of a tax is used for one religion; the Haj subsidy challenge failed).
- The **State may spend general revenue** on religious matters without violating Art. 27 as long as no special tax is levied (e.g., pilgrimage facilities, security at fairs).
- **Haj subsidy:** challenged under Art. 27; in *Union of India v. Rafique Shaikh Bhikan* (2012) 6 SCC 265 the Supreme Court directed that the subsidy be **phased out** over ten years; the Government discontinued it in **2018** *(verify)*.
- **Limits:** prohibition covers a "particular religion or denomination", not the promotion of religion in general; Art. 27 does not bar the State from taxing a religion's property under general law.

## 6. Article 28: freedom as to attendance at religious instruction or worship in educational institutions

### 6.1 Text

> **Art. 28.** *(1) No religious instruction shall be provided in any educational institution **wholly maintained out of State funds**.*
> *(2) Nothing in clause (1) shall apply to an educational institution **administered by the State** but established under any **endowment or trust** which requires that religious instruction shall be imparted in such institution.*
> *(3) No person attending any educational institution **recognised by the State** or **receiving aid out of State funds** shall be required to take part in any religious instruction that may be imparted in such institution or to attend any religious worship that may be conducted in such institution or in any premises attached thereto **unless such person or, if such person is a minor, his guardian has given his consent**.*

### 6.2 Categories of institutions

| Type | Religious instruction |
|---|---|
| **Wholly maintained by State funds** | **Prohibited** (Art. 28(1)) |
| **State-administered, established under an endowment/trust requiring it** | **Allowed** (Art. 28(2)) |
| **Recognised or State-aided** | Allowed, but **no compulsion** to attend without consent (Art. 28(3)) |
| **Private, unaided, unrecognised** | Free from Art. 28 |

### 6.3 Cases
- ***Aruna Roy v. Union of India*** (2002) 7 SCC 368: the introduction of **teaching about religions** (value education; the National Curriculum Framework) in schools is not "religious instruction" in the prohibited sense; the distinction is between **teaching religious doctrine** and **teaching about the study of religions** and moral values.
- ***D.A.V. College v. State of Punjab*** (1971) 2 SCC 269: the teaching of Guru Nanak's life and teachings in a university as a subject of study is not prohibited religious instruction *(verify)*.
- **Related:** *Bijoe Emmanuel* (1986): no compulsion to join in worship (national anthem) against conscience.

## 7. Related issues: conversion and "love jihad" laws, UCC and personal laws

- **Anti-conversion laws:** several States (e.g., Orissa 1967, Madhya Pradesh 1968, and later Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal, Gujarat) prohibit conversion by force, fraud or inducement. *Stainislaus* upheld the early laws; recent laws regulating marriage-linked conversion are under challenge, and the Supreme Court has examined their constitutionality and privacy implications *(verify status)*. Contrast the protected **right to marry a person of one's choice** (*Shafin Jahan*, 2018; Unit 8).
- **Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44):** see Unit 13 (DPSP). **Shah Bano** (1985) and **Shayara Bano** (2017) show the tension between personal laws and Fundamental Rights.
- **Hindu religious endowment laws:** State control of Hindu temple administration (Madras HRE Act and its successors) is criticised as discriminatory because mosques and churches are not similarly managed; courts have upheld State regulation of secular administration but not takeover of religious affairs (*Shirur Mutt*; *Ratilal*; *Tilkayat*).

## 8. Criticism and balance

1. **ERP doctrine** places judges as theologians; it favours dominant, text-based practices and may disadvantage oral traditions.
2. **Art. 25(2)(b)** treats Hindu religion differently (State can throw open temples and reform), which critics describe as asymmetric; defenders point to historical caste exclusion.
3. **Conversion:** the right to propagate is circumscribed by State laws that arguably give broad discretion to the State.
4. **Personal laws and Fundamental Rights:** conflict between gender equality and religious autonomy (triple talaq, Sabarimala, hijab).
5. **Strengths:** a flexible, reform-friendly secular model; strong denomination autonomy over religious affairs; judicial willingness to test practices against equality and dignity.
