## 1. Background: how the Constitution came to be

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1946 | Cabinet Mission Plan; Constituent Assembly elected indirectly by provincial assemblies |
| 9 Dec 1946 | First meeting of the Constituent Assembly (Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha as temporary president; Dr. Rajendra Prasad elected president on 11 Dec) |
| 13 Dec 1946 | Jawaharlal Nehru moved the **Objectives Resolution**; adopted 22 Jan 1947 (source of the Preamble) |
| 29 Aug 1947 | **Drafting Committee** set up, chaired by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** |
| 26 Nov 1949 | Constitution **adopted** (the Preamble's "this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949") |
| 26 Jan 1950 | Constitution **came into force** (Republic Day; chosen to mark the 1930 *Purna Swaraj* resolution) |

**Original size:** 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. **Today:** over 450 Articles (counting lettered Articles), 25 Parts and 12 Schedules (the Ninth to Twelfth Schedules were added by amendment). Some provisions (citizenship, elections, provisional Parliament) took effect on 26 Nov 1949.

**Also remember:** the Assembly took **2 years, 11 months, 18 days**; it held eleven sessions. Ambedkar called the Constitution *"workable… flexible… strong enough to hold the country together both in peace time and in war time"* (speech of 25 Nov 1949).

## 2. General nature (features) of the Constitution

1. **Written and the lengthiest** among written constitutions. Detail is borrowed from the 1935 Act, and covers Union–State relations, administration, services, elections, and emergency.
2. **Partly rigid and partly flexible.** Art. 368 and elsewhere give three amendment methods:

| Method | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Simple majority** of Parliament (outside Art. 368) | Admission/formation of States (Arts. 2–4), citizenship, Sixth Schedule changes, abolition or creation of Legislative Councils (Art. 169) |
| **Special majority** (majority of the total membership **and** two-thirds of members present and voting in each House) | Most provisions, including Fundamental Rights and DPSPs |
| **Special majority + ratification by at least half the States** (Art. 368(2) proviso) | Federal provisions: election of the President (Arts. 54–55), extent of executive/legislative power (Arts. 73, 162, 241, 279A), Supreme Court and High Courts, Seventh Schedule lists, representation of States in Parliament, Art. 368 itself |

3. **Parliamentary democracy with a republican head** (see Part B below).
4. **Federal system with unitary features** (see Part A below).
5. **Fundamental Rights (Part III), enforceable**, together with **Directive Principles (Part IV)**, non-enforceable but "fundamental in the governance of the country" (Art. 37), and **Fundamental Duties (Part IVA)**.
6. **Independent and integrated judiciary** with judicial review.
7. **Secular State** (Preamble; Arts. 25–28; *S.R. Bommai*).
8. **Single citizenship**; **universal adult franchise** (Art. 326; 61st Amendment reduced voting age to 18).
9. **Emergency provisions** (Part XVIII; Arts. 352, 356, 360).
10. **Special provisions** for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, backward classes, minorities, women.
11. **Three-tier local government** (Parts IX, IXA: 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992).
12. **Basic structure** limiting Parliament's amending power (*Kesavananda Bharati*).

# PART A — FEDERAL STRUCTURE

## A.1 The word "federal" is not in the Constitution
Art. 1(1): *"India, that is Bharat, shall be a **Union of States**."* Ambedkar explained (Constituent Assembly, 4 Nov 1948) why the Drafting Committee chose "Union": (i) the Indian federation is **not the result of an agreement between States**; (ii) the States have **no right to secede**. He added that the Constitution was *"a Federation"* but the federation was *"a Union because it is indestructible"*.

## A.2 What is a "federation"? Wheare's test and Dicey's marks
**K.C. Wheare** (*Federal Government*, 1946): federal principle = a method of dividing powers so that the general and regional governments are **each, within a sphere, co-ordinate and independent**. **Dicey's essentials:** (1) supremacy of a **written constitution**, (2) **distribution of powers**, (3) an **independent judiciary** as interpreter.

Wheare called the Indian Constitution **"quasi-federal"** ("a unitary State with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal State with subsidiary unitary features"). **Ivor Jennings:** "a federation with a strong centralising tendency". **Granville Austin:** **"cooperative federalism"**. **Morris-Jones:** "bargaining federalism".

## A.3 Federal features (the Constitution as a federation)

| Feature | Provision |
|---|---|
| **Written, supreme Constitution** | Arts. 13, 245; laws contrary to it are void |
| **Dual polity**; two sets of governments | Union (Part V) and States (Part VI) |
| **Distribution of legislative powers** | Art. 246 and **Seventh Schedule**: Union List, State List, Concurrent List (the entries number roughly 100 / 60 / 50; counts differ between sources because of omissions and additions, so follow your textbook) |
| **Distribution of executive power** | Arts. 73 and 162 |
| **Distribution of financial resources** | Arts. 268–293; Finance Commission (Art. 280) |
| **Rigid amendment of federal provisions** | Art. 368(2) proviso (ratification by half the States) |
| **Independent judiciary** and **judicial review** of Union–State disputes | Arts. 131 (original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in disputes between governments), 32, 226 |
| **Bicameral Parliament with representation of States** | Council of States (Rajya Sabha), Art. 80 |
| **Inter-State relations machinery** | Arts. 262 (water disputes), 263 (Inter-State Council), 279A (GST Council, 101st Amendment, 2016), Arts. 301–307 (trade) |

## A.4 Unitary (centralising) features

| Feature | Provision |
|---|---|
| **Union's power to alter States' areas and names** by simple law | Art. 3 (*Berubari*; *Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1960 SC 51). "Indestructible Union of destructible States." |
| **Strong Centre in legislation** | Union List supremacy (Art. 246(1)); **residuary powers** with Union (Art. 248; Entry 97, List I); **repugnancy** rule: Union law prevails in the Concurrent List (Art. 254) |
| **Parliament may legislate on State subjects** in special circumstances | Art. 249 (Rajya Sabha's two-thirds resolution on national interest), Art. 250 (Emergency), Art. 252 (States' consent), Art. 253 (treaties), Art. 356 (President's Rule) |
| **Emergency provisions** convert the federal system into a unitary one | Arts. 352 (national), 356 (State), 360 (financial) |
| **Governor appointed by the President**, holds office at his pleasure | Arts. 153–156; Governor may reserve Bills for the President (Art. 200) |
| **Directions by the Union to States** | Arts. 256, 257 |
| **Integrated judiciary**; Supreme Court on top | Single hierarchy; appeals under Arts. 132–136 |
| **All-India Services** (IAS, IPS, IFS) | Art. 312 |
| **Single citizenship** and **single election machinery** | Part II; Art. 324 (Election Commission) |
| **Financial dependence of States** on the Centre | Grants-in-aid (Art. 275), Centrally Sponsored Schemes |
| **Comptroller and Auditor-General** | Art. 148, audit of both Union and State accounts |
| **Unequal representation in Rajya Sabha** (proportional to population) and **no equality of States** | Fourth Schedule |
| **Inter-State Council not obligatory** | Art. 263 ("may" establish) |

## A.5 Asymmetric federalism (special provisions for some States)
- Art. 371 to 371J give special provisions to **Maharashtra and Gujarat (371), Nagaland (371A), Assam (371B), Manipur (371C), Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (371D, 371E), Sikkim (371F), Mizoram (371G), Arunachal Pradesh (371H), Goa (371I), Karnataka (371J)**.
- **Art. 370** (Jammu and Kashmir) was abrogated in August 2019; the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation in ***In re Article 370 of the Constitution*** (judgment of **11 December 2023**, five-judge Constitution Bench headed by CJI D.Y. Chandrachud). Reported holdings include that Art. 370 was a **temporary** provision and that J&K, unlike the other States, retained no **internal sovereignty** after the Instrument of Accession; the Court also accepted that the Constitution allows **asymmetric federalism**. *(Obtain the SCC/INSC citation and read the operative paragraphs on statehood and the Union Territory of Ladakh before using this in an answer.)*

## A.6 Judicial opinion on the nature of Indian federalism

| Case | Citation | Holding |
|---|---|---|
| **State of West Bengal v. Union of India** | AIR 1963 SC 1241 | India is **not a federation in the strict sense**; the States are not sovereign; the Union is not a compact; Parliament may by law affect the States; ***quasi-federal*** (Sinha C.J.) |
| **State of Rajasthan v. Union of India** | (1977) 3 SCC 592 | Challenge to the Centre's dissolution of nine State Assemblies in 1977 failed; the Court accepted that Art. 356 satisfaction is reviewable only on narrow grounds such as *mala fides* (a position later widened in *Bommai*) |
| **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala** | (1973) 4 SCC 225 | **Federal character** forms part of the **basic structure** |
| **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India** | (1994) 3 SCC 1 | **Federalism is part of the basic structure**; "States are not mere appendages of the Centre"; Art. 356 is **justiciable**; see §A.7 |
| **Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India** | (2006) 7 SCC 1 | Removal of the domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha membership upheld; federalism is basic structure but is not a rigid, fixed pattern |
| **Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018)** | (2018) 8 SCC 501 | Constitution Bench: **cooperative / collaborative federalism**; the Lieutenant-Governor is bound by the aid and advice of the Delhi Council of Ministers except in matters outside its competence |
| **Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd.** | (2022) 10 SCC 700 | **GST Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding**; Union and States have equal, simultaneous legislative power over GST; federalism as "cooperative" (*verify holdings*) |
| **Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2023)** | (2023) 9 SCC 1 | Constitution Bench: elected Delhi government controls "services" in the fields within its legislative competence; responsible government and triple chain of accountability |
| **State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu** | Judgment of 8 April 2025 (Pardiwala J. and Mahadevan J.); *obtain citation* | Governor cannot hold Bills indefinitely or exercise a "pocket veto"; time-limits on Governor/President; Court used Art. 142 to treat ten re-passed Bills as assented to |
| **In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India** (Presidential Reference under Art. 143) | Opinion of **20 Nov 2025**, five-judge bench headed by CJI B.R. Gavai | The Court **cannot fix timelines** for Governor/President; **no "deemed assent"**; but Governors may not sit on Bills indefinitely. *(Read the opinion's text on Art. 200 options and limited judicial review before relying on details.)* |

## A.7 Article 356 and the *Bommai* guidelines (a favourite)

- **Art. 356:** if the President is satisfied that the government of a State **cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution**, he may assume the State's executive functions; Parliament must approve within **two months** (Art. 356(3)); ordinarily lasts six months, extendable up to three years (Art. 356(4)–(5); 44th Amendment limits).
- **Misuse history:** the Centre invoked it more than 100 times, particularly before 1994 (the dismissal of the Kerala government in 1959; wholesale dismissal of nine State governments in 1977 and again in 1980).
- ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*** (1994) 3 SCC 1 (nine-judge bench). Principal holdings:
  1. The President's satisfaction is **subject to judicial review**; the Court may examine whether there was relevant material and whether the action was **mala fide** or based on irrelevant grounds.
  2. The proclamation is **not immune**; if invalid, the Court may **restore the dismissed government and revive the Assembly**.
  3. The State Assembly **should not be dissolved** until Parliament approves the proclamation; it is kept under **suspended animation**.
  4. **Floor test** on the floor of the House is the **proper place** to test a government's majority, not the Governor's subjective opinion.
  5. **Secularism is a basic feature**; a State government acting against secularism may be dismissed.
  6. **Federalism is part of the basic structure.**
- ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India*** (2006) 2 SCC 1: dissolution of the Bihar Assembly (2005) held unconstitutional; Governor's report cannot be based on "political" apprehension of horse-trading.
- ***Nabam Rebia and Bamang Felix v. Deputy Speaker*** (2016) 8 SCC 1: the Governor's discretion in summoning/advancing the Assembly session is limited (Art. 163); Arunachal Pradesh order set aside.
- ***Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra*** (2023, Constitution Bench; *obtain citation*): the Governor's decision to call for a floor test was not supported by objective material; the Court could not restore the earlier government since the Chief Minister had resigned without facing a floor test.

## A.8 Recommendations of Commissions on Union–State relations

| Commission | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Rajamannar Committee (Tamil Nadu, 1969)** | Greater State autonomy; abolish Art. 356; make Inter-State Council effective |
| **Sarkaria Commission (1983–88)** | Retain a strong Centre; use Art. 356 as a **last resort**; Governor should be an eminent person from outside the State, not active in politics; permanent Inter-State Council; All-India Services |
| **NCRWC (Venkatachaliah, 2002)** | Governors' tenure of five years; Art. 356 only after warning |
| **Punchhi Commission (2007–10)** | "Localising emergency provisions" (Arts. 355, 356); Governors' removal only by impeachment-like process; time limits on Governors' sanction for prosecuting ministers |

*(Inter-State Council set up in 1990 under Art. 263; GST Council under Art. 279A is the first constitutional body for sharing taxation decisions.)*

# PART B — FORM OF GOVERNMENT

## B.1 Parliamentary, democratic republic

**Preamble:** India is a **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic.** The Constitution adopts the **Westminster-type parliamentary system** both at the Union and State levels.

| Element | Provision | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal head: President** | Arts. 52, 53 | Executive power vested in the President, exercised directly or through subordinates |
| **Real executive: Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister** | Arts. 74, 75 | Art. 74(1): the President **shall act in accordance with the aid and advice** of the Council (42nd Amendment); 44th Amendment proviso lets him **ask for reconsideration once**, then bound |
| **Collective responsibility** | Art. 75(3) | Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the **Lok Sabha** |
| **Ministers from the legislature** | Art. 75(5) | A non-member must become a member within six months |
| **Bicameral legislature** | Arts. 79, 80, 81 | Lok Sabha (directly elected), Rajya Sabha (indirectly elected, permanent, one-third retiring every two years) |
| **Government can be dissolved/lose confidence** | Arts. 85, 75(3) | President may dissolve the Lok Sabha on the advice of the Prime Minister (who commands majority) |
| **Republic (elected head)** | Arts. 54, 55 | President elected by an electoral college, **not hereditary** |
| **States**: Governor (nominal) and Council of Ministers headed by Chief Minister (real) | Arts. 153–167 | Art. 163: Governor acts on aid and advice, except where the Constitution requires discretion |
| **Money Bills** | Art. 110 | Only in Lok Sabha; Rajya Sabha can only recommend |
| **Rule of law and fundamental rights** limiting the executive | Part III | |

**Union Parliament** = President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha (Art. 79).

## B.2 Judicial interpretation of the President's position

| Case | Citation | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| **Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab** | AIR 1955 SC 549 | Indian President is a **constitutional head** like the English Crown; the real executive power lies with the Council of Ministers |
| **U.N. Rao v. Indira Gandhi** | (1971) 2 SCC 63 | Council of Ministers continues till the new Lok Sabha meets after dissolution; Art. 74 advice |
| **Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab** | (1974) 2 SCC 831 | President and Governors act **on the advice of Ministers** except in situations where they have discretion expressly or by necessary implication (e.g. appointment of PM where no majority; dismissal where Ministry loses majority) |
| **Union of India v. Jyoti Prakash Mitter** | AIR 1971 SC 1093 | President's decision on a High Court judge's age is final but not entirely beyond review |
| **Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu** | 1992 Supp (2) SCC 651 | Tenth Schedule (anti-defection) valid; Speaker's decisions reviewable (finality clause, para 7, struck down) |

**Conventions:** the President appoints the leader of the majority as Prime Minister (Art. 75(1)); in a hung Parliament he exercises a measure of **discretion**; the Prime Minister is *"primus inter pares"*; ministers hold office during the President's pleasure in theory but in fact during the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

## B.3 Parliamentary v. Presidential system

| Basis | India (parliamentary) | USA (presidential) |
|---|---|---|
| Head of State/government | President is head of State; PM is head of government | President is both |
| Executive and legislature | **Fused**: ministers are drawn from the legislature | **Separated**: Cabinet are not members of Congress |
| Responsibility | Collective to Lok Sabha | President not responsible to Congress (except by impeachment) |
| Tenure | Council of Ministers can fall by no-confidence | Fixed four-year term |
| Dissolution | Lok Sabha can be dissolved | Congress cannot be dissolved |
| Separation of powers | Flexible (functional separation) | Rigid with checks and balances |

**Separation of powers in India.** Not strict. *Ram Jawaya Kapur* (1955): "the Indian Constitution has not indeed recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity"; but the **functions of the three organs are sufficiently differentiated**. *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) Supp SCC 1 and *Kesavananda* treat separation of powers as part of the basic structure, and ***I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu*** (2007) 2 SCC 1 repeats the point.

## 3. Is the Indian Constitution federal or unitary? (conclusion for answers)

Combine the lists. **Suggested thesis:** *"The Indian Constitution is federal in structure but unitary in spirit: a federal system with a strong Centre that functions as unitary during an Emergency. The Supreme Court in Bommai has declared federalism a basic feature, and recent decisions on Delhi's services (2023), GST (2022) and Governors' assent (2025) show that the Court treats federalism as enforceable limits on the Centre, though not in a rigid, American sense."*

## 4. Criticism (for balance)

1. **Centralising bias:** Art. 3, Art. 249, Art. 356, Art. 200, residuary powers, financial dependence.
2. **Governor's office** as agent of the Centre (Sarkaria, Punchhi).
3. **Concurrent List expansion** (e.g. education moved to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, from the State List).
4. **Slow Inter-State Council** and under-use of Art. 263.
5. **Fiscal federalism:** GST has reduced the States' independent taxing power; compensation and cess disputes.
6. **Asymmetry** is unequal and has produced demands for special status.

**Defence:** Ambedkar and the Assembly designed a strong Centre because of **Partition**, princely-state integration, linguistic and communal tensions. The Constitution's *flexibility* allows federal practice to adjust (language-based reorganisation of States in 1956; creation of Telangana in 2014, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh in 2000).

## 5. Current relevance and recent developments (as on 28 Oct 2026)

- **Governor–State conflicts:** the **8 April 2025** judgment in *State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor* sought to end the "pocket veto"; the **Presidential Reference opinion of 20 Nov 2025** restored the position that **courts cannot prescribe timelines** or deem assent, while saying Governors cannot delay indefinitely. In an exam, **state both** and say the later Constitution Bench opinion governs the point of timelines; remind the examiner that the two decisions are not identical in outcome.
- **Delhi:** after the 2023 Constitution Bench ruling on services, Parliament enacted the Government of National Capital Territory of Delhi (Amendment) Act, 2023; a challenge was reported pending. *(Verify current status.)*
- **GST:** the post-*Mohit Minerals* position is that the GST Council operates through consensus and its recommendations are not binding.
- **Article 370:** the December 2023 decision settled the 2019 abrogation; demand for restoration of **statehood** for Jammu and Kashmir remains a political issue. *(Verify the Court's directions on statehood and elections before writing about it.)*
- **Women's reservation (106th Amendment).** The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, 2023 was passed on 20–21 Sept 2023 (assent 28 Sept 2023). Secondary sources report that the Centre notified it **into force in April 2026**, with seat reservation to follow only after the next **Census and delimitation**. *(One source gives 16 April 2026; confirm from the Gazette. It is not automatic for the 2029 election as per one report.)*
