## 1. Text and background

> **Art. 32.** *(1) The right to move the Supreme Court by **appropriate proceedings** for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is **guaranteed**.*
> *(2) The Supreme Court shall have power to issue **directions or orders or writs**, including writs in the nature of **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari**, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by this Part.*
> *(3) Without prejudice to the powers conferred on the Supreme Court by clauses (1) and (2), **Parliament may by law empower any other court** to exercise within the local limits of its jurisdiction all or any of the powers exercisable by the Supreme Court under clause (2).*
> *(4) The right guaranteed by this article shall **not be suspended** except as otherwise provided for by this Constitution.*

- **Ambedkar** in the Constituent Assembly (9 December 1948): *"If I was asked to name any particular article in this Constitution as the most important, an article without which this Constitution would be a nullity, I could not refer to any other article except this one. It is the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it."* *(Quote from memory of CAD Vol. VII; verify wording before using it in an exam.)*
- **Why it matters:** a right without a remedy is empty (*ubi jus ibi remedium*). Art. 32 is **itself a Fundamental Right** (it is in Part III), so the Court cannot refuse relief merely because another remedy exists (contrast Art. 226).
- **Suspension:** only under Art. 359 (Emergency) and only for the rights named in the Presidential Order; Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended (Unit 3).
- **Supreme Court as "protector and guarantor":** *Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 124.
- **Related:** Art. 139 (Parliament may confer on the Supreme Court power to issue writs for purposes **other than** Fundamental Rights); Art. 35(a): Parliament alone may legislate on matters in Art. 32(3).

### 1.1 Key features
1. **Guaranteed, not discretionary:** the Court is bound to entertain a petition for a Fundamental Right violation (*Prem Chand Garg v. Excise Commissioner, U.P.*, AIR 1963 SC 996: the Supreme Court Rules requiring a **security deposit** for a petition were struck down as an impediment to Art. 32).
2. **Only for Part III rights** (not ordinary legal rights): Art. 226 covers both.
3. **"Appropriate proceedings":** not limited to formal writs: letters and postcards have been treated as petitions (*epistolary jurisdiction*).
4. **Wide remedial power:** "directions, orders or writs"; the Court can **mould relief**, award **compensation**, issue **guidelines** and monitor implementation (**continuing mandamus**).
5. **Respondents:** the **State** (Art. 12). Private persons only where the right is enforceable against them (Arts. 15(2), 17, 23, 24) or, after *Kaushal Kishor* (2023), where the State fails its positive duty (Unit 3).
6. **Locus standi:** relaxed in PIL (below).

## 2. The five writs

*Latin/Anglo roots: prerogative writs of English law, adapted to the Indian Constitution as "writs in the nature of" (so the Court is not bound by technicalities of English procedure).*

| Writ | Literal meaning | Purpose | Issued against | Key conditions / grounds | Leading cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas corpus** | "You may have the body" | Secure the **release of a person unlawfully detained**; test the **legality of detention** | State or private person | Detention without authority of law or in violation of procedure; can be filed by the detenu or **any person on his behalf**; the Court looks at the legality of detention at the time of the hearing; **production of the body not essential** | *Kanu Sanyal v. District Magistrate, Darjeeling* (1973) 2 SCC 674; *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494; *Sebastian M. Hongray v. Union of India* (1984) 1 SCC 339 (disappearance; exemplary costs); *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 (compensation) |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Command a **public authority to perform a public/statutory duty** | Public officials, authorities, Government, even a private body performing a public duty | (i) A **legal right** of the petitioner; (ii) a **legal public duty** on the respondent; (iii) failure/refusal; (iv) not to enforce a discretionary or purely contractual obligation; **not** against the President or Governor personally (Art. 361); **not** to compel a legislature to enact a law | *Lekhraj Sathramdas Lalvani v. N.M. Shah* AIR 1966 SC 334; *Praga Tools Corporation v. C.A. Imanual* (1969) 1 SCC 585 (private company with a public duty); *Supreme Court Employees' Welfare Association v. Union of India* (1989) 4 SCC 187 (no mandamus to legislate) *(verify)* |
| **Prohibition** | "To forbid" | **Forbid an inferior court/tribunal** from exceeding jurisdiction or acting contrary to natural justice, **while proceedings are pending** | Courts, tribunals, quasi-judicial authorities | Lack/excess of jurisdiction; violation of natural justice; **issued before the decision** | *East India Commercial Co. v. Collector of Customs* AIR 1962 SC 1893 |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | **Quash** an order already made by an inferior court/tribunal/quasi-judicial body | Courts, tribunals, quasi-judicial authorities (and administrative bodies affecting rights after *Ridge v. Baldwin* reasoning) | (i) **Lack or excess of jurisdiction**; (ii) **error of law apparent on the face of the record**; (iii) **violation of natural justice**; (iv) **fraud/bias**; not for mere errors of fact or for review on merits | *T.C. Basappa v. T. Nagappa* AIR 1954 SC 440; *Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Ahmad Ishaque* AIR 1955 SC 233; *Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan* AIR 1964 SC 477 (limits: no re-appreciation of evidence); *Ujjam Bai v. State of U.P.* AIR 1962 SC 1621 |
| **Quo warranto** | "By what authority" | **Challenge a person's right to hold a public office** | Holder of a public office | The office is **public**, created by **statute or the Constitution**, **substantive** (not a mere employment); the holder **lacks the qualifications** or was appointed in violation of law; **any person** may apply (no personal injury required) | *University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao* AIR 1965 SC 491; *Kumar Padma Prasad v. Union of India* (1992) 2 SCC 428 (unqualified appointment as High Court Chief Justice quashed) |

**Prohibition v. certiorari (a classic short note):** prohibition is **preventive** (during proceedings); certiorari is **curative** (after the decision). Both rest on jurisdictional error and natural justice.

### 2.1 Exceptions and limits to the writs
- **Res judicata and Art. 32:** if a High Court has dismissed an Art. 226 petition **on merits** after a speaking order, a petition on the same facts under Art. 32 is barred (*Daryao v. State of U.P.* AIR 1961 SC 1457); a dismissal **in limine** or on delay does not bar. For **habeas corpus**, a fresh petition is permitted (*Lallubhai Jogibhai Patel v. Union of India*, (1981) 2 SCC 427; *verify*).
- **Laches:** unexplained delay can defeat a writ (discretionary under Art. 226; a stronger ground to refuse under Art. 32 on grounds other than FR).
- **Alternative remedy:** under Art. 226 a rule of self-restraint; under Art. 32 not a bar for Fundamental Rights breach but the Court prefers High Courts for fact-heavy disputes.
- **No writ** against the President or Governor personally for exercise of their powers (Art. 361), but against the Government for acts done in their name.
- **Private bodies:** only where they perform **public functions** or are "State" under Art. 12 (*Binny Ltd. v. V. Sadasivan*, (2005) 6 SCC 657; *St. Mary's Education Society v. Rajendra Prasad Bhargava*, (2023) 4 SCC 498: a private unaided school's service dispute is not a public-law matter) *(verify)*.

## 3. Compensation and "continuing mandamus" under Art. 32

- **Public-law compensation:** *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 (prisoner detained for about 14 years after acquittal; the Court awarded further compensation of Rs. 30,000 over the Rs. 5,000 already paid; verify the figures); *Bhim Singh v. State of J&K* (1985) 4 SCC 677; *Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa* (1993) 2 SCC 746 (custodial death; compensation is a remedy in public law and is **not barred** by sovereign immunity). *Compensation under Art. 32 is without prejudice to a civil suit and is **not** a bar to prosecution.*
- **Continuing mandamus:** the Court retains the case and monitors compliance: *Vineet Narain v. Union of India* (1998) 1 SCC 226 (Hawala case: guidelines for CBI independence, Central Vigilance Commission); *M.C. Mehta* environmental orders; *Vishaka* (1997) (guidelines in absence of legislation).
- **Guidelines in the absence of law:** *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241; *D.K. Basu v. State of W.B.* (1997) 1 SCC 416 (arrest guidelines).

## 4. Article 32 and Article 226 compared

| Basis | Art. 32 | Art. 226 |
|---|---|---|
| Court | **Supreme Court** | **High Courts** |
| Nature | **Itself a Fundamental Right** (Part III) | Constitutional power; **not** a Fundamental Right |
| Purpose | **Only for Fundamental Rights** | Fundamental Rights **and "any other purpose"** (legal rights) |
| Duty to entertain | **Must** entertain; cannot refuse merely because an alternative remedy exists | **Discretionary**; alternative remedy and laches are ordinarily grounds to refuse |
| Territorial jurisdiction | Whole of India | Within the territory over which the High Court has jurisdiction (and where the cause of action arises: Art. 226(2)) |
| Writs | Directions, orders **or writs** | Directions, orders **or writs** (same five) |
| Against whom | State/authorities within Art. 12 | Any **person or authority**, including the Government (wider: "any person") |
| Suspension | Not suspended except by the Constitution (Art. 32(4)) | Not covered by Art. 32(4); but judicial review under Arts. 226/227 is basic structure and cannot be excluded (*L. Chandra Kumar*) |
| Interim relief | Yes | Yes (stay, injunctions) |

- ***L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India*** (1997) 3 SCC 261 (seven judges): the power of judicial review of the Supreme Court (Art. 32) and the High Courts (Arts. 226/227) is part of the **basic structure**; tribunals' decisions are subject to High Court review.
- **Modern trend:** the Supreme Court sometimes asks petitioners to approach the High Court first (especially for fact-heavy matters), but retains Art. 32 for serious Fundamental Rights issues (e.g., *Arnab Goswami v. State of Maharashtra*, (2020) 14 SCC 12: personal liberty; High Courts should not decline interim relief) *(verify)*.

## 5. Public interest litigation (PIL)

### 5.1 Meaning and origin
- **PIL** (also "social action litigation") allows a **public-spirited person or group** to approach the court for **enforcement of the rights of persons who cannot do so themselves** (poor, illiterate, disadvantaged) or for **diffuse public rights** (environment, consumers, governance).
- **Traditional rule of *locus standi*:** only the person whose right is violated could sue. In PIL, the Court **relaxed** it: *any member of the public acting bona fide* may petition.
- **Roots:** Justices **V.R. Krishna Iyer** and **P.N. Bhagwati** in the late 1970s–80s, in reaction to the Emergency's damage to the Court's image; influenced by the US "public law litigation" model.

### 5.2 Landmark cases

| Case | Citation | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar** | (1980) 1 SCC 81 | First major PIL: **undertrial prisoners** held beyond maximum sentences; speedy trial and free legal aid |
| **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India** (Judges' Transfer case) | 1981 Supp SCC 87 | Liberalised *locus standi*: any member of the public may seek redress for a legal injury to persons who cannot approach the court |
| **People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India** | (1982) 3 SCC 235 | Asiad workers: Art. 23 and minimum wages |
| **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India** | (1984) 3 SCC 161 | Bonded labour: letter treated as a writ; Court-appointed Commissioners |
| **Fertilizer Corporation Kamgar Union v. Union of India** | (1981) 1 SCC 568 | Locus standi relaxed but a **PIL cannot be used to challenge policy without legal injury** *(verify)* |
| **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India** (series) | (1987) 1 SCC 395 and others | Environmental PILs: absolute liability, Ganga pollution, Taj Mahal |
| **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan** | (1997) 6 SCC 241 | Guidelines on sexual harassment |
| **Vineet Narain v. Union of India** | (1998) 1 SCC 226 | Continuing mandamus on corruption investigation |

### 5.3 Features of PIL
1. **Relaxed *locus standi*.**
2. **Epistolary jurisdiction:** letters, newspaper reports treated as petitions (*suo motu* cognisance).
3. **Non-adversarial procedure:** the Court as a facilitator; **Commissioners** and *amicus curiae* appointed to gather facts.
4. **Innovative remedies:** guidelines, continuing mandamus, compensation, monitoring committees.
5. **Cost and delay** reduced for the poor.

### 5.4 Abuse and guidelines
- Criticism: PIL has been used for **publicity, personal vendetta, political purposes** ("publicity interest litigation"), clogging courts, and judicial overreach into policy.
- ***State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal*** (2010) 3 SCC 402: detailed **guidelines** for PIL: courts should verify the credentials of the petitioner, prima facie correctness of the information, ensure that the petition is for **public good** and not for private gain, and impose **costs** on frivolous petitions *(verify)*.
- ***Ashok Kumar Pandey v. State of W.B.*** (2004) 3 SCC 349: PIL is a weapon to be used with care; courts must prevent its abuse *(verify)*.
- **Judicial overreach debate:** executive and legislative functions (policy, budgets) v. rights protection; the Court's reply: it acts only where the executive fails its duty.

## 7. Criticism and balance

1. **Overuse of Art. 32:** the Supreme Court is often approached directly for matters that High Courts can decide; the Court sometimes redirects.
2. **PIL excesses:** policy intrusion; weak follow-up; a conflict with the separation of powers.
3. **Access:** delay and cost mean that for ordinary citizens, Art. 32 remains difficult to use; it is more accessible via PIL by organisations.
4. **Tribunalisation:** *L. Chandra Kumar* preserved High Court review.
5. **Strengths:** a fast, constitutionally guaranteed remedy; flexible reliefs (compensation, guidelines); the foundation of rights jurisprudence (*Maneka Gandhi*, *Vishaka*, *Puttaswamy*).

---

## 8. Current relevance and recent developments

- **Technology and Art. 32:** e-filing and virtual hearings have widened access; open-court live-streaming of Constitution Bench hearings began in 2022.
- **Direct petitions on liberty and speech:** the Court has repeatedly entertained Art. 32 petitions for FIR quashing and protection from arrest in speech-related and journalist cases while cautioning that Art. 32 is not a substitute for ordinary remedies *(no specific case cited; verify before writing)*.
- **PIL on environment and climate:** *M.K. Ranjitsinh* (2024) (Unit 8).
- **Caution for the exam:** do not say Art. 32 is available for every legal right. It is confined to Fundamental Rights.

## 9. Quick-revision box

- **Art. 32:** (1) right to move the SC guaranteed; (2) directions, orders or writs; (3) Parliament may empower other courts; (4) not suspended except by the Constitution. **"Heart and soul"** (Ambedkar).
- **Five writs:** habeas corpus (body), mandamus (command), prohibition (forbid, pending), certiorari (quash, after), quo warranto (authority).
- **Art. 32 is a Fundamental Right; Art. 226 is not.** Art. 226 is wider in purpose ("any other purpose") and in persons; Art. 32 is confined to FRs.
- **Res judicata:** *Daryao* (1961). **Prem Chand Garg** (1963): no security deposit.
- **Compensation:** *Rudul Sah* (1983); *Nilabati Behera* (1993). **Continuing mandamus:** *Vineet Narain* (1998).
- **PIL:** *Hussainara* (1980); *S.P. Gupta* (1981); guidelines in *Balwant Singh Chaufal* (2010).
- **Basic structure:** judicial review under Arts. 32/226/227 (*L. Chandra Kumar*, 1997).

**Examiner keywords:** guaranteed remedy, protector and guarantor, locus standi, epistolary jurisdiction, continuing mandamus, public law compensation, res judicata, alternative remedy, public duty, jurisdictional error.
