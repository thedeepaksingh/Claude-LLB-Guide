## C-13 · Savigny's historical school: the *Volksgeist* theory of law, with criticism

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Historical School: Savigny"). Related: Dec 2024 Q6 (B-25, *Volksgeist*), Dec 2022 Q6 (B-26, "Law grows with the growth of people…"), Dec 2025 Q4 (A-59, books of Savigny).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** background and reaction against natural law 4 · Volksgeist and main propositions 7 · Thibaut controversy 3 · criticism 4 · contribution and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The historical school arose in Germany in the early nineteenth century as a reaction against the eighteenth-century belief that universal reason could produce a perfect code of law for all nations. Its leader was **Friedrich Carl von Savigny** (1779–1861), a professor at Berlin and later a Prussian minister for legislative revision. His main works are *Das Recht des Besitzes* (*The Law of Possession*, 1803), *Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft* (*Of the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence*, 1814), *Geschichte des römischen Rechts im Mittelalter* (1815–31) and *System des heutigen römischen Rechts* (1840–49).

**1. Background**
1. **Reaction against natural law and the French Revolution.** The natural-law thinkers had said that reason could discover the perfect law. The Revolution and the Napoleonic Code (1804) tried to give France such a law and to impose it on Europe, which stirred German national feeling.
2. **Romanticism and nationalism.** There was a new interest in language, folklore and national character.
3. **Precursors.** *Montesquieu* (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) said laws must suit the climate, religion, customs and character of a people. *Edmund Burke* (1790) saw society as a partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn and favoured gradual change. *Gustav Hugo* (1764–1844) held that law grows historically and is not made by the will of a legislator.
4. **The Thibaut–Savigny controversy (1814).** A.F.J. Thibaut urged Germany to adopt one civil code on rational lines to unite the country. Savigny replied in *Vom Beruf* that Germany was not ready: the historical study of law was too undeveloped, and a hasty code would be defective and would arrest natural growth. The pamphlet is the manifesto of the historical school.

**2. The central theory: *Volksgeist***
1. **Law is the product of the *Volksgeist*** (the spirit or common consciousness of the people). Like language, law is not invented by anyone. It grows organically and unconsciously from the life, customs and convictions of a people, and its form is found in their common consciousness. In the words usually quoted from Savigny, law "grows with the growth and strengthens with the strength of the people, and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality".
2. **Law is national, not universal.** Each people has its own law, as it has its own language. A code made for all mankind is a mistake.
3. **Law is found, not made.** The legislator does not create law; he declares what already lives in the people's consciousness.
4. **Stages of growth.** (i) At first law lives in custom and popular belief. (ii) As society becomes complex, jurists become the representatives of the people's consciousness, and law acquires a technical, juristic form. (iii) Legislation comes last, as a refinement of law already formed.
5. **The jurist's role.** The jurist is the technical spokesman of the *Volksgeist*. The study of law must therefore be historical: he must trace the development of institutions, especially Roman and Germanic law.
6. **Opposition to codification.** Savigny did not oppose codes for ever, but held that a code could be framed only after the historical study of law had been completed.
7. **Law and language analogy.** Both arise from the common life of a people; both develop gradually and unconsciously; both are peculiar to each nation; neither can be created by decree.

**3. Later development.** Savigny's followers, *Puchta* (1798–1846) and *Windscheid* (1817–92), the "Pandectists", developed a systematic, conceptual science of Roman law, and their work supplied the basis of the German Civil Code (*Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch*), which came into force on 1 January 1900. It was the very code Savigny had said Germany was not ready for.

**4. Savigny and other schools**
- **Against natural law:** law is not found by universal reason; it is particular to each people.
- **Against Austin:** law is not the command of a sovereign. Maine used the same point against Austin; he noted that Ranjit Singh, in the Punjab, made hardly any laws and the people lived by custom.
- **Towards sociological jurisprudence:** the insistence that law grows out of social life leads to Ehrlich's "living law".

**5. Criticism**
1. **The *Volksgeist* is vague and mystical.** Whose spirit? A modern society has many groups with conflicting interests and practices. The theory ignores class conflict.
2. **Undervalues legislation.** In modern life law is made deliberately to reform society: labour law, consumer law, tax law, environmental law. It does not always wait for slow custom.
3. **Glorifies custom.** Many customs are unjust (sati, untouchability, child marriage) and must be changed by law, not preserved.
4. **Historically inconsistent.** Savigny championed Roman law, but Germany had *received* it from abroad. Borrowing of law is common (the Indian Contract Act 1872 drew on English law), which contradicts the idea that each people's law is unique.
5. **Conservative.** It gives no standard for reform, and could be used to defend privilege.
6. **Codes have worked.** The German Civil Code (1900), the Swiss Civil Code (1907) and, in India, the Indian Penal Code (1860) and the Contract Act (1872) show that a good code can serve a society.
7. **Neglects the judge** and the influence of ideas from other societies.
8. **Ignores the use of law as an instrument** of conscious social change (Pound; see C-16).

**6. Merits**
1. It introduced the historical method into law and revived respect for custom.
2. It showed that law is rooted in social life and must be understood in its setting.
3. It paved the way for the sociological school and for comparative law.
4. It gave a lasting warning against copying foreign laws or making laws without knowing the people.

**7. Indian illustration**
- **Custom and personal law.** The Hindu, Muslim and tribal laws in India show law growing out of community traditions. The Constitution itself, in Art. 13(3)(a), counts "custom or usage having the force of law" as law, and Arts. 371A (Nagaland) and 371G (Mizoram) protect customary law in those States. Courts require a custom to be ancient, certain, continuous, reasonable and not opposed to statute or public policy (*Hurpurshad v. Sheo Dayal*, 1876; *Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy*, 1868, where the Privy Council said that clear proof of usage outweighs the written text of the law).
- **The limits of the theory.** Where a custom conflicts with fundamental rights, it must give way: *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) set aside instant triple talaq; *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) preferred constitutional morality to social morality; the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987 and Art. 17 override harmful custom. These examples answer Savigny: law may need to lead society, not follow it.
- **The codification debate.** The discussion on a Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44) recalls the Thibaut–Savigny dispute: supporters stress equality and unity; opponents stress the diversity of community traditions and the need for gradual, consensual change. Uttarakhand enacted a State Uniform Civil Code in 2024.

**Conclusion.** Savigny's *Volksgeist* theory made law a part of the life of a people and taught the importance of history and custom. Its weakness is that it makes law the passive product of the national spirit and cannot explain reform or borrowing. Modern law uses both views: it respects social tradition and also uses legislation to change it.

## C-14 · "The movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from status to contract." Comment, and show how far it applies in India

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q10 ("The movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from status to contract. Comment on this statement and show how far it is applicable in India?"); Dec 2022 Q13 ("'Movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from status to contract.' Comment.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Maine, his method and the statement 4 · meaning of status and contract with examples 5 · evidence for the thesis 3 · criticism and reverse movement 4 · India 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** The statement comes from **Sir Henry Maine's** *Ancient Law* (1861), chapter 5: "the movement of the progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract". Maine (1822–88) was Regius Professor of Civil Law at Cambridge, Law Member of the Governor-General's Council in India (1862–69), and later Corpus Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford. He used the historical-comparative method, studying Roman, Hindu, Hebrew, Celtic and Germanic law to find laws of development common to societies. The statement is his best-known thesis on the growth of individual freedom in law.

**1. Meaning of "status" and "contract"**
- **Status** is a condition fixed by birth or by position in a group, which determines a person's rights, duties and capacities regardless of his will. In early society the unit of law is the **family**, not the individual. Examples: a Roman son under the *patria potestas* of the father; a slave; a woman under perpetual guardianship; a member of a caste or joint family in Hindu society.
- **Contract** is the creation of rights and duties by the **free agreement of individuals**. The unit of law is the **individual**. Examples: a person freely choosing his employment, buying and selling property, making a will, marrying by consent.
- Maine did not mean that contract replaced all legal relations. He meant that the family-based order, in which the individual's position was settled by birth, gave way to an order in which the individual could make his own legal position by agreement.

**2. Evidence given by Maine**
1. **The father's power** (*patria potestas*) in Roman law weakened over time: sons gradually acquired capacity to hold property (*peculium*), and ties of agnatic kinship gave way to blood relationship (cognation).
2. **Slavery** declined.
3. **Women**: perpetual guardianship of females by their agnatic relatives disappeared.
4. **Property** moved from family property to individual ownership.
5. **Contract and will** became general: individuals could dispose of property by will and bind themselves by agreement.
6. **Stages of legal growth** (*themistes*, custom, codes, and then change by fictions, equity and legislation) were the means by which the law was made to fit new freedom (see C-15).

**3. Criticism**
1. **A reverse movement from contract to status.** The modern welfare State protects groups: employees, tenants, consumers, women and children. The terms of the employment relationship, for instance, are now fixed largely by law (minimum wages, hours, safety, social security) and not by free bargaining. W. Friedmann and others argued that the modern movement is *from contract back to status* (a "new status" based on membership of a protected class).
2. **Freedom of contract is often illusory.** Standard-form contracts imposed by powerful parties leave the weaker party no real choice; courts and legislatures then intervene.
3. **Status never disappeared.** Marriage, citizenship, minority and legal capacity are still matters of status.
4. **Narrow evidence.** Maine relied mainly on Roman, Hindu and Hebrew law, and his scheme of "static" and "progressive" societies is now regarded as Eurocentric.
5. **Economic factors.** The change in the role of contract depends on trade and industry, which Maine did not stress.
6. **Not a straight line.** Societies may move back and forth.

**4. Application in India**

*(a) The movement from status to contract*
1. **Ancient and traditional position.** In classical Hindu society, rights and duties depended largely on *varna*, caste, sex and family: the *Dharmashastra* gave different duties, punishments and capacities to different classes. The joint Hindu family, with the *karta* and coparcenary by birth, is a status institution. Slavery and bonded labour existed in different forms.
2. **The Constitution.** It rejects status based on birth: Art. 14 (equality), Art. 15 (no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth), Art. 16, Art. 17 (abolition of untouchability), Art. 18 (abolition of titles), Art. 23 (prohibition of forced labour) and Art. 24 (no child labour in hazardous work).
3. **Statutes.** The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and Special Marriage Act, 1954 turned marriage into a legal relationship entered by consent; the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (amended 2005) gave daughters equal rights in coparcenary property, and *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma* (2020) held that daughters are coparceners by birth; the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 abolished bonded labour (enforced in *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India*, 1984); the Indian Contract Act, 1872 rests on free consent. *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) struck down the adultery offence, which treated a wife as her husband's property.
4. **Economic life.** The shift from a mostly agrarian economy to a market economy increased the area of contract: employment, trade, finance, company and consumer transactions.

*(b) The reverse movement in India*
1. **Protective law.** Reservation (Arts. 15(4) and 16(4)), the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, the labour laws (the four Labour Codes passed in 2019–20 were brought into force on 21 November 2025, replacing a number of earlier labour Acts), the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961, rent control laws, and the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 treat people as members of a class who need protection, so their rights depend on their class (employee, consumer, tenant, woman, Scheduled Caste or Tribe).
2. **Control of unfair contracts.** In *Central Inland Water Transport Corporation v. Brojo Nath Ganguly* (1986) the Supreme Court held an unconscionable termination clause in a standard-form employment contract void under Art. 14 and s. 23 of the Contract Act because of the unequal bargaining power of the parties. *LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995) applied similar reasoning to insurance.
3. **Personal law is still status-based.** Marriage, adoption, succession and guardianship are governed by personal laws attached to religion, which a person acquires by birth (Hindu, Muslim, Christian and Parsi laws). The debate on Art. 44 (Uniform Civil Code) concerns the reduction of this status element.
4. **Caste and group identity still matter.** Reservation, caste-based discrimination and community-based customs show that status has not disappeared.

**5. Assessment.** Maine's thesis is a good description of the growth of individual freedom and of the replacement of birth-based hierarchy by consent. In India the Constitution and legislation moved the law away from status-based inequality. But Indian law today combines both: it extends contract in the market, and it uses status-like protection for weaker groups. The statement is therefore true in part. It describes a major direction of change, but it is not a complete or permanent law of social development.

**Conclusion.** Maine saw the history of progressive societies as the rise of the free individual who makes his own legal relations. India's constitutional and statutory changes follow that direction by removing legal disabilities based on birth. However, the modern movement towards protective, class-based legislation shows that, as Friedmann said, welfare States are moving from contract back to status. Indian law is a mixture of the two.

## C-15 · Maine's theories: stages of legal growth, static and progressive societies, patriarchal theory, village communities; custom; Savigny and Maine compared

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Historical School: Sir Henry Sumner Maine"). Related: C-13, C-14; Dec 2025 Q4 (A-59, books of Maine), Dec 2022 Q6 and Dec 2024 Q6 (B-25, B-26).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** method, works and life 3 · stages of growth and the three instrumentalities 6 · patriarchal theory and village communities 3 · custom and Indian cases 3 · comparison with Savigny and criticism 5

### Answer

**Introduction.** Sir Henry James Sumner Maine (1822–88) is the leading English figure of the historical school. His book *Ancient Law* (1861), followed by *Village Communities in the East and West* (1871), *Early History of Institutions* (1875), *Dissertations on Early Law and Custom* (1883), *Popular Government* (1885) and *International Law* (1888), applied the **historical-comparative method**: he compared the development of Roman, Hindu, Hebrew, Celtic and Germanic law to find the common laws of legal growth. His years as Law Member of the Governor-General's Council in India (1862–69) gave him direct knowledge of Indian society.

**1. The stages of legal development**

1. **Themistes (divine judgments).** In the earliest period, the king gave judgments that were believed to be inspired by the gods. There was as yet no body of law, only separate decisions (in Homer, *themistes*).
2. **Customary law.** The judgments hardened into customs, which were kept in the memory of a privileged class (a priestly or aristocratic class) that administered them.
3. **Codes.** With writing, custom was recorded to put an end to the monopoly of the privileged class: the Twelve Tables of Rome, the Greek codes and the Code of Manu.
4. **Modern growth through three instrumentalities.** After the codes, a society that progresses must adapt its law to change. Maine named three means:
   - **Legal fictions**: an assumption, not open to challenge, that a fact is so when it is not, which changes the working of the law without changing its letter. Examples are the Roman and Hindu adoption (a stranger treated as a natural son) and the English fiction by which the claimant in ejectment was made to appear as *John Doe* against *Richard Roe*.
   - **Equity**: a body of principles claiming a higher justice that stands beside the civil law and overrides it where it is harsh. Examples are the Roman *praetor's edict* and the English Court of Chancery.
   - **Legislation**: conscious law-making by a recognised body, the last and most powerful means of change.

**2. Static and progressive societies.** Maine observed that most societies are "stationary": after the code stage their law stops growing (he cited the Hindu, Chinese and other Eastern societies). Only a few societies, chiefly of Western Europe, are "progressive" and continued to change their law by fictions, equity and legislation. This distinction is now considered Eurocentric, and it underrates change in Indian and other non-Western law.

**3. From status to contract.** The movement of progressive societies from status (position fixed by birth in the family) to contract (rights created by individual agreement) is the best-known thesis of Maine (see C-14).

**4. The patriarchal theory.** Maine held that the earliest social unit was the patriarchal family, under the eldest male ascendant who had absolute power over the members. Larger groups (the house, the clan, the tribe, the State) arose by extension of the family; kinship was through males (agnatic). Critics, notably J.F. McLennan (*Primitive Marriage*, 1865) and L.H. Morgan (*Ancient Society*, 1877), argued that early societies were matriarchal or without fixed marriage and that descent was traced through women. Later anthropology shows many forms of early society, so Maine's theory, based largely on Roman, Hindu and Hebrew evidence, is too narrow.

**5. Village communities.** In *Village Communities in the East and West* (1871) Maine studied the Indian village community, a body of related families holding land jointly and administering justice by custom, as a surviving picture of early institutions. This work became a foundation of legal anthropology. Later research showed that the Indian village was less static and less equal than he supposed.

**6. Maine on Austin.** Maine criticised Austin's command theory by pointing to early and Eastern societies, where custom, not command, governed. He noted that Ranjit Singh made hardly any laws, and yet his subjects lived under law (see C-08).

**7. Custom as a source of law (linked topic)**
- **Meaning.** A rule of conduct observed continuously for a long time by a community, which has acquired the force of law.
- **Essentials.** Antiquity; continuity; certainty; reasonableness; peaceable enjoyment (not by force, secrecy or permission); obligatory force; conformity with statute, public policy and morality; and proof by evidence.
- **Indian law.** Art. 13(3)(a) of the Constitution includes "custom or usage having the force of law" in "law", so custom is subject to Part III. Section 3(a) of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 defines "custom" and "usage" (a rule continuously and uniformly observed for a long time, certain, reasonable and not opposed to public policy; and, if applicable only to a family, not discontinued).
- **Cases.** *Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy* (1868): clear proof of usage outweighs the written text of the law. *Ramalakshmi Ammal v. Sivanatha Perumal Sethuraya Thevar* (1872): what is proved to be custom is to be taken as law. *Hurpurshad v. Sheo Dayal* (1876): antiquity, certainty and reasonableness must be proved by clear evidence.

**8. Savigny and Maine compared**

| Basis | Savigny | Maine |
|---|---|---|
| Tradition | German; Roman and Germanic law | English; comparative (Roman, Hindu, Celtic, Hebrew) |
| Main idea | *Volksgeist*: law grows from the people's consciousness | Law evolves through stages; status to contract |
| Source of law | Custom first, then jurists, then legislation | *Themistes*, custom, codes, then fictions, equity, legislation |
| Codification | Opposed as premature | A normal stage; Maine helped Indian law reform |
| Law and society | National and unique to each people | Common stages across societies |
| Method | Historical | Historical-comparative |
| Common ground | Both see law as organic and historical, and reject abstract natural law and Austin's command theory | |

**9. Criticism of Maine**
1. Evidence was narrow (mostly Roman, Hindu and Hebrew).
2. Stages are not universal; many societies did not pass through them in that order.
3. The patriarchal theory has been challenged by anthropology.
4. "Static" and "progressive" societies is a Eurocentric division.
5. Status to contract is not a straight line (the reverse movement in the welfare State).
6. He underplays economic factors and the conscious role of legislation.

**10. Merits.** He founded comparative legal history and influenced anthropology and the sociology of law; showed how fictions, equity and legislation reconcile law with change; and offered a memorable thesis on the growth of personal freedom.

**11. Indian illustration.** Adoption (a legal fiction in Hindu law), the Indian codes enacted in Maine's period, such as the Indian Succession Act, 1865, and the continuing role of custom in Hindu and tribal law, all show Maine's categories at work.

**Conclusion.** Maine added a comparative and evolutionary dimension to the historical school. Where Savigny looked to the national spirit, Maine looked for general stages of growth common to different societies. His thesis of status to contract, his three instrumentalities of legal change and his study of Indian village communities remain his lasting contributions, though many of his generalisations have been corrected.

## C-16 · Roscoe Pound's theory of social engineering: law as the balancing of competing interests

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q11 ("The aim of social engineering is to build an efficient structure of society as possible, which involves the balancing of competing interests. Discuss."); Dec 2024 Q10 ("Write a detailed essay on the 'Social Engineering Theory.'"); Dec 2023 Q9 ("What do you mean by Dean Roscoe Pound's theory of Social Engineering? Discuss."); Dec 2022 Q12 (same wording as Dec 2025 Q11). **Asked in all four sittings.**
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Pound and the idea of social engineering 4 · interests (classification chart) 6 · jural postulates 3 · other features and Indian application 4 · criticism and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** Roscoe Pound (1870–1964), Dean of Harvard Law School (1916–36), was the leading American exponent of sociological jurisprudence. His main works are *Law in Books and Law in Action* (1910), *The Scope and Purpose of Sociological Jurisprudence* (1911–12), *Interpretations of Legal History* (1923), *An Introduction to the Philosophy of Law* (1922), *Social Control Through Law* (1942) and the five-volume *Jurisprudence* (1959). His best-known idea is that law is a form of **social engineering**: the engineer's job is to design a structure so that it serves human wants with the least waste, and the lawyer's job is to build an efficient structure of society by securing as many of the competing claims of people as possible with the least friction and waste.

**1. The idea of social engineering**
1. **Law as a social institution.** Law is a means of social control, the most highly developed one, alongside morality, religion and education. Its task is to maintain and improve civilised society.
2. **Wants, claims and demands.** People living together have many wants, claims and demands. Because resources are limited, these conflict. The task of law is to reconcile them.
3. **The engineering function.** The jurist, legislator and judge are like engineers. They (i) study the social facts and conflicts, (ii) identify the interests involved, (iii) balance them, and (iv) shape rules and institutions so that the largest number of interests are secured with the least sacrifice of others. Pound described legal history as a continually wider recognition and satisfaction of human claims, with more effective elimination of waste and friction, "a continually more efficacious social engineering".
4. **Law in action.** The test of a rule is its effect in society, not its logical consistency. Pound attacked *mechanical jurisprudence*, in which courts applied rules logically without regard to results, and asked that the gap between *law in books* and *law in action* be reduced.

**2. Theory of interests**
An *interest* is a claim, demand or desire that people, individually or in groups, seek to satisfy and which therefore must be considered in arranging human relations. In every legal system, the law must (i) **recognise** the interests, (ii) **define** the limits within which they will be given legal effect, (iii) **secure** them within those limits, and (iv) **weigh** them, by the jural postulates of the civilisation, when they conflict.

*Pound's classification of interests*

| Class | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual interests** | Claims of the individual as such | *Personality* (body, reputation, privacy, freedom of will, belief); *domestic relations* (marriage, family); *interests of substance* (property, contract, association, employment) |
| **Public interests** | Claims of the State as a legal person, or as guardian of social interests | The State's integrity, honour, property and revenue; the State as guardian of social interests |
| **Social interests** | Claims of society as a whole | (1) *General security* (peace, health, security of acquisitions and transactions); (2) *security of social institutions* (family, religion, politics, economy); (3) *general morals*; (4) *conservation of social resources*; (5) *general progress* (economic, political, cultural); (6) *individual life* (self-assertion, opportunity, conditions of life) |

Pound stressed the **social interests**, because in his view all claims, whether made by an individual or by the State, are in the end claims of society to be secured. The conflict between interests is to be solved by looking at them *on the same plane*: that is, one should compare individual interests with social interests as claims of society, rather than set one class against another.

**3. Jural postulates.** Pound said that in each civilisation there are assumptions about what people may expect of each other, which law should secure. He stated them for the society of his time:
1. People must be able to assume that others will not commit *intentional aggression* upon them.
2. They must be able to *control for beneficial purposes what they have discovered, created or acquired*.
3. They must be able to assume that those with whom they deal will act in *good faith*.
4. They must be able to assume that those who act will use *due care* not to injure them.
5. They must be able to assume that those who keep things *likely to escape and do damage* will keep them within bounds (compare *Rylands v. Fletcher*).
The postulates are relative to time and place, and Pound later added further ones for modern industrial society (for example, that the burden of risk of enterprise should be spread on those who benefit from it). They give the judge and legislator a standard for weighing interests.

**4. Stages of legal development.** Pound described five stages: (i) *primitive law* (custom and blood-feud); (ii) *strict law* (formal rules); (iii) *equity and natural law* (moral standards enter law); (iv) *maturity of law* (equality and certainty); (v) *socialisation of law* (law turns to social interests and welfare).

**5. Method and the role of the judge.** The judge is to individualise justice and decide cases by looking to the social purposes of law, using social facts in addition to precedent. Pound admired Brandeis's use of social data in *Muller v. Oregon* (208 US 412, 1908) and Cardozo's idea of judging by social welfare (*The Nature of the Judicial Process*, 1921).

**6. Application in India**
The Indian Constitution is itself an instrument of social engineering. The Preamble promises social, economic and political justice. Part III secures individual interests; Part IV directs the State towards social interests (Arts. 38, 39, 41, 43, 46); Art. 51A lists duties. The courts, in turn, balance competing interests:
1. **Reconciling individual and social interests.** The doctrine of *reasonable restrictions* (Art. 19(2)–(6)) and the test of **proportionality** weigh a claimed right against the interest of the public (*Modern Dental College v. State of M.P.*, 2016; *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India*, 2017, and the Aadhaar decision of 2018 (reported 2019); *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India*, 2020, on internet shutdowns).
2. **Public interest litigation.** In *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) and *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985, the right to livelihood of pavement dwellers within Art. 21), the Court enlarged access to justice for the weak.
3. **Industrial risk.** In *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (the Oleum gas leak case, 1986/1987), the Court laid down the rule of absolute liability for enterprises engaged in hazardous activity, a rule that places the cost of risk on those who profit by it (compare Pound's postulate on risk and *Rylands v. Fletcher*).
4. **Environment.** *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) accepted sustainable development and the precautionary principle in balancing development against environmental protection.
5. **Gender justice.** *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) laid down guidelines on sexual harassment at work.
6. **Reservation.** *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* (1992) balanced equality of opportunity with the need to remedy backwardness.
7. **Speech and reputation.** *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) upheld criminal defamation as a reasonable restriction on free speech in the interest of reputation, an example of weighing an individual interest against another.

**7. Evaluation and criticism**
1. **The classification of interests is arbitrary and overlapping.** The same interest can be put in different classes. Julius Stone said they are the same interests seen from different angles.
2. **No scale of values.** Pound gave no firm test for deciding which interest should prevail when they conflict; the jural postulates reflect the American society of his time.
3. **"Engineering" suggests an elite of experts** who manipulate society. Law is not simply a tool, and a democratic society must decide its own ends.
4. **Culturally limited.** It reflects the conditions of early twentieth-century America.
5. **"Interest" is vague.**
6. **Not enough empirical work.** Realists said Pound talked about social facts without studying them.
7. **Blurs law and policy**, the positivists object.
8. **Danger of judicial overreach.** Courts balancing interests may take over the policy functions of legislatures.

**8. Merits.** Pound made law dynamic and purposive, linked law with social science, gave a framework for balancing interests that is still used in constitutional adjudication (proportionality), and encouraged welfare legislation and judicial concern for the social effect of decisions.

**Conclusion.** Pound's theory sees law as a tool for building an efficient social structure by recognising, delimiting and balancing the many interests of individuals, the State and society. Despite its lack of a fixed scale of values, it has become part of the working method of modern courts, and is especially visible in the Indian Constitution and in the Supreme Court's constitutional decisions.

## C-17 · The sociological school of jurisprudence: background and characteristics, with the contributions of Jhering and Ehrlich

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Sociological School: Background and Characteristics"). Related: B-32 (Jhering), B-33 (Ehrlich), B-37 (characteristics), Dec 2024 Q9 (C-20).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** background 5 · precursors (Comte to Ehrlich) 6 · characteristics 5 · evaluation and Indian relevance 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** The sociological school studies law as a social phenomenon: its origins in social needs, its working in society and its effect on social life. It flourished from the late nineteenth century, and it differs from the analytical school (law as command), the historical school (law as a growth from the past) and natural law (law as an expression of reason or justice). The aim is to ask what law *does*.

**1. Background**
1. **Industrialisation and social conflict.** Factories, urban poverty, labour unrest and new dangers (railways, machinery) showed that the law of individual rights and free contract did not fit the needs of the age.
2. **Dissatisfaction with earlier schools.** The analytical school treated law as a closed system of commands; the historical school looked to the past and distrusted legislation; natural law was vague. None of them asked what law does in society.
3. **The rise of sociology.**
   - **Auguste Comte** (1798–1857) named "sociology" and called for the scientific study of society (positivism).
   - **Herbert Spencer** (*Social Statics*, 1851) saw society as an organism and proposed a "law of equal freedom".
   - **Émile Durkheim** (*The Division of Labour in Society*, 1893) distinguished *mechanical solidarity* (resemblance, in simple societies) from *organic solidarity* (interdependence through division of labour, in modern societies), and treated law as the visible index of solidarity: repressive law belongs with mechanical solidarity and restitutive law with organic solidarity.
   - **Max Weber** (*Economy and Society*) studied types of legal authority and the formal rationality of modern law.
4. **Movements within law.** Holmes (*The Common Law*, 1881: "the life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience"), the Free Law Movement (Gény, Kantorowicz), Brandeis's use of social facts in court, and Cardozo's idea of the judge as weighing social welfare.

**2. Precursors**

*(a) Rudolf von Jhering (1818–92).*
- Works: *Der Kampf ums Recht* (*The Struggle for Law*, 1872) and *Der Zweck im Recht* (*Law as a Means to an End*, 1877–83).
- **Law has a purpose.** Law is not simply the will of the sovereign, or the spirit of a people; it is a means to a social end, and the end is the protection of the conditions of social life. He defined law as the sum of the conditions of social life in the widest sense, secured by the State through external compulsion.
- **Interests.** A right is a legally protected interest. Law balances the interests of individuals and of society (social utilitarianism).
- **Struggle for law.** Each person has a duty to assert his legal rights, because the defence of rights is also the defence of law itself and serves society.

*(b) Eugen Ehrlich (1862–1922).*
- Work: *Grundlegung der Soziologie des Rechts* (*Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law*, 1913), based on study of the customary law of Bukovina.
- **Living law.** The centre of gravity of legal development lies "not in legislation, nor in juristic science, nor in judicial decision, but in society itself". The *living law* is the law that actually governs social life (in families, associations, trade, villages), as distinct from *State law*, the rules used by courts to decide disputes.
- **Consequence.** Officials and courts should understand and respect the living law. A code that departs from it will not work.
- **Indian echo.** Lok Adalats under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, and the settlement of disputes by village panchayats are examples of the working of living law.

*(c) Free Law Movement.* François Gény (*Méthode d'interprétation et sources en droit privé positif*, 1899), and Hermann Kantorowicz (*The Battle for Legal Science*, 1906) said that judges, when the statute is silent or unclear, should find law by free scientific research into social purposes, not by mere logic.

*(d) Roscoe Pound and Duguit* are the principal later figures (see C-16 and C-18).

**3. Characteristics of the school**
1. Law is a **social phenomenon** to be studied by its function and effect, not only by its form.
2. Emphasis on **law in action**, not only on law in books.
3. Law is an instrument of **social control and social change**; legislation is a tool of social engineering.
4. Preference for **interests and social purposes** over abstract rights.
5. The school is **interdisciplinary**: law is studied with sociology, economics, psychology and anthropology.
6. It is **empirical** in method, using facts, statistics and social surveys (the "Brandeis brief" in *Muller v. Oregon*, 1908).
7. It is **pragmatic and reform-minded**.
8. It stresses **individualisation of justice**: a reasonable and just solution of each case.
9. **Pound's programme** (*The Scope and Purpose of Sociological Jurisprudence*, 1911–12) lists six points: study of the actual social effects of legal institutions and doctrines; sociological study in preparation for law-making; study of the means of making legal rules effective; study of juridical method; a sociological legal history; and the importance of reasonable and just solution of individual cases.

**4. Sociological jurisprudence and the sociology of law.** *Sociological jurisprudence* (Pound) is jurisprudence informed by social facts, aiming at law reform. The *sociology of law* (Ehrlich, Weber) is a descriptive social science of law as a social phenomenon.

**5. The sociological school compared with others**

| Basis | Analytical | Historical | Sociological |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | Law as it is (command, norm) | Origin and growth | Function and effect |
| Method | Analysis | History | Social facts, interests |
| Source of law | Sovereign | Custom, *Volksgeist* | Needs of society |
| Role of legislation | Central | Secondary | A tool of social engineering |
| Idea of law | Command or norm | Organic growth | Social control |
| Typical thinkers | Austin, Kelsen, Hart | Savigny, Maine | Jhering, Ehrlich, Pound, Duguit |

**6. Evaluation**
*Merits.* It brought law into touch with social reality; encouraged social legislation and welfare policy; supported purposive interpretation; and widened the training of lawyers.
*Criticism.* (i) It lacks a single, clear theory: its writers differ. (ii) "Social interest" and "social need" are vague, and no scale of values is offered. (iii) It may undervalue the autonomy of law, certainty and the protection of individual rights (the positivist and Kelsenian objection). (iv) Ehrlich's "living law" is hard to identify and may merge law with social custom. (v) It may give judges too much freedom. (vi) It may be used to justify State control.

**7. The sociological approach in India.** The Constitution itself is an instrument of social change (Preamble; Parts III and IV). Courts have adopted a sociological approach in public interest litigation (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984), the right to livelihood (*Olga Tellis*, 1985), the right to a speedy trial for undertrials (*Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar*, 1979), environmental protection (*M.C. Mehta*, *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum*) and gender justice (*Vishaka*, 1997). Parliament has used law as an instrument of social change: the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (as amended in 2005), the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955, the Right to Education Act, 2009, the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.

**Conclusion.** The sociological school arose out of dissatisfaction with abstract and formal theories of law and the rise of the social sciences. Its contribution is to see law as a means to social ends and to ask how it works in fact. Its weakness is the absence of a settled standard for choosing among social interests, but its method now underlies the work of modern courts and legislatures.

## C-18 · Leon Duguit's theory of social solidarity

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q8 (B, "Write short note on Duguit's doctrine of 'Social Solidarity'"); Dec 2024 Q5 (A, "Social Solidarity"); Dec 2023 Q4 (A, "Duguit"); Dec 2025 Q5 (A, "Contribution of Leon Duguit"). **Asked in all four sittings** (as 4- and 10-mark questions; this is the 20-mark version).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** life, works, background 3 · social solidarity and objective law 6 · State, sovereignty, rights 4 · property and public service 3 · criticism and India 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** Léon Duguit (1859–1928), a French jurist and professor of law at Bordeaux, developed a sociological theory of law centred on **social solidarity**. He rejected the doctrines of individual natural rights and of the sovereign State's will. His main works are *L'État, le droit objectif et la loi positive* (1901), *Le droit social, le droit individuel et la transformation de l'État* (1908), *Traité de droit constitutionnel* (1911), *Les transformations générales du droit privé depuis le Code Napoléon* (1912) and *Les transformations du droit public* (1913), translated as *Law in the Modern State* (1919). He drew on **Auguste Comte** and **Émile Durkheim**.

**1. Social solidarity as the basis of law**
1. **Starting point: a social fact.** Human beings live in society and are interdependent. This interdependence, which Duguit called social solidarity, is a fact of life; it is not a mere theory.
2. **Two kinds of solidarity** (taken from Durkheim): (i) *solidarity by similarity* (people share common needs and ideas, which they satisfy by living together), and (ii) *solidarity by division of labour* (people exchange services and so depend on each other).
3. **Objective law (*droit objectif*).** The rule of conduct that arises from social solidarity is the objective law. It exists as a **fact**, before any statute; it is neither a command of the State nor a natural right nor an abstract rule of reason.
4. **How a rule becomes law.** A rule is law when the community feels that its violation is a breach of social solidarity and that a social reaction against it (not necessarily State force) is justified. Statutes (*positive law*) are valid only so far as they put the objective law into effect.
5. **Duty to promote solidarity.** The basic rule is that every person must do nothing against social solidarity and must do everything to promote it.

**2. Rejection of sovereignty and of the State's will**
- Duguit rejected the idea of the State as a sovereign legal person with a will above the law. For him, sovereignty is a metaphysical fiction.
- The State is simply the *strongest group in society*, those who in fact have the power to command and compel. Its power is **limited by the objective law**.
- Its authority is justified only so far as it serves social solidarity, and a law which does not is not binding.

**3. Rejection of individual rights**
- Individuals have no natural or subjective rights to assert against society. They have *duties*: each person has a social function to perform, by which he contributes to solidarity.
- What are called "rights" are only powers to perform social functions. A person has the protection of law so far as he fulfils his function.

**4. Property as a social function**
Property is not an absolute right but a social function. The owner holds property for the benefit of society, and is bound to use it for productive purposes and consistently with social needs; if he does not, the law may intervene. Duguit's idea has influenced the modern treatment of ownership as carrying duties.

**5. Public service (*service public*).** The State is not a ruler but a cooperative of public services (education, transport, justice, security) existing to promote solidarity. The public official is a servant of the public, and the State is responsible for failures of public service. This became a pillar of French administrative law and the welfare State.

**6. Duguit compared with other thinkers**
- *Austin:* Duguit rejects the command and sovereignty theory.
- *Kelsen:* Duguit shares the identification of State and law in some respects, but Kelsen finds the basis of law in a presupposed norm, Duguit in social fact.
- *Pound:* Both are sociological, but Pound balances interests, while Duguit stresses duty and function; Pound keeps individual interests, Duguit denies subjective rights.
- *Marx:* Duguit's society is one of cooperation, not class struggle.

**7. Criticism**
1. **"Social solidarity" is vague and subjective;** it does not give precise rules or resolve conflicts.
2. **Denial of rights is impractical and dangerous.** A theory based on duties and social functions can be used to justify State control of the individual and collectivism.
3. **Confuses "is" and "ought"** (Kelsen, Hart): to treat law as a social fact does not explain why it binds.
4. **Underrates the State and legislation.** Modern society cannot do without sovereign authority and statutes.
5. **No test** to tell whether a given law promotes solidarity.
6. **Ignores conflict, power and class** (Marxist critique).
7. **Hard to square with enforceable fundamental rights** in a constitutional system.

**8. Merits.** He shifted attention from the State's will to social need; he weakened the idea of absolute State power and absolute property; gave the welfare State and public-service idea a legal basis; and influenced administrative law and the movement for the social control of property.

**9. Indian parallels**
1. **Property.** Arts. 39(b) and (c) direct the State to secure that the ownership and control of the material resources of the community are distributed to subserve the common good, and that the economic system does not concentrate wealth. The right to property was removed from the list of fundamental rights by the 44th Amendment (1978) and now stands as a constitutional right in Art. 300A. In *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024) a nine-Judge Bench held that not every privately owned resource is a "material resource of the community" under Art. 39(b), and did not accept the broad view of Krishna Iyer J. (in *State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy*, 1977, followed in *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal*, 1983) that all private resources are included. The social-function idea remains in Art. 39(b) but within limits.
2. **Duties.** Art. 51A lists fundamental duties of citizens, which echo Duguit's stress on duty and social function.
3. **Public service.** The State is treated as a welfare State, with duties in education (Art. 21A), health, and livelihood, and the courts hold State officials accountable. Compensation for the failure of public authorities is awarded under public law (*Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar*, 1983).
4. **Limits.** Unlike Duguit, the Indian Constitution protects individual rights (Part III) and enforces them through the courts, so it balances duty and right.

**Conclusion.** Duguit placed the foundation of law in the interdependence of human beings and the duty of each to serve it. His theory weakened the idea of absolute State will and absolute private property and helped to build the idea of the welfare State and public service. It fails to give a clear test of solidarity and risks undervaluing individual rights, but its influence is visible in the directive principles of the Indian Constitution and in the social control of property.
