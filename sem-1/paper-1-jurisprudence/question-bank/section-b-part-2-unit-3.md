## B-14 · What is the difference between Austin and Kelsen as jurists?

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q7 ("What is difference between Austin and Kelson [Kelsen] as Jurists?"). Related: C-10, A-41.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each jurist in one line 2 · table of differences 5 · similarities 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**John Austin** (1790–1859; *Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, 1832) defined law as the command of a sovereign backed by sanction. **Hans Kelsen** (1881–1973; *Pure Theory of Law*, 1934/1960) defined law as a hierarchy of coercive norms deriving validity from a basic norm.

| Point | Austin | Kelsen |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of law | Command | Norm (an "ought") |
| Source of validity | The sovereign's will | A higher norm, ending in the Grundnorm |
| Sovereign | Central, legally unlimited | Rejected; State is the legal order itself |
| Sanction | Evil threatened to the subject | Norm directing officials to apply sanction |
| Method | Empirical analysis of power | Logical, "pure", non-causal |
| International law | Positive morality | True law (monism) |
| Constitution | Positive morality | Highest positive law |
| Efficacy | Habit of obedience | Condition of validity, not its source |

**Similarities.** Both are positivists: they separate law from morality, treat sanction as essential, and exclude natural law. Kelsen was influenced by Austin but rejected his psychological and sociological elements.

**Evaluation.** Austin explains statute law but not constitutional or customary law; Kelsen explains the unity of a legal system and judicial review but is too formal and rests on a fiction-like Grundnorm.

## B-15 · Is it correct to say that the Pure Theory of Law has left only the dry bones of law, deprived of flesh and blood? Explain

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q7 ("Is it correct to say that Pure Theory of Law has left dry bones of law, deprived of flesh and blood, which give them life? Explain."). 20-mark form: C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** the charge 2 · why it is made 3 · Kelsen's defence 3 · verdict 2

### Answer

**The charge.** Critics say Kelsen's Pure Theory leaves only the skeleton of law, its logical structure of norms, while stripping away the "flesh and blood": justice, social purpose, history, politics and human behaviour.

**Why it is made**
1. Kelsen excluded ethics, sociology, psychology and politics to build a "pure" science of law.
2. The theory cannot say whether a law is just, or why people obey it.
3. The Grundnorm is a presupposition without content; the theory has been used to explain coups (*State v. Dosso*, 1958).
4. Efficacy is a fact, so the claim of purity is doubtful.

**Kelsen's defence.** Purity is a method, not a denial of the other aspects: a science must have its own object. Law as a structure of norms can be described accurately only if morals and sociology are kept apart. The skeleton is needed before the flesh can be studied, and other sciences may supply it. It also explains the unity of the legal system and judicial review.

**Verdict.** The charge is correct that the theory gives only the structure of law. But Kelsen accepted this deliberately; the structure is useful though incomplete without the sociological and ethical views.

## B-16 · "According to Hart, rules are concerned not with what happens but with what is to be done." Explain

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q8 ("According to Hart rules are concerned not with what happens but with what is to be done?"). 20-mark form: C-11.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** habit and rule 3 · internal aspect 3 · contrast with Austin and the realists 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

In *The Concept of Law* (1961) Hart distinguishes a **habit** from a **rule**.

**Habit.** A habit is merely convergent behaviour: many people do the same thing. It describes *what happens*.

**Rule.** A rule sets a standard of what *is to be done*. Besides regular conduct, it has an **internal aspect**: those who follow it treat it as a reason for acting, and as a ground for criticising deviation ("you must stop at a red light"; the rule in chess that the bishop moves diagonally). The language of rules is "ought", "must", "should".

**Application.** (1) *Against Austin:* law cannot be explained by the habit of obedience to a sovereign, since that covers only what happens; people also feel they have an obligation. (2) *Against the realists:* if law were only the prediction of what courts will do (Holmes), it would describe what happens; but a judge applying a rule does not predict his own decision, he accepts the rule as his standard (the **internal point of view**).

**Conclusion.** Law is normative: it tells what is to be done, not merely what usually happens.

## B-17 · Austin's theory of sovereignty

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Austin"). Related: C-08, A-38.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** the definition 3 · characteristics 3 · criticism 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Definition.** "If a determinate human superior, not in a habit of obedience to a like superior, receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, that superior is sovereign in that society, and the society (including the superior) is a society political and independent."

**Characteristics**
1. *Determinate:* a person or a definite body of persons.
2. *Habitual obedience* by the bulk of society.
3. *Independent:* he himself obeys no like superior.
4. *Indivisible:* one sovereign in each society.
5. *Legally illimitable:* a legally limited sovereign is a contradiction; limits are only moral or political.
6. In a democracy the sovereign is the electorate with the legislature (in England, the Crown, the Lords and the electors of the Commons).

**Criticism**
1. In a federal State, or one with a written constitution, sovereignty is divided or limited.
2. Habit of obedience cannot explain continuity of law when rulers change (Hart's Rex I and Rex II).
3. The sovereign is often bound by law (manner and form).
4. It neglects custom and international law.

**India.** The Constitution is supreme; Parliament is limited by the basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973; *Minerva Mills*, 1980). The Austinian sovereign does not exist.

## B-18 · Kelsen's Grundnorm (basic norm)

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q3 (A, "Ground Norm"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-42. Related: C-10.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · functions and efficacy 3 · revolution 2 · criticism 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** The Grundnorm (basic norm, "ground norm") is the highest norm of a legal system, from which all other norms derive validity. It is not enacted by anyone; it is *presupposed* by juristic thought as a condition of making sense of the legal order. Its content is: "the historically first constitution ought to be obeyed."

**Function.** In Kelsen's hierarchy (*Stufenbau*), a norm is valid because it was created as authorised by a higher norm. The chain runs from individual acts, through delegated legislation and statutes, to the Constitution, and ends in the Grundnorm. It thus gives the system its unity and explains why the Constitution is binding.

**Efficacy.** A Grundnorm is presupposed only for a legal order that is by and large effective. Efficacy is a condition of validity, not its source.

**Revolution.** If a revolution succeeds and the new order becomes effective, a new Grundnorm is presupposed (*State v. Dosso*, Pakistan, 1958; overruled in *Asma Jilani*, 1972).

**Criticism.** It is a fiction (Kelsen's later work used Vaihinger's "as if"); it is difficult to identify; the reliance on efficacy undermines purity; and it can legitimise a coup. Hart's rule of recognition is an empirical social fact in its place.

*Word count: 199*

## B-19 · Jeremy Bentham's contribution to jurisprudence

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q4; Dec 2023 Q2; Dec 2022 Q4 (all Section A as "Bentham"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-34.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** life and works 2 · utility 2 · expositorial/censorial 2 · other contributions 3 · criticism 1

### Answer

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), an English jurist and reformer, is the father of the analytical school and of utilitarianism. Works: *A Fragment on Government* (1776); *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789); *Of Laws in General* (written about 1782, published 1945).

1. **Principle of utility.** Actions and laws are to be judged by whether they promote the greatest happiness of the greatest number.
2. **Expositorial and censorial jurisprudence.** The first states what the law is; the second criticises what it ought to be, by the test of utility. He thus separated law from morals.
3. **Law as command.** He treated law as the command of the sovereign, an idea that Austin developed.
4. **Attack on natural law and natural rights.** He called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts" and rejected the social contract (in the *Fragment*, against Blackstone).
5. **Codification.** He urged complete, clear codes (he coined the word "codification"); his ideas influenced Macaulay's Indian Penal Code and the codes of the nineteenth century.
6. **Reform.** Prison reform (the Panopticon), law reform, representative government.

**Criticism.** Utility ignores justice to minorities and the quality of pleasures (J.S. Mill, Rawls); the command theory is narrow.

## B-20 · Hart's primary and secondary rules

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Hart"). Related: C-11, A-48, A-49.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** primary rules 2 · secondary rules (three) 5 · union 2 · criticism 1

### Answer

In *The Concept of Law* (1961) Hart replaced Austin's command model by the union of two types of rule.

**Primary rules** impose duties to do or abstain from acts (the rules against violence, theft and fraud). A society with only primary rules is a simple, pre-legal one. It has three defects, each cured by a secondary rule:

| Defect | Secondary rule | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncertainty** (which rules count?) | **Rule of recognition** | Identifies the authoritative rules of the system |
| **Static character** (no way to change rules) | **Rule of change** | Empowers persons or bodies to make, amend or repeal rules |
| **Inefficiency** (no authoritative decision on breach) | **Rule of adjudication** | Empowers courts to decide whether a rule has been broken and to apply sanctions |

**Secondary rules** are about the primary rules and confer powers (public and private). Hart called the union of the two "the key to the science of jurisprudence".

**Indian illustration.** The Constitution as accepted by the courts supplies recognition; Arts. 245–246 and 368 supply change; Arts. 124, 214 and 141 supply adjudication.

**Criticism.** Dworkin: law also includes principles; critics: the rule of recognition may be uncertain, and the division of rules is too neat.

*Word count: 191*

## B-21 · Is international law "law"? The views of Austin, Kelsen and Hart

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q5 (A, "Does international law is law according to Austin?"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-36. Related: C-08.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Austin 3 · Kelsen 2 · Hart 3 · India and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Austin.** International law is not law "properly so called" but "positive international morality", because it is not set by a sovereign to subjects: there is no sovereign over states and no determinate commander. Its sanction is only the opinion of nations.

**Criticism of Austin.** States regard international law as binding and give reasons based on it. Sanctions exist in the form of reprisals, countermeasures, war and Security Council action.

**Kelsen.** International law is true law: it is a coercive order that attaches sanctions (reprisals and war) to wrongful acts, and its basic norm is that states ought to behave as they have customarily behaved. He preferred the *monist* view, the primacy of international law, with national law derived from it.

**Hart.** International law is law, though in a primitive form because it lacks a secondary rule of change and adjudication with compulsory jurisdiction and a unifying rule of recognition. Obligation does not depend on sanctions but on states' acceptance of the rules.

**India.** Art. 51(c) directs the State to foster respect for international law; Art. 253 lets Parliament legislate to implement treaties; courts read international norms into domestic law in the absence of conflict (*Vishaka*, 1997).

*Word count: 196*

## B-22 · Sanction: its meaning and role in law

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q1 (A, "Sanction"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-35.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · kinds 3 · views of jurists 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** A sanction is the evil, punishment or compulsion that follows disobedience of a rule and gives the rule its force. In Austin's terms it is the "evil" threatened by the sovereign, and it is what turns a wish into a command.

**Kinds**
1. *Punishment* (fine, imprisonment, death) in criminal law.
2. *Restitution and compensation* (damages, restoration, specific performance) in civil law.
3. *Nullity* (void or voidable acts), such as an agreement made without free consent.
Bentham also recognised rewards (remunerative sanctions).

**Views**
1. **Austin:** sanction is essential to law; a rule without it is not a law properly so called.
2. **Salmond:** law is enforced by the State's coercive power, with a physical sanction.
3. **Kelsen:** law is a coercive order; the norm directs officials to apply sanctions; a rule without a sanction is not a legal norm.
4. **Hart:** sanction is a natural necessity in view of human vulnerability, not a definitional feature; people also obey because they accept rules (internal point of view).
5. **Natural law and sociological schools:** obedience rests on reason, justice or social solidarity.

**Conclusion.** Sanction is important but not the whole of law.

## B-23 · Kelsen's theory and revolution: *Dosso*, *Asma Jilani* and *Matovu*

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Kelsen"). Related: C-10, B-18, A-47.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** theory 2 · each case 2 · evaluation 2

### Answer

**Theory.** For Kelsen a legal order is valid if it is by and large effective. If a revolution or coup succeeds and the new order becomes effective, a new Grundnorm is presupposed; the new constitution is valid even though not made as the old one provided.

**State v. Dosso** (PLD 1958 SC 533, Pakistan). In October 1958 the President abrogated the 1956 Constitution and martial law was proclaimed. Chief Justice Munir, relying on Kelsen, held that a successful revolution is a law-creating fact, and the new order was valid because effective.

**Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab** (PLD 1972 SC 139). The Supreme Court overruled *Dosso*, holding that Kelsen's theory had been misapplied and that a usurper does not become lawful merely by success.

**Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex p. Matovu** ([1966] EA 514). The High Court of Uganda held that after the 1966 abrogation of the 1962 Constitution, the new order, being effective, was the valid legal order.

**Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke** ([1969] 1 AC 645, PC). The Privy Council refused to treat the 1965 Rhodesian regime as lawful.

**Evaluation.** The cases show the risk that efficacy may legitimise force.

## B-24 · Hart's internal point of view and open texture

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Hart"). Related: C-11, A-50, A-51.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** internal and external points of view 4 · open texture and discretion 4 · significance 2

### Answer

**Internal and external points of view.** The *external* observer notes regularities of behaviour and the threat of punishment, but does not accept the rule. The *internal* participant accepts the rule as a standard for his own conduct and for criticising others. Law cannot be understood without the internal point of view. A legal system exists where the officials accept the secondary rules, especially the rule of recognition, from the internal point of view, and the citizens generally obey.

**Open texture.** Every general rule has a core of settled meaning and a penumbra of doubt. Hart's example is "No vehicles in the park": a car is plainly covered, but a bicycle, roller-skates or a toy car may not be. In the penumbra the judge must exercise discretion and so makes law within limits.

**Significance.** Open texture is Hart's middle path between formalism (judges only apply rules) and rule-scepticism (law is what judges do). Dworkin disagreed that judges have such discretion.

**India.** Terms such as "reasonable restrictions" (Art. 19), "procedure established by law" (Art. 21) and "equal protection" (Art. 14) show open texture; the Supreme Court gives them content (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978).

*Word count: 190*
