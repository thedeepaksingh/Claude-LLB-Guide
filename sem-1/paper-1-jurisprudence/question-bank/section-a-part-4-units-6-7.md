<a id="a-88"></a>
**A-88 · Justice Holmes** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q2*

Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (1841–1935), Justice of the US Supreme Court (1902–32), is the intellectual father of American realism. In *The Common Law* (1881) he said "the life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience"; in *The Path of the Law* (1897) he defined law as a prediction of what courts will do and introduced the "bad man". He dissented in *Lochner v. New York* (1905).

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-89"></a>
**A-89 · Prediction theory of law** · *Related: B-38*

Holmes: "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the Law" (*The Path of the Law*, 1897). Law is not a system of abstract rules or moral principles but a prediction of how courts will decide. Criticism: it is circular, a judge cannot predict his own decision, and it ignores legislation (Hart).

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-90"></a>
**A-90 · The "bad man" theory** · *Related: B-38*

Holmes said the law should be seen as a "bad man" sees it: he cares nothing for ethical rules or the logic of doctrine, but only for the material consequences (damages, imprisonment) that the courts will visit on him. The theory separates law from morals for the purpose of analysis, and shows law in terms of its practical effect.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-91"></a>
**A-91 · "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience"** · *Related: C-19*

Holmes (*The Common Law*, 1881) meant that the felt necessities of the time, prevalent moral and political theories, public policy and even the prejudices of judges have shaped legal rules more than syllogisms. It is a protest against formalism or "mechanical jurisprudence", and became a slogan of realism and sociological jurisprudence.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-92"></a>
**A-92 · Gray's view of law** · *Related: B-39*

John Chipman Gray (*The Nature and Sources of the Law*, 1909) held that the law of a State consists of the rules the courts lay down. A statute is only a source of law, like precedent or custom, until a court interprets and applies it. In effect all law is judge-made. Criticism: it ignores legislation and is circular.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-93"></a>
**A-93 · Llewellyn** · *Related: B-40*

Karl Llewellyn (1893–1962), Columbia professor and chief reporter of the Uniform Commercial Code, held that law is what officials do about disputes. He distinguished "paper rules" from "real rules", stressed rule-scepticism, listed the "law-jobs" of a group (*The Cheyenne Way*, 1941), and later spoke of the Grand Style of judging. Works: *The Bramble Bush* (1930); *Some Realism About Realism* (1931).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-94"></a>
**A-94 · Jerome Frank** · *Related: B-40*

Jerome Frank (1889–1957), judge of the Second Circuit, in *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930) and *Courts on Trial* (1949), argued that the real uncertainty of law lies in the facts found at trial, because witnesses err, juries are unpredictable and the judge's personality and "hunch" matter. This is *fact-scepticism*. Certainty in law is a myth; *actual law* is a past decision, *probable law* a guess.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-95"></a>
**A-95 · Rule-sceptics and fact-sceptics** · *Related: B-40*

Rule-sceptics (Llewellyn) doubt that legal rules by themselves decide cases, and study what courts actually do. Fact-sceptics (Frank) hold that even if rules were certain, the finding of facts at trial is uncertain, being affected by unreliable witnesses and the psychology of judge and jury. Both reject the claim that law is a closed system that gives certain answers.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-96"></a>
**A-96 · Features of American realism** · *Related: B-41*

Realism is a movement (c. 1900–1940), not a school. Its features: law is in flux; law is a means to social ends; temporary separation of "is" and "ought"; rule-scepticism; focus on what judges and officials actually do; evaluation of law by its effects; empirical and functional method; anti-formalism. Llewellyn listed these in *Some Realism About Realism* (1931).

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-97"></a>
**A-97 · American and Scandinavian realism** · *Related: B-41*

American realism (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank) is behavioural and predictive: it studies what judges do. Scandinavian realism (Hägerström, Olivecrona, Lundstedt, Ross) is psychological: it rejects metaphysical ideas such as "right" and "duty" and treats law as social and psychological fact; Ross defined valid law as the rules judges will actually apply and feel bound by.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-98"></a>
**A-98 · Criticism of realism** · *Related: B-42*

Realism overstates uncertainty, since most cases are routine and rules constrain judges. Law as prediction is useless to the judge and the citizen. It is circular, neglects legislation, threatens the rule of law by stressing judges' personality, and gives no guidance on what judges ought to do. Hart argued that rule-scepticism ignores the internal point of view.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-99"></a>
**A-99 · Realism in India** · *Related: B-43*

Indian practice supports the realist claim that judges make law: Art. 141 (law declared by the Supreme Court binds all courts), the basic-structure doctrine (*Kesavananda*, 1973), expansion of Art. 21 (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978), absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987), the *Vishaka* guidelines (1997) and the collegium. The courts do not, however, call themselves realist.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-100"></a>
**A-100 · Realism and sociological jurisprudence: difference** · *Related: C-20*

Both study law in action and attack formalism. The sociological school (Pound) looks at social interests and the effect of law and wants to improve it by balancing interests. The realists focus on judges and officials, doubt that rules decide cases, and claim to describe before they evaluate. Pound criticised the realists for exaggerating judicial subjectivity.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-101"></a>
**A-101 · Marx's economic definition of law** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q1*

In the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) Marx and Engels told the bourgeoisie: "Your jurisprudence is but the will of your class made into a law for all, a will whose essential character and direction are determined by the economic conditions of existence of your class." Law is thus the will of the ruling economic class made binding on all, and its content is determined by the economic conditions.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-102"></a>
**A-102 · Base and superstructure** · *Related: B-44*

In the Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) Marx said that the economic structure (forces and relations of production) is the real foundation, or *base*, on which a legal and political *superstructure* rises. Law, the State, religion, morality and ideology belong to the superstructure and reflect the base. Engels added that the economy is decisive only "in the last instance".

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-103"></a>
**A-103 · Law as an instrument of class rule** · *Related: B-45*

For Marxists, law expresses the will of the ruling class and protects its property and power. Formal equality hides real inequality (a worker is "free" to contract but must sell his labour). Law is also ideology: it presents class rule as neutral justice and so wins consent. The State is the machinery of the ruling class.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-104"></a>
**A-104 · Withering away of the State and law** · *Related: B-45*

Marx and Engels held that after a transitional dictatorship of the proletariat, in higher communism there are no classes and no need for coercion, so the State "withers away" (Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, 1878) and with it law, replaced by the administration of things. The principle is "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs". The prediction has not come true.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-105"></a>
**A-105 · Engels' contribution** · *Related: C-21*

Friedrich Engels (1820–95), Marx's collaborator, wrote *Anti-Dühring* (1878) and *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884), tracing State and law to the rise of private property and classes. In letters of 1890 (to Bloch and Schmidt) he qualified economic determinism: the economy is decisive only "in the last instance", and law and politics have relative autonomy.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-106"></a>
**A-106 · Lenin on the State** · *Related: B-48*

Lenin (*The State and Revolution*, 1917) held that the State is a machine of class oppression. The bourgeois State must be smashed by revolution and replaced by a proletarian State which uses law against the old ruling class and will wither away as classes disappear. His view shaped the Soviet legal system.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-107"></a>
**A-107 · Pashukanis** · *Related: B-48*

Evgeny Pashukanis (1891–1937), in *The General Theory of Law and Marxism* (1924), argued that the legal form (equal legal subjects holding rights and making contracts) arises from the exchange of commodities in capitalism. When commodity exchange ends under communism, law will wither away. This is the commodity-exchange theory. He was purged and executed in 1937.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-108"></a>
**A-108 · Criticism of the Marxist theory of law** · *Related: B-46*

Economic determinism ignores religion, culture and politics, and the way law shapes the economy. Law is not only a class tool: labour and welfare laws protect the weak, and the rule of law restrains rulers (E.P. Thompson). The State and law did not wither away in socialist States. Class is not the only source of conflict, and the theory is unfalsifiable.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-109"></a>
**A-109 · Law and economics: Posner** · *Related: B-47*

Richard Posner (*Economic Analysis of Law*, 1973) applied economics to law, arguing that the common law tends to produce efficient rules and that the goal of law is wealth maximisation. The Hand formula (*United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 1947) explains negligence: a person is liable if the burden of precaution is less than probability of harm times loss. Dworkin criticised wealth maximisation.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-110"></a>
**A-110 · Coase theorem** · *Related: B-47*

Ronald Coase ("The Problem of Social Cost", 1960): if transaction costs are zero, parties will bargain to an efficient outcome whatever the initial legal rule of liability. Where transaction costs are high, the law's allocation of rights matters and should favour the party who can avoid the harm at lowest cost. The theorem is a benchmark, since transaction costs are never zero.

*Word count: 62*
