<a id="a-01"></a>
**A-01 · Meaning of "jurisprudence"** · *Related: C-01*

The word comes from the Latin *juris* (of law) and *prudentia* (knowledge or skill): knowledge of law. In modern use it is the theoretical, systematic and critical study of law: its nature, sources, basic concepts, purposes and working in society. It is concerned with general principles, not with the details of any one statute.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-02"></a>
**A-02 · Ulpian's definition** · *Related: C-01*

Ulpian (died 223), in the Digest (1.1.10.2), defined jurisprudence as "the knowledge of things divine and human, the science of the just and the unjust". It is the oldest definition and joins law with religion and ethics. The criticism is that it is too wide: it does not separate what law is from what it ought to be.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-03"></a>
**A-03 · Is jurisprudence the study of the law of one nation?** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q2*

No. Jurisprudence studies the general principles of law (its nature, sources and basic concepts) which are common to legal systems. The law of one nation is the subject of its own branches (for example, Indian contract law). Austin called the general study "general jurisprudence" and the study of one system "particular jurisprudence". Illustrations and comparisons are drawn from many systems, so the study is not national.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-04"></a>
**A-04 · Salmond** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q3*

Sir John Salmond (1862–1924), author of *Jurisprudence* (1902), defined jurisprudence as "the science of the first principles of the civil law", that is, the law of the land applied by courts. In the wider sense he divided it into legal exposition, legal history and the science of legislation. He also defined law as "the body of principles recognised and applied by the State in the administration of justice".

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-05"></a>
**A-05 · Holland's definition** · *Related: C-03*

T.E. Holland (*Elements of Jurisprudence*, 1880) defined jurisprudence as "the formal science of positive law". "Formal" means that it studies the relations that law governs and the ideas of those relations, not the content of the rules. Holland rejected Austin's division into general and particular jurisprudence. The criticism is that "formal" excludes the content, purpose and history of law.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-06"></a>
**A-06 · "Jurisprudence is lawyer's extroversion" (Stone)** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q5*

Julius Stone (*The Province and Function of Law*, first published 1946–47) described jurisprudence as "the lawyer's extroversion": the lawyer's examination of the precepts, ideals and techniques of law in the light of knowledge drawn from outside law, such as sociology, economics, ethics and psychology. It shows that jurisprudence is interdisciplinary and looks outward beyond the rules of a single system.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-07"></a>
**A-07 · Gray's definition** · *Related: C-01*

John Chipman Gray (*The Nature and Sources of the Law*, 1909) defined jurisprudence as "the science of law, that is, the statement and systematic arrangement of the rules followed by the courts and the principles involved in those rules". It is court-centred and treats statutes as sources of law. The criticism is that it ignores legislation and the social setting of law.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-08"></a>
**A-08 · "Jurisprudence is the philosophy of positive law" (Austin)** · *Related: C-09*

John Austin (*The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, 1832) limited jurisprudence to positive law, that is, law set by a political superior to political inferiors, and studied it by analysis of its concepts. Divine law, positive morality and law by analogy (including international law) are outside it. Ethics belongs to the science of legislation. The definition is clear but narrow.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-09"></a>
**A-09 · Keeton and Dias on jurisprudence** · *Related: C-01*

G.W. Keeton described jurisprudence as "the study and systematic arrangement of the general principles of law". R.W.M. Dias treated it as legal theory: the study of the nature of law and of the ideas lawyers use. Both definitions are neutral between the schools and wide enough to include analytical, historical, ethical and sociological study.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-10"></a>
**A-10 · Expositorial and censorial jurisprudence** · *Related: B-04*

Bentham divided jurisprudence into *expositorial* jurisprudence, which states what the law is, and *censorial* jurisprudence, which criticises what it ought to be, by the test of utility. Expositorial jurisprudence may be authoritative (by the legislator) or unauthoritative (by writers). The distinction is the origin of the analytical separation of law as it is from law as it ought to be.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-11"></a>
**A-11 · General and particular jurisprudence** · *Related: C-03*

Austin: *general* jurisprudence studies the principles, notions and distinctions common to mature legal systems (right, duty, injury, sanction); *particular* jurisprudence is the science of a given actual system, such as English or Indian law. Holland denied that jurisprudence has two kinds. Salmond accepted that first principles may be studied for law in general or for one system.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-12"></a>
**A-12 · Law and morality: difference** · *Related: B-03, C-24*

Law is made and enforced by the State and has organised sanctions (fine, imprisonment, damages); morality rests on conscience, religion and social opinion and has inner or social sanctions. Law governs mainly external conduct, morality also motive. They overlap (murder is both illegal and immoral), but not everything immoral is unlawful (ingratitude) and some laws are morally neutral (rule of the road).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-13"></a>
**A-13 · Nature of jurisprudence: is it a science?** · *Related: C-02*

Jurisprudence is a social science: organised knowledge of law, reached by reasoning, history and observation of society. It is not an exact or experimental science, and its conclusions are contested between schools. It is theoretical and general, both descriptive (what law is) and normative (what it ought to be), dynamic (theories change with the age) and interdisciplinary.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-14"></a>
**A-14 · Utility of the study of jurisprudence** · *Related: B-01, C-02*

Jurisprudence explains the basic concepts of every branch of law; trains legal reasoning; guides interpretation of statutes and the Constitution; helps judges (*Gopalan* v. *Maneka Gandhi*, *Puttaswamy*); guides law reform and codification; compares legal systems; and links law with justice and society. Laski is said to have called it "the eye of law".

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-15"></a>
**A-15 · Jurisprudence and sociology** · *Related: B-05*

Law is a social institution, so jurisprudence and sociology overlap. Sociology studies society as a whole; jurisprudence studies law, but the sociological school (Ehrlich's living law, Pound's social engineering, Duguit's social solidarity) examines law's origin in social needs and its effect on society. Sociology of law is descriptive; sociological jurisprudence aims also at law reform.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-16"></a>
**A-16 · Natural law** · *Related: B-07, C-05*

Natural law is a body of principles of right, derived from nature, reason or God, which is higher than man-made law and by which it is judged. It is universal and unchanging, discovered by reason and not made by will, and the source of justice and natural rights. Main thinkers: Aristotle, Cicero, Aquinas, Grotius, Locke; later Stammler, Kohler, Fuller and Finnis.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-17"></a>
**A-17 · Manu** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q1; Dec 2023 Q1; Dec 2022 Q3*

Manu is the traditional first lawgiver in Hindu thought; the *Manusmriti* (*Manava Dharmashastra*), usually placed between about 200 BC and AD 200, bears his name. It deals with dharma: duties of the four *varnas* and stages of life, family law, inheritance, contract, crime and punishment, and the king's duties, in eighteen titles of law. It is criticised for caste and gender inequality.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-18"></a>
**A-18 · Sources of dharma (Manu) and sources of law (Kautilya)** · *Related: B-13*

Manu names four sources of dharma: the Veda, the *smriti*, the conduct of the virtuous (*sadachara*) and one's own conscience (*atmatushti*). Kautilya's *Arthashastra* names four sources of law: *dharma* (sacred law), *vyavahara* (transactions and evidence), *charitra* (custom) and *rajashasana* (royal edict); when they conflict, the later one overrides the earlier.

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-19"></a>
**A-19 · Aristotle on natural justice** · *Related: C-05*

Aristotle (384–322 BC, *Nicomachean Ethics*) distinguished natural justice, which has the same force everywhere and does not depend on opinion, from legal (conventional) justice, which is a matter of local enactment. He also distinguished distributive justice (division of honours and goods according to merit) from corrective justice (setting right wrongs between persons).

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-20"></a>
**A-20 · The Stoics and natural law** · *Related: C-05*

The Stoics (Zeno, 3rd century BC) taught that the universe is ordered by reason (*logos*) and that man, being rational, shares in it. To live according to nature is to live according to reason, so the law of nature is the law of reason, the same for all rational beings. The idea was taken by Cicero and the Roman jurists.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-21"></a>
**A-21 · Cicero on natural law** · *Related: C-05*

Cicero (106–43 BC, *De Republica*) described true law as "right reason in agreement with nature", of universal application, unchanging and eternal, which neither Senate nor people can repeal. Roman jurists distinguished *jus naturale* (natural law), *jus gentium* (the law of nations, common to all peoples) and *jus civile* (the civil law of Rome).

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-22"></a>
**A-22 · Aquinas: four kinds of law** · *Related: B-11*

St Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae*) distinguished: *eternal law*, the reason of God governing the universe; *divine law*, revealed in scripture; *natural law*, the part of eternal law known to man by reason; and *human law*, made by rulers for the common good. A human law that conflicts with natural law is a "perversion of law" and does not bind in conscience.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-23"></a>
**A-23 · Grotius** · *Related: C-06*

Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), in *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625), founded modern international law and secularised natural law. He held that natural law is the dictate of right reason, derived from man's rational and social nature, and would be valid "even if we were to concede that there is no God". He thus separated natural law from theology.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-24"></a>
**A-24 · Social contract: Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau** · *Related: C-05*

*Hobbes* (*Leviathan*, 1651): men in a state of nature make a contract surrendering their rights to an absolute sovereign for peace. *Locke* (*Second Treatise*, 1689): natural rights to life, liberty and property exist before government, which rests on consent and may be resisted if it breaks its trust. *Rousseau* (*The Social Contract*, 1762): law is the expression of the general will.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-25"></a>
**A-25 · Stammler** · *Related: B-09*

Rudolf Stammler (1856–1938), in *Die Lehre von dem richtigen Rechte* (1902), revived natural law as "natural law with a variable content": the form or method of just law is constant but its content varies with time and place. The ideal is a "community of free-willing men", with principles of respecting and participating. The criticism is that it is too formal.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-26"></a>
**A-26 · Kohler** · *Related: B-10*

Josef Kohler (1849–1919), a Hegelian jurist (*Philosophy of Law*, 1909), held that law is a product of civilisation and must be judged by how far it promotes the advance of civilisation. Law is relative: each age's law must suit its own civilisation. Judges must interpret law in the light of social needs. The criticism is that "civilisation" is vague.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-27"></a>
**A-27 · Fuller's eight desiderata** · *Related: B-12*

Lon Fuller (*The Morality of Law*, 1964) listed eight requirements of the "inner morality" of law: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between declared rule and official action. A total failure in any one does not produce a bad legal system but none at all.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-28"></a>
**A-28 · Finnis** · *Related: B-08*

John Finnis (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980) grounded natural law in basic human goods known by practical reason: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability (friendship), practical reasonableness and religion. These goods are self-evident and cannot be reduced to one another. Law is justified when it helps to secure them for the common good. This is a modern Thomist revival.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-29"></a>
**A-29 · Rawls** · *Related: B-08*

John Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971) argued that principles of justice are those that free and rational persons would choose in an "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance", not knowing their place in society. They chose equal basic liberties and a difference principle (inequalities allowed only if they help the least advantaged). It is a secular, contract-based form of natural-law thinking.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-30"></a>
**A-30 · Radbruch formula** · *Related: B-08, C-24*

Gustav Radbruch (1878–1949), after the Nazi regime, argued in 1946 that where a statute's conflict with justice reaches an intolerable degree, it must yield to justice and is "unlawful law". German courts used the idea in trials of Nazi-era crimes. It is a classic example of the revived natural-law view, opposed by Hart's positivism.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-31"></a>
**A-31 · Natural law ideas in India** · *Related: C-05, C-07*

Natural-law ideas appear in the basic-structure doctrine (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973), in the reading of fairness into Art. 21 (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978) and in *Puttaswamy* (2017), which held that life and liberty are natural rights existing before the Constitution and overruled *ADM Jabalpur* (1976). Earlier, *dharma* in the Indian tradition was a higher law binding even the king.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-32"></a>
**A-32 · Criticism of natural law** · *Related: B-07*

Its content is vague and disputed; it has defended opposite causes (slavery and its abolition; absolute and limited government). It confuses law with morals (Bentham, Austin). Its principles are assumed, not proved. It has no machinery of enforcement. The historical school showed that law varies with each society. Yet it supplies a standard for criticising unjust law and underlies human rights.

*Word count: 61*
