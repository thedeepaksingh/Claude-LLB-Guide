## C-08 · "Law is the command of the sovereign." Examine critically and say whether the concept applies to the Indian judicial system

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q10 ("Critically examine the statement that 'Law is the command of the sovereign' and state whether this concept applies to Indian Judicial system."). Related: C-09, C-04, B-17 (Austin's sovereignty).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** the theory and its elements 5 · criticism 6 · application to India 7 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The command theory was stated by John Austin (1790–1859) in *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832), building on Jeremy Bentham. Austin held that law, "properly so called", is a command given by a sovereign to his subjects and backed by a sanction. The theory is the foundation of analytical positivism. The question has two parts: a critical examination of the theory, and a test of how far it fits the Indian legal system.

**1. The theory**
1. **Command.** A command is a wish expressed by a determinate person that another shall do or forbear, with an evil (sanction) threatened if the wish is not obeyed. The power and purpose to inflict the evil is what turns a wish into a command.
2. **Duty and sanction.** A person commanded is under a duty to obey; a sanction is the evil that follows disobedience. Duty and command are correlative.
3. **General command.** A law commands a *class* of acts or forbearances, not a single act. An occasional command ("shut the window now") is not law.
4. **The sovereign.** If a determinate human superior, not himself in the habit of obedience to a like superior, receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, he is sovereign and the society is political and independent. Sovereignty is *determinate*, *independent*, *indivisible* and *legally illimitable*.
5. **Classification.** Positive law, set by a political superior, is the only subject of jurisprudence. Divine law and positive morality are outside it, and rules of fashion, honour and international law are "laws improperly so called".
6. **Custom and judge-made law.** Custom and precedent become law only when the sovereign adopts them: what he permits, he commands (a "tacit command").
7. **Law and morals.** Law may be unjust and yet be law; the question whether it ought to be obeyed is one of utility.

**2. Critical examination**
1. **Custom and early law.** In early societies law was custom, formed before any sovereign existed. Maine pointed out that Ranjit Singh, a powerful Sikh ruler, made hardly any laws while his society lived by custom. A rule of Hindu law does not become law because a sovereign tacitly adopts it. The historical school (Savigny) makes the same attack.
2. **Rules that confer powers.** Rules on making a contract, a will or a marriage do not command anyone. They give people the power to create legal effects (Hart). They cannot be reduced to commands backed by threats.
3. **The gunman model.** Austin's law is like a gunman's order. Hart said that it explains being *obliged* but not having an *obligation*: people obey law because they accept it as a standard, not only from fear.
4. **Sanction is not the whole of law.** Many laws are obeyed from habit, respect or conviction, and some rules (a statute declaring the age of majority) have no sanction attached.
5. **International law.** Austin denied it the status of law, calling it positive morality, although states treat it as binding and act on it.
6. **Constitutional law.** Austin's sovereign is not under any legal limit, so constitutional restrictions on the legislature become "positive morality". Yet constitutional law is the highest law of a state.
7. **The sovereign is hard to find.** In a democracy, and in a federal state in which power is divided, there is no single determinate person with unlimited power.
8. **Continuity.** Habit of obedience cannot explain why law continues unchanged when a ruler dies or is replaced (Hart's Rex I and Rex II).
9. **The ruler is bound by law.** A legislature is often bound by rules of manner and form, which the theory cannot explain.
10. **Judges and precedent.** Judges make law, but the command model gives them no independent role.
11. **Ignores morality.** It leaves out the part played by justice in the authority of law (Fuller; natural-law writers).

**Merits.** The theory is clear and precise; it separates law from morality, so that one can criticise a bad law; it stresses enforcement; and it fits statute law, which is the main form of modern law. It suited the codifying aims of Bentham and of the colonial Indian codes drafted by Macaulay and others.

**3. Does it apply to the Indian system?**

*(a) Where the theory fits*
1. **Colonial law.** Before 1947 the British Parliament and the Governor-General in Council legislated for India and the courts enforced their orders. The Indian Penal Code (1860), Code of Criminal Procedure and Contract Act (1872) were enacted laws, backed by sanctions. This was close to Austin's model.
2. **Enacted law today.** Most Indian law is statute law. Parliament and the State Legislatures enact laws that bind citizens and are enforced by sanctions (the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 replaced the IPC from 1 July 2024).
3. **Sanction and enforcement.** Courts and the police enforce penal laws and civil decrees by coercion.
4. **Delegated legislation.** Rules and notifications made under a statute are commands by authority derived from the legislature.

*(b) Where it does not fit*
1. **No legally illimitable sovereign.** The Constitution of India is supreme. Parliament and State Legislatures must act within Art. 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule, and every law is subject to the fundamental rights (Art. 13). In *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) the Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to amend does not extend to destroying the basic structure; *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980) held that the limited nature of that power is itself part of the basic structure. Sovereignty belongs to "We, the people" (Preamble), which is not a determinate person. The Austinian sovereign does not exist in India.
2. **Federalism.** Legislative power is divided between the Union and the States, and the Constitution, not any one body, is the source of both.
3. **Judicial review.** Under Arts. 13, 32 and 226 courts strike down laws that conflict with the Constitution. In Austin's theory a court could not question the sovereign's command.
4. **Judge-made law.** Art. 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts. In *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) the Court laid down guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace by using Arts. 14, 15, 19 and 21 and international conventions, and its guidelines operated as law until Parliament passed the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013. This is law-making that does not arise from a sovereign's command.
5. **Custom and personal law.** Art. 13(3)(a) includes "custom or usage having the force of law" in "law". Hindu and Muslim personal laws and tribal customs are applied by courts, and Arts. 371A (Nagaland) and 371G (Mizoram) protect customary law of those States from being overridden by Union Acts unless the State Assembly agrees. Such law is not a command of the Union or State sovereign. In *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) the Supreme Court (3:2) set aside instant triple talaq, a practice of a personal law, showing that custom is subject to the Constitution.
6. **Power-conferring rules.** The law of marriage, contract, wills and the rules for making bye-laws and amendments are enabling rules.
7. **International law.** The State must "foster respect for international law" (Art. 51(c)), Parliament can legislate to implement treaties (Art. 253), and courts read international norms into domestic law where there is no conflict (as in *Vishaka*).
8. **Rule of law and natural justice.** Courts read fairness into Arts. 14 and 21 (*Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India*, 1978), so that even valid statutes must pass tests of reasonableness. Austin's positivism fits the narrow reading in *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950), which has since been left behind.
9. **Constitutional morality.** In *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) the Court preferred constitutional morality to social morality and read down Section 377 IPC; this brings values into the identification of what is protected by law.

**4. Assessment.** The command theory is a useful description of **statute law and its enforcement** and of the colonial legal order. It is not an accurate description of the **Indian constitutional system**, in which (i) legal sovereignty is not placed in any person or body, (ii) all organs act under a written Constitution enforced by independent courts, (iii) judges make law and (iv) custom and personal law have independent force. The Hartian picture, of a system of rules with a rule of recognition (here, the Constitution), is closer. Even so, the Austinian insight that law must be enforced, and the distinction between what law is and what it ought to be, remain valuable.

**Conclusion.** The statement that law is the command of the sovereign captures one important feature of law, namely its source in authority and its backing by force, but it is too narrow as a general theory. For India it applies in part: to enacted law and to the colonial period; it fails to explain a Constitution that binds even Parliament, a judiciary that makes and reviews law, and custom and personal laws that operate alongside statutes.

## C-09 · "Jurisprudence is the philosophy of positive law." (Austin) Discuss

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q10 ("'Jurisprudence is the philosophy of Positive Law.' (Austin) Discuss it."). Related: C-01, C-08, A-08.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning of "philosophy" and "positive law" 4 · Austin's classification of laws 6 · exclusions 3 · criticism 5 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** John Austin, the first Professor of Jurisprudence at the University of London, defined the "province" of jurisprudence in his lectures of 1828–32 published as *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832). The definition quoted in the question describes jurisprudence as the study of law as it is laid down by authority, and not of law as it ought to be. It marks the beginning of the analytical school.

**1. Meaning of the terms**
- **"Philosophy."** In Austin's day the word meant a rational, systematic inquiry into the principles and concepts of a subject. Jurisprudence, so understood, does not describe the details of any one statute; it analyses basic ideas such as law, command, duty, right, sanction, sovereignty and liability.
- **"Positive law."** The word comes from *positum*, "laid down" or "set". It means law that is *set* by a political superior to political inferiors. It is law "simply and strictly so called", as opposed to law by analogy or metaphor.

**2. Austin's classification of laws**
Austin began with the general term "law" (a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him) and divided it:
1. **Laws properly so called (commands):**
   - *Divine law*: the laws set by God to men. Outside jurisprudence.
   - *Human law*: laws set by men to men. These are of two kinds:
     - **Positive law** (law simply and strictly so called): set by political superiors, as such, to political inferiors. This is **the only subject-matter of jurisprudence**.
     - **Positive morality**: set by men who are not political superiors, or not acting as such, for example the rules of a club or society.
2. **Laws improperly so called:**
   - *By analogy*: rules of fashion and honour, and **international law**, which Austin called "positive international morality".
   - *By metaphor*: laws of science, such as the law of gravity.

Austin also noted that some **laws are set by private persons in pursuance of legal rights** (a guardian to a ward) and treated them as positive law, since the right comes from the sovereign.

**3. The command theory underlies the definition.** Positive law is "the command of the sovereign": a command is a wish backed by power to inflict an evil; the sovereign is the determinate human superior who receives habitual obedience and himself obeys no one (see C-08). These ideas give "positive law" its content.

**4. What the definition excludes**
1. **Morality and justice.** The question whether a law is good or bad belongs to the *science of legislation* or ethics. "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another." A law exists even if it conflicts with the standard by which we approve or disapprove it. This is the **separation thesis**.
2. **Natural law and divine law.** Not commands of a human sovereign, and so not positive law.
3. **Custom** until the sovereign adopts it.
4. **International law and constitutional law**, which Austin treated as positive morality.
5. **History and sociology.** Jurisprudence studies law as it is now; how it came about is another study.

**5. Austin's method.** (i) Define the key terms by analysis; (ii) arrange them in a logical system; (iii) deduce consequences; (iv) keep the study separate from ethics. Austin was a follower of Bentham's utilitarianism; he held that the test of *good* law is the principle of utility, but that this belongs to legislation, not jurisprudence.

**6. Merits**
1. **Clarity and precision.** It gave jurisprudence a clear subject and method.
2. **Separation of law and morals.** It permits the criticism of bad law (the same lawyer can say "this is the law" and "this law is unjust"). Hart says this is the key merit of positivism.
3. **Fits statute law.** It suited the nineteenth-century movement of codification.
4. **Focus on enforcement.** It makes law a matter of social fact.

**7. Criticism**
1. **Too narrow.** It leaves out custom, international law, constitutional law and the ethical and historical sides of law. Savigny and Maine, Ehrlich, Pound and the realists all attack it for this.
2. **Defective theory of sovereignty.** The sovereign is hard to find in a federal or democratic State and in a State governed by a written constitution (see C-08).
3. **Not all laws are commands.** Rules conferring powers cannot be explained as commands (Hart).
4. **Neglects the judge.** The law actually applied in courts is not only the sovereign's command (Gray, Holmes, Llewellyn).
5. **Ignores the social basis of law.** Law is also the product of the habits, beliefs and needs of society.
6. **Separation of law and morals is overstated.** The natural-law writers, Fuller and Finnis say that law has an inner morality. After the Nazi period (see the Hart–Fuller debate), many thought the strict separation unsafe.
7. **Positivism and tyranny.** Extreme positivism was used to support the argument that "law is law" (ADM Jabalpur is an Indian example; it was overruled in *Puttaswamy*, 2017).

**8. Indian relevance**
- *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950): "procedure established by law" in Art. 21 meant procedure laid down by an enacted law, and the Court declined to read in natural justice. This is a positivist reading.
- *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) moved away from it: the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.
- *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) held that during an Emergency, with Art. 21 suspended, a detainee had no remedy; a majority took a narrowly positivist view; it was overruled by *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017).
- Indian codification (IPC 1860, now the BNS 2023) reflects the Benthamite and Austinian faith in enacted law.

**9. Later development.** Kelsen (law as a hierarchy of norms) and Hart (law as a union of primary and secondary rules) kept Austin's separation of law from morality but rejected his command-and-sanction model. Contemporary positivism is thus of two kinds: exclusive (Raz) and inclusive (Hart's *Postscript*).

**Conclusion.** Austin's definition limits jurisprudence to law as it is, laid down by the sovereign. Its great merit is clarity, its great defect narrowness. The analytical school developed from it, and later thinkers repaired it while retaining its core: that the existence of law should be separated from its moral merit.

## C-10 · Hans Kelsen's Pure Theory of Law

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q11 ("Describe Hans Kelsen's Pure theory of Law."); Dec 2023 Q13 ("Discuss Kelson's Pure Theory of Law."). Related: Dec 2025 Q7 (B-15, "dry bones"), Dec 2024 Q3 (A-42, Ground Norm), Dec 2023 Q7 (B-14, Austin v. Kelsen), Dec 2025 Q2 (A-41, Kelsen and Austin).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and purity 4 · norms, *is/ought*, hierarchy 5 · Grundnorm 4 · other features 2 · cases 2 · criticism 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** Hans Kelsen (1881–1973), an Austrian jurist, was the principal drafter of the Austrian Constitution of 1920, which created a Constitutional Court. He later taught in Vienna, Geneva, Berkeley and Harvard. His main works are *Reine Rechtslehre* (*Pure Theory of Law*, 1934; second edition 1960) and *General Theory of Law and State* (1945). The Pure Theory, which Kelsen worked out over many years, is the most complete system of analytical positivism.

**1. Purpose: a "pure" science of law.** Kelsen wanted to build a science of law, free from everything that is not strictly law. A theory is "pure" when it excludes:
1. **Politics and ethics** (natural law and ideas of justice);
2. **Sociology and psychology** (the causes and effects of law in society);
3. **Natural science** methods (cause and effect).
The task of legal science is to describe the law in its own terms, that is, in terms of *norms*. It does not say what the law ought to be, and it does not explain how law works in society.

**2. "Is" and "ought".** The natural sciences study what *is* (causal laws: if A, then B follows). Legal science studies what *ought* to be (norms: if A, then B ought to follow). A norm is the meaning of an act of will directed at the behaviour of others. For example: "If a person commits theft, then a sanction ought to be applied to him." The relation between condition and consequence in law is one of *imputation*, not of *causality*.

**3. Law as a coercive order.** Law is a specific technique of social organisation: it attaches sanctions to certain conduct. Its norms are, in the first place, directions to officials to apply sanctions. A man has a legal duty because a sanction is attached to his opposite conduct. A law without sanction is not a legal norm. Austin saw a command addressed to the subject; Kelsen sees a norm addressed to the official.

**4. Hierarchy of norms (*Stufenbau*).** A norm is valid because it was made in the way authorised by a higher norm, not because of its content. Therefore a legal system is a pyramid:
- The **Grundnorm** (basic norm) at the top;
- the **Constitution** (the first historical constitution);
- **statutes and valid custom**;
- **delegated legislation** (rules, regulations, orders);
- **judicial and administrative decisions**;
- **individual legal acts** (contracts, sentences, executions).
Each norm is the creation of a norm above it and the authority for norms below it. This explains the *unity* of the legal system: all norms trace their validity to one source. In India the chain runs from the Constitution to statutes (Parliament and State Legislatures), to delegated legislation, to orders, decrees and contracts, and judicial review under Art. 13 and Arts. 32 and 226 checks each lower norm against the higher.

**5. The Grundnorm**
1. The highest norm, from which all others derive validity. It is *not enacted* but *presupposed* by juristic thought: "the historically first constitution ought to be obeyed" (a transcendental-logical condition, in Kant's sense).
2. **Validity and efficacy.** A norm is valid if it was created in accordance with a higher norm; but the whole system must be by and large effective (obeyed and applied) for the Grundnorm to be presupposed. Efficacy is a condition of validity, not its source.
3. **Revolution.** If a revolution or coup succeeds and the new order becomes effective, a new Grundnorm is presupposed and the new constitution is valid, even though it was not made in the way the old one provided. Kelsen applied this to a revolution in law.
4. In his later work, Kelsen described the Grundnorm as a "fiction" in the sense of Vaihinger's "as if" philosophy, which gave critics a point to attack.

**6. Other features**
1. **State and law are identical.** The State is simply the legal order personified. Kelsen rejects the dualism of State and law and any idea of a sovereign above the law.
2. **Monism.** National and international law form one system. Kelsen preferred the primacy of international law. International law is true law, with reprisals and war as sanctions (a contrast with Austin).
3. **The legal person** is not a natural entity but a point of attribution of rights and duties under norms.
4. **Interpretation.** A norm is a frame within which several meanings are possible; the choice among them by a court is an act of will (political), not of pure knowledge.
5. **Dynamic and static systems.** A legal system is dynamic: norms are valid because of how they were created, not because their content follows logically from a higher norm (as in a moral system).

**7. Kelsen and Austin compared** (in brief; for the 10-mark version see B-14)

| Point | Austin | Kelsen |
|---|---|---|
| Law is | Command of the sovereign | Hierarchy of coercive norms |
| Validity from | Source: the sovereign | Higher norm, ending in the Grundnorm |
| Sovereign | Central, legally unlimited | Rejected; State = legal order |
| International law | Positive morality | True law (monism) |
| Morals | Separated | Excluded ("pure") |

**8. Case law**
- **State v. Dosso** (PLD 1958 SC 533, Pakistan): after the President abrogated the 1956 Constitution and martial law was declared (7 October 1958), Chief Justice Munir used Kelsen's theory to hold that a successful revolution is a law-creating fact, and the new order (including the Laws (Continuance in Force) Order, 1958) was valid because it was effective. The case arose from a murder conviction under the Frontier Crimes Regulation, which the High Court had held unconstitutional.
- **Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab** (PLD 1972 SC 139, 20 April 1972): after the fall of Yahya Khan the Supreme Court (Hamoodur Rahman C.J.) held *Dosso* unsustainable, because Kelsen's theory was not a universally accepted doctrine and had been misapplied to a temporary martial law, and held that a usurper cannot be legitimate by reason of success alone. It applied the doctrine of necessity only to save some acts of the usurper's regime.
- **Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex p. Matovu** ([1966] EA 514): the High Court of Uganda held that the abrogation of the 1962 Constitution in 1966 created a new legal order, and the new Constitution was valid as effective (Kelsen's efficacy).
- **Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke** ([1969] 1 AC 645, PC): the Privy Council refused to treat the regime set up by the 1965 Rhodesian Unilateral Declaration of Independence as lawful.
- **India.** The basic-structure doctrine (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) and judicial review reflect the idea of a hierarchy of norms in which lower norms must conform to the higher. However, the Supreme Court has not called the basic structure a "Grundnorm"; that comparison is academic.

**9. Criticism**
1. **The Grundnorm is vague** or fiction-like; it is hard to identify in an unwritten, federal or evolving constitution.
2. **Efficacy compromises purity.** If validity depends on effectiveness, which is a fact, the theory is not pure after all (Hart's rule of recognition is openly a social fact).
3. **Too formal.** It excludes justice and social reality, so it cannot tell us whether a law is just or why people obey it.
4. **Danger of legitimating coups** (*Dosso*).
5. **Cannot choose between rival regimes** in a contested transition.
6. **Judicial discretion** and the role of **principles** (Dworkin) are under-explained.
7. **Ignores the sociological dimension** (Ehrlich, Pound).
8. **Natural-law critics**: law cannot be separated from justice.

**10. Merits.** The theory explains the **unity and validity** of a legal system; clarifies **delegated legislation, federalism and revolutions**; supports **judicial review** and the constitutional-court model (Austria 1920, later followed in Germany, Italy and elsewhere); and treats **international law** as law. It influenced Hart and, more broadly, modern constitutional theory.

**Conclusion.** Kelsen's Pure Theory sees law as a hierarchical order of coercive norms deriving validity from one presupposed basic norm. Its strength is logical clarity and its weakness is that, by leaving out morality and social facts, it cannot answer whether an effective regime has the right to be obeyed. The reply of the courts in *Asma Jilani* shows the limit of purity.

## C-11 · H.L.A. Hart's *The Concept of Law*: primary and secondary rules, the rule of recognition and Hart's debates

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Hart's concept of law"). Related: Dec 2024 Q8 (B-16, "rules are concerned not with what happens but with what is to be done").
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** attack on Austin 4 · primary and secondary rules 6 · rule of recognition and internal point of view 4 · open texture, minimum content 3 · debates and criticism 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** H.L.A. Hart (1907–92), Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford (1952–68), published *The Concept of Law* in 1961 (second edition 1994 with a *Postscript*). He rebuilt analytical positivism after criticising Austin. He also drew on ordinary-language philosophy (Wittgenstein, J.L. Austin). His main aim was to explain how law differs from orders backed by threats and why rules, not habits, are at the centre of law.

**1. Hart's attack on Austin**
1. **The gunman situation.** A gunman who orders a bank clerk to hand over the money *obliges* him but does not give him an *obligation*. Austin's law is like the gunman's order. It cannot explain why people feel bound.
2. **Not all laws are orders.** Rules that confer powers (making contracts, wills, marriages, statutes) are not orders.
3. **Continuity.** Habits of obedience to Rex I cannot explain why Rex II has authority on Rex I's death; law persists beyond the ruler.
4. **The sovereign is bound by law.** Legislatures act under rules of "manner and form".
5. **Custom and international law** are not accounted for.
6. **Habit v. rule.** A habit is convergent behaviour. A rule has an *internal aspect*: those who follow it regard it as a standard and criticise deviation from it.

**2. Primary and secondary rules**
A simple, pre-legal society has only **primary rules**: rules that impose duties to do or abstain from acts (the rules against murder, theft and violence). Such a regime has three defects, and each is cured by a **secondary rule**:

| Defect | Cure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Uncertainty**: no way to settle which rules count | **Rule of recognition** | Identifies the authoritative rules of the system |
| **Static character**: no way to change rules | **Rule of change** | Empowers persons or bodies to make, amend or repeal rules |
| **Inefficiency**: no authoritative decision on breach | **Rule of adjudication** | Empowers courts to decide breaches and apply sanctions |

The union of primary and secondary rules is, Hart says, "the key to the science of jurisprudence". Primary rules impose duties; secondary rules confer powers and concern the primary rules themselves.

**3. The rule of recognition**
1. It is the ultimate criterion of validity in a legal system. In the United Kingdom it can be put as "what the Queen in Parliament enacts is law"; in India, one can say that the rule of recognition is found in the Constitution, as accepted and applied by courts and officials. (Hart himself spoke of the United Kingdom; the Indian statement is an application.)
2. It is a **social rule**. It exists as a matter of fact, shown by the practice of officials (judges, administrators) who accept it from the internal point of view. We ask not whether it is "valid" but whether it *exists*.
3. **Contrast with Kelsen's Grundnorm.** The Grundnorm is a presupposition; the rule of recognition is an empirical social fact.

**4. Internal and external points of view.** The *external* observer records regularities and threats (what Austin did). The *internal* participant accepts the rule as a standard for his own conduct and for criticising others. Law cannot be understood without the internal point of view. Hart says a legal system exists when (i) most citizens obey the primary rules, and (ii) officials accept the secondary rules, especially the rule of recognition, from the internal point of view.

**5. Open texture and discretion.** Every general rule has a core of settled meaning and a penumbra of doubt. Hart's example is "No vehicles in the park": a car is clearly covered, but a bicycle, roller-skates or a toy car may not be (Fuller, replying to Hart, asked about an army lorry mounted on a plinth as a war memorial). In the penumbra the judge exercises discretion, so that he makes law within limits. This is Hart's middle way between **formalism** (the judge only applies rules) and **rule-scepticism** (the realists: law is only what judges do). In a later essay (1977) he described two extremes to be avoided: the "nightmare" (judges simply make law as they please) and the "noble dream" (judges only discover and apply law that is already complete).

**6. Minimum content of natural law.** Hart accepts that, given five facts about human beings (vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, and limited understanding and strength of will), any workable legal system will contain basic rules on violence, property and promises. This is a modest concession to natural law, which leaves the separation of law and morals untouched.

**7. Law and morals; the debates**
1. **Hart–Fuller (1958, 71 Harvard Law Review 593 and 630).** Hart wrote "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals"; Fuller replied. The test case was the Nazi *grudge informer* case, in which a woman who denounced her husband for insulting Hitler was prosecuted after the war. Hart said the Nazi statute was law but too evil to obey, and that it is better to say openly that a law is bad than to pretend it is not law. Fuller said a system that fails the "inner morality of law" (generality, promulgation, clarity, consistency etc.) lacks legality.
2. **Hart–Devlin (1957–63).** After the Wolfenden Report (1957), Lord Devlin argued that society may use the criminal law to protect its shared morality. Hart, following J.S. Mill's harm principle, said private immorality is not the law's concern. In India, *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) preferred constitutional morality to social morality in decriminalising consensual same-sex acts between adults.
3. **Hart–Dworkin.** Dworkin (*Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977) said judges apply not only rules but also principles (as in *Riggs v. Palmer*, 1889, where a murderer was barred from inheriting under his victim's will), and have no strong discretion. Hart's *Postscript* (1994) defended "soft" or inclusive positivism: the rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria.

**8. "Rules are concerned not with what happens but with what is to be done."** This (Dec 2024 Q8; see B-16) distinguishes **rules** from **predictions**. For Hart, a rule is a standard of conduct, an "ought", not a prediction of what will happen. This is how he answers the realists (such as Holmes, who defined law as a prediction of what courts will do). A prediction of what a judge will do is the viewpoint of an outside observer; a rule is used by the participant to justify and criticise.

**9. Criticism**
1. **Dworkin:** the model of rules ignores principles and policies; judges do not have strong discretion.
2. **Natural-law writers (Fuller, Finnis):** the strict separation of law and morals is false.
3. **Rule of recognition** may be vague in a complex or divided system; critics ask whether there is a single rule.
4. **Raz:** Hart concedes too much to morality (the sources thesis: legal validity depends on social sources only).
5. **Critical and Marxist writers:** ignores power and class interests.
6. **Realists:** exaggerates the extent to which rules guide courts.
7. **Sanction:** Hart treats coercion as a natural necessity, but critics say it is more central to law than he allows.

**10. Merits.** Gave positivism a realistic base; explained obligation and validity; reconciled rules with discretion; remains the leading positivist account of law.

**11. Indian illustration.** The Indian courts and officials accept the Constitution as the ultimate source of validity (rule of recognition). Parliament, the amending power (Art. 368) and the courts (Art. 141) give examples of the rules of change and adjudication. Open texture appears in how "reasonable restrictions" (Art. 19), "procedure established by law" (Art. 21) and "equal protection" (Art. 14) are applied. In the penumbra, the Supreme Court has made new law (*Maneka Gandhi*, *Vishaka*, *Puttaswamy*).

**Conclusion.** Hart replaced the command-and-habit model with a model of rules, distinguished obligation from coercion, and found in the union of primary and secondary rules the key to a legal system. His theory is the best modern defence of the view that the existence of law and its moral merit are different questions, but it faces Dworkin's challenge that law includes principles and Fuller's argument that law has an inner morality.

## C-12 · Salient features of the analytical school; distinguish between the analytical and historical schools

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q13 ("Describe the salient features of analytical school of Jurisprudence. Distinguish between analytical and historical school of Jurisprudence."). Related: C-13 (Savigny), C-14 (Maine), Dec 2024 Q9 (C-20).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** origin and thinkers 4 · salient features 7 · distinction (table plus explanation) 6 · criticism and conclusion 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** The analytical school, also called the *positivist* or *imperative* school, treats jurisprudence as the analysis of law as it is. It began with Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) and John Austin (1790–1859), was carried on in England by Holland, Markby and Salmond, and in the twentieth century by Hans Kelsen (the Pure Theory) and H.L.A. Hart (*The Concept of Law*). Its great rival in the nineteenth century was the historical school of Savigny and Maine, which saw law as a growth of society.

**1. Background.** The school arose when (i) the nation-State with a strong legislature had come into being; (ii) codification was a major political aim (Bentham argued for codes); (iii) the natural-law idea of fixed rights had been discredited by Bentham's criticism ("nonsense upon stilts"); and (iv) the English law of the time was confused and in need of clear definition.

**2. Salient features**
1. **Law is the command of the sovereign** (Austin), or a hierarchy of norms (Kelsen), or a union of primary and secondary rules (Hart). In each form law is a human creation (*positum*).
2. **Law as it is, not as it ought to be.** The existence of law is one thing, its merit another. Jurisprudence studies the former.
3. **Separation of law and morals.** Law is distinguished from ethics, religion and justice.
4. **Analysis of legal concepts.** Its method is the logical analysis of basic notions: law, right, duty, ownership, possession, liability, sanction.
5. **Sovereign and sanction.** In Austin's form, law depends on a determinate sovereign and is enforced by sanctions.
6. **Legislation is the chief source of law.** Custom and precedent are law only when the sovereign accepts them.
7. **Logical, deductive and closed system.** The system of law is logically complete and judges find answers by deduction (a tendency later called "mechanical jurisprudence").
8. **Universal concepts.** A general jurisprudence of ideas common to all mature systems (Austin's "general jurisprudence").
9. **Exclusion of history, sociology and ethics.** They are different studies.
10. **Codification** is favoured as a way of making law certain.
11. **Later forms:** Kelsen's pure theory (norms and the Grundnorm) and Hart's soft positivism (rule of recognition, internal point of view).

**3. Contributions**
1. Precision of legal language and concepts.
2. A rational basis for codification and legislative reform (Indian codes, Macaulay).
3. The separation of law from morals, which allows moral criticism of law.
4. A model for statute-centred modern legal systems.
5. Kelsen's idea of a hierarchy of norms underlies constitutional courts; Hart's theory explains obligation and officials' practice.

**4. Criticism**
1. It is too narrow: it ignores custom, international law, and the social and historical origins of law.
2. The command theory does not fit power-conferring rules, federal states or constitutional limits.
3. It disregards the influence of morality and public opinion on law.
4. The deductive method gives an unreal picture of how judges decide (realists).
5. Obedience is not explained by sanctions alone.
6. Extreme positivism can support unjust regimes ("law is law").

**5. The historical school in outline.** The historical school was led in Germany by **Friedrich Carl von Savigny** (1779–1861; *Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft*, 1814; *System des heutigen römischen Rechts*) and in England by **Sir Henry Maine** (1822–88; *Ancient Law*, 1861; *Village Communities in the East and West*, 1871; *Early History of Institutions*, 1875). It holds that law is not made by a sovereign but is found in the life of a people and grows with it. Savigny's key idea is the *Volksgeist* (spirit of the people); Maine's is the study of the stages of legal growth and the movement "from status to contract". (See C-13, C-14.)

**6. Analytical and historical schools distinguished**

| Point | Analytical school | Historical school |
|---|---|---|
| Chief thinkers | Bentham, Austin, Holland, Kelsen, Hart | Savigny, Puchta, Maine |
| Origin of law | The will of the sovereign (Austin); a norm created under a higher norm (Kelsen) | The *Volksgeist*; custom and the common consciousness of the people |
| Nature of law | Command, norm, or rule; artificial and made | Organic growth; found, not made |
| Method | Logical analysis of existing law | Historical study of origin and development |
| Subject | Law as it is (positive law) | Law as it has become |
| Place of custom | Law only if adopted by the sovereign | The primary and truest form of law |
| Place of legislation | Chief source and instrument of reform | Secondary; legislation should only record custom (Savigny opposed hasty codification) |
| Role of the State | Source of law and its sanction | Law is prior to the State |
| Universal or local | Universal concepts for mature systems | Law is peculiar to each people; no universal law |
| Attitude to reform | Favours codification and rational reform | Cautious about legislative change |
| Law and morals | Separated | Law and the national spirit are linked |
| Weakness | Narrow; ignores history and society | Vague *Volksgeist*; ignores deliberate law-making and foreign borrowing |

**7. Relation between the two.** The schools are complementary. The analytical school explains what law *is* and how it is built; the historical school explains how it *became* what it is. Maine's work in India shows the historical method at its best (village communities, joint family, custom); Austin's and Macaulay's work shows the analytical method in practice (the Indian codes). The sociological and realist schools later drew on both.

**8. Indian illustration.** The Indian codes of the nineteenth century (Penal Code, Contract Act) and their replacements (BNS, BNSS and BSA, 2023) express the analytical attitude. The recognition of custom in Hindu and tribal law, and the courts' use of long usage to interpret statutes, express the historical attitude. Art. 13(3)(a) of the Constitution accepts both, by including "custom or usage having the force of law" in "law".

**Conclusion.** The analytical school made jurisprudence a precise study of law as it is, and the historical school showed law as a product of the life of a people. Each corrects the other, and modern jurisprudence uses both.
