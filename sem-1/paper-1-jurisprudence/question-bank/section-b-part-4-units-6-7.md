## B-38 · "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the Law." Discuss

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q8 (same words). Related: C-19, A-88, A-89, A-90.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** source and meaning 3 · explanation 3 · criticism 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

The statement is from Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (1841–1935), in *The Path of the Law* (10 Harvard Law Review 457, 1897). It is the best-known statement of the realist view that law is what courts actually do.

**Meaning.** To understand law, look at it as a "bad man" would, one who cares nothing for ethics but wants to know what the public force will do to him. Law is therefore a prediction of how the courts will decide, not a set of abstract rules or moral principles.

**Merits.** It turns attention to actual practice; separates law from morals for analysis; and recognises the part played by judges and public policy.

**Criticism**
1. It is circular: a judge cannot predict his own decision, and must decide by some rule (Hart).
2. It gives no help to the judge, and reduces law to the point of view of an outsider (the internal point of view is neglected).
3. It ignores legislation and constitutional text.
4. It overstates uncertainty.

**India.** Judge-made law, such as the basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973), illustrates it, but Art. 141 makes the Court's declaration of law binding, not a prophecy.

## B-39 · John Chipman Gray: law as what the courts lay down

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("American Modern Realism"). Related: C-19, A-92.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** work and thesis 2 · statutes as sources 3 · judge-made law 2 · criticism 3

### Answer

John Chipman Gray (1839–1915), a Harvard professor, is a forerunner of American realism. His book is *The Nature and Sources of the Law* (1909).

**Thesis.** The law of a State consists of the rules which the courts lay down for the determination of legal rights and duties. What the courts apply is law, and nothing else is.

**Statutes.** A statute is not itself law; it is a *source* of law, like precedent, custom, juristic opinion and principles of morality. A statute becomes law only when a court interprets and applies it. Gray approved the remark of Bishop Hoadly that whoever has absolute authority to interpret a law is the true lawgiver, not the person who first wrote it.

**Judge-made law.** All law is judge-made, since even where a rule exists the judge's reading gives it effect. Judges find and make law; they do not merely declare it (a rejection of the Blackstonian declaratory theory).

**Comparison.** For Austin the sovereign is the legislator; for Gray, the judge.

**Criticism.** It ignores the legislature's primary role; it is circular (what rules do the judges follow?); and it neglects the social setting of law.

*Word count: 190*

## B-40 · Llewellyn and Frank: rule-scepticism and fact-scepticism

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("American Modern Realism"). Related: C-19, A-93, A-94, A-95.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Llewellyn 4 · Frank 4 · difference and evaluation 2

### Answer

**Karl Llewellyn** (1893–1962), Columbia professor and chief reporter of the Uniform Commercial Code. Works: *The Bramble Bush* (1930), *Some Realism About Realism* (1931), *The Cheyenne Way* (1941), *The Common Law Tradition* (1960).
1. Law is what officials do about disputes.
2. *Paper rules* (what books say) differ from *real rules* (what courts do).
3. **Rule-scepticism:** rules do not by themselves decide cases.
4. **Law-jobs:** every group needs law to clear up trouble, prevent trouble, allocate authority, give direction, and provide juristic method.
5. Later, the *Grand Style* and "situation sense": judges reason openly from purpose; and he admitted that rules do constrain judges.

**Jerome Frank** (1889–1957), Judge of the Second Circuit. Works: *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930), *Courts on Trial* (1949).
1. **Fact-scepticism:** the real uncertainty is at the trial, in finding facts: witnesses err, juries are unpredictable, and the judge's personality and "hunch" affect the result.
2. Certainty in law is a myth, a childish wish for a father-figure.
3. *Actual law* is a past decision; *probable law* is a guess.

**Difference.** Llewellyn doubts the rules; Frank doubts the facts.

**Evaluation.** Both exaggerate uncertainty, but they exposed judicial discretion and the importance of trial.

*Word count: 196*

## B-41 · Features of American realism

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("American Modern Realism"). Related: C-19, C-20, A-96.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 3 · features 5 · school or movement 2

### Answer

American realism flourished from about 1900 to 1940. It arose as a reaction to formalism or "mechanical jurisprudence", which treated law as a closed logical system (for example, in *Lochner v. New York*, 1905), and was influenced by pragmatism (James, Dewey), Pound's sociological jurisprudence, Holmes and the social upheaval of the New Deal.

**Features** (Llewellyn, *Some Realism About Realism*, 1931)
1. Law is in flux, and judicial creation of law is a constant fact.
2. Law is a means to social ends, not an end in itself.
3. Society changes faster than law.
4. A temporary separation of "is" and "ought" for study.
5. Distrust of traditional rules as descriptions of what courts actually do (rule-scepticism).
6. Evaluation of law by its effects.
7. Focus on judicial behaviour, including personality and background.
8. Empirical, functional method; law and the social sciences.
9. Anti-formalism: logic alone does not decide cases.

**A movement, not a school.** Llewellyn said that realists shared a method and attitude, not a doctrine. Its centres were Columbia and Yale.

**Related movement.** Scandinavian realism (Hägerström, Olivecrona, Lundstedt, Ross) is psychological and rejects metaphysical ideas such as "right" and "duty".

## B-42 · American realism: criticism and contribution

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("American Modern Realism"). Related: C-19, A-98.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** criticism 5 · Hart's critique 2 · contribution 3

### Answer

**Criticism**
1. **Overstates uncertainty.** Most disputes are routine; rules do constrain judges.
2. **Law reduced to prediction.** It is useless to the judge and to the citizen who needs to know what the law requires.
3. **Circular.** "Law is what judges decide" does not say on what basis they decide.
4. **Neglects legislation** and constitutional text.
5. **Threat to the rule of law,** by stressing judges' personality over rules.
6. **No normative guidance,** since it splits "is" from "ought".
7. **Weak empirical basis;** Frank's psychoanalytic claims were speculative.
8. **Internal differences:** Llewellyn (rule-sceptic) and Frank (fact-sceptic).

**Hart's critique** (*Concept of Law*, ch. VII): most cases are decided under clear rules; the prediction theory ignores the internal point of view; and courts need a rule of recognition. Hart's open texture is a middle path.

**Contribution**
1. It exposed judicial discretion, policy and social context.
2. It made law purposive and effects-oriented.
3. It encouraged empirical legal research and changed legal education.
4. It shaped New Deal jurisprudence and the Uniform Commercial Code.
5. It was a forerunner of law and economics, and of Critical Legal Studies.

## B-43 · Realism and the Indian judiciary: judges as law-makers

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("American Modern Realism"). Related: C-19, A-99.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** thesis 2 · examples 6 · caution 2

### Answer

The central realist claim, that judges make law, is plainly illustrated by Indian practice. Indian courts do not call themselves realist; the point is that their practice supports the claim.

**Examples**
1. **Constitutional basis.** Art. 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts; Art. 142 permits orders necessary for "complete justice".
2. **Basic structure.** *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) created a limit on the amending power that is not in the text.
3. **Art. 21.** Expanded from *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) to include livelihood, privacy, dignity and a clean environment.
4. **Gap-filling.** *Vishaka* (1997): guidelines on sexual harassment, which operated as law until the 2013 Act.
5. **Absolute liability.** *M.C. Mehta* (1987).
6. **Collegium.** Created by interpretation of "consultation" (*Second Judges Case*, 1993; *Third Judges Case*, 1998); the NJAC was struck down in the *Fourth Judges Case* (2015).
7. **Prospective overruling.** Adopted in *Golaknath* (1967), borrowed from American practice.

**Debate.** The extension of judicial law-making has raised the debate between judicial activism and judicial overreach and the separation of powers.

**Caution.** The examples illustrate a realist claim; they are not a formal adoption of realism.

*Word count: 187*

## B-44 · Marx's economic approach to law: base and superstructure

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q1 (A, "Write the economic definition of Law given by Karl Marx."); Dec 2022 Q11 (C, "economic approach to law"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-101, A-102. Related: C-21.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 2 · materialism and stages 2 · base and superstructure 4 · evaluation 2

### Answer

Karl Marx (1818–83) and Friedrich Engels (1820–95) did not write a systematic theory of law; it is reconstructed from the *Communist Manifesto* (1848), *The German Ideology* (1845–46), the Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859) and Engels' later writings.

**Definition.** In the *Manifesto*, addressing the bourgeoisie: "Your jurisprudence is but the will of your class made into a law for all, a will whose essential character and direction are determined by the economic conditions of existence of your class."

**Materialism.** History is driven by material conditions: the mode of production. Societies pass from primitive communism, through slave society, feudalism and capitalism, to socialism and communism. Each stage ends in class conflict.

**Base and superstructure.** The economic structure (forces and relations of production) is the *base*. On it rises the *superstructure*: law, the State, politics, religion, morality and ideology. Law reflects and protects the base; when the base changes, law changes.

**Engels' qualification (1890).** The economy is decisive only "in the last instance"; the superstructure has relative autonomy and reacts on the base.

**Evaluation.** The theory exposes the link between law and economic power, but is criticised as deterministic.

## B-45 · Law as an instrument of class rule and its withering away (Marxist view)

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Marxist Economic Approach"). Related: C-21, A-103, A-104.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** class instrument 4 · transition 3 · withering away 3

### Answer

**Law as class instrument**
1. Law is the will of the ruling class, made binding on all.
2. The State is the machinery of that class (in the *Manifesto*'s phrase, a committee for managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie).
3. Property, contract and criminal law protect the owners of the means of production; private property and exchange appear as "natural" rights.
4. Formal equality hides real inequality: a worker is free to contract, but must sell his labour to live.
5. Law is also ideology: it presents class rule as neutral justice and so wins consent.

**Transition.** After revolution the proletariat uses the State and law against the old ruling class (the dictatorship of the proletariat). Even then a "bourgeois right" remains: an equal standard is applied to unequal people (*Critique of the Gotha Programme*, 1875).

**Withering away.** In higher communism there are no classes and no need for coercion, so the State withers away (Engels, *Anti-Dühring*, 1878) and law with it, replaced by the administration of things. The principle is "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs".

**Note.** The prediction has not come true in any State.

*Word count: 194*

## B-46 · Criticism of the Marxist theory of law

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Marxist Economic Approach"). Related: C-21, C-22, A-108.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** criticism 6 · merits 3 · India 1

### Answer

**Criticism**
1. **Economic determinism.** Law is shaped also by religion, culture, politics and morality, and itself shapes the economy (property, contract, company law).
2. **Law is not only a class tool.** Labour, factory, welfare and consumer laws protect the weak, and the rule of law restrains rulers (E.P. Thompson, *Whigs and Hunters*, 1975).
3. **Failed prediction.** The State and law did not wither away in socialist States; they expanded.
4. **Ignores universal functions** of law: criminal law, family law, procedure.
5. **Class is not the only source of conflict;** caste, gender, race and religion matter, especially in India.
6. **Unfalsifiable:** "the will of the ruling class" can explain any law.
7. **Rights have value** in protecting individuals against the State.

**Merits**
1. It exposes economic inequality behind formal equality.
2. It shows the link between law and power, and the ideological role of law.
3. It influenced labour and welfare legislation, the sociology of law and Critical Legal Studies.

**India.** The Constitution is not Marxist: socialism in the Preamble means economic and social justice and does not bar private enterprise (*Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India*, 25 November 2024).

## B-47 · Law and economics: Coase, Calabresi and Posner

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Economic Approach of Law"). Related: C-22, A-109, A-110.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · three writers 5 · criticism 3

### Answer

**Meaning.** Law and economics (the Chicago school) applies economic reasoning (scarcity, cost, incentives, efficiency) to legal rules, asking what effect rules have and whether they are efficient.

1. **Ronald Coase**, "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960). The **Coase theorem**: if transaction costs are zero, parties bargain to an efficient outcome whatever the initial legal rule. Where costs are high, the law should assign rights to the party who can avoid harm most cheaply.
2. **Guido Calabresi**, *The Costs of Accidents* (1970). Accident law should reduce the sum of the costs of accidents and of avoiding them; liability falls on the "cheapest cost avoider".
3. **Richard Posner**, *Economic Analysis of Law* (1973). The common law tends to efficiency; the goal of law is wealth maximisation. The **Hand formula** (*United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 1947) explains negligence: liable if B < P × L (burden of precaution less than probability times loss).

**Criticism**
1. Efficiency is not justice; distribution is ignored (Dworkin).
2. People are not always rational.
3. Transaction costs are never zero.
4. Values like dignity cannot be priced.

**Contrast with Marx.** Marx asks whose interests law serves; law and economics asks whether it is efficient.

*Word count: 195*

## B-48 · Lenin and Pashukanis: the later Marxist theory of State and law

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Marxist Economic Approach"). Related: C-21, A-106, A-107.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Lenin 3 · Pashukanis 4 · others and evaluation 3

### Answer

**Lenin** (1870–1924), in *The State and Revolution* (1917), held that the State is a machine of class oppression. The bourgeois State must be smashed by revolution and replaced by a proletarian State, which uses law to suppress the former ruling class and will itself wither away as classes disappear.

**Evgeny Pashukanis** (1891–1937), in *The General Theory of Law and Marxism* (1924), developed the **commodity-exchange theory**. The legal form, that is, equal legal subjects holding rights and making contracts, arises from the exchange of commodities in capitalist society. Law is therefore a bourgeois form, and when commodity exchange disappears under communism, law will wither away. He was purged and executed in 1937.

**Others**
1. **Vyshinsky:** law is the will of the ruling class expressed in legislation; "socialist legality" serves the Party-State.
2. **Gramsci:** hegemony; the ruling class rules also by consent, through culture and law.
3. **Althusser:** law as part of the ideological State apparatus.

**Evaluation.** Pashukanis explains the link between legal form and market exchange, but his view that law will wither away was not borne out, and the socialist States kept and used law.

*Word count: 185*
