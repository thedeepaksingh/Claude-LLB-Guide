## C-01 · How would you define jurisprudence? Which definition is the most appropriate?

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q12 ("How would you define Jurisprudence? Which definition according to you would be the most appropriate one?"). Related: Dec 2023 Q11 (C-02).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and difficulty of defining 3 · classical and analytical definitions 5 · modern definitions 5 · criticism of the definitions 3 · your own choice with reasons 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** The word *jurisprudence* comes from the Latin *juris* (of law) and *prudentia* (knowledge or skill), so it means knowledge of, or skill in, law. There is no single agreed definition. Each writer defines it according to the school he belongs to: a natural-law writer stresses justice, a positivist stresses the command of the sovereign, a sociologist stresses the effect of law on society. A good answer therefore gives the main definitions, criticises them, and then chooses one with reasons.

**1. The Roman definition.** *Ulpian* (died 223), in the Digest (1.1.10.2): "jurisprudence is the knowledge of things divine and human, the science of the just and the unjust". It is the oldest definition. It is too wide, because it joins law with religion and ethics and does not separate what law *is* from what it *ought to be*.

**2. Analytical definitions**
- **John Austin** (*The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, 1832): jurisprudence is "the philosophy of positive law", that is, of laws "properly so called", which are commands of a sovereign to subjects, backed by sanctions. The definition is precise, but it is too narrow. It leaves out custom, international law, constitutional law and the ethical side of law.
- **T.E. Holland** (*Elements of Jurisprudence*, 1880): "the formal science of positive law". It studies the relations that law governs and the concepts of law, not its content or its justice. "Formal" is the weak word: the content of law cannot be ignored.
- **Sir John Salmond** (*Jurisprudence*, 1902): "the science of the first principles of the civil law", where "civil law" means the law of the land administered by the courts. Salmond also treats jurisprudence in a wider sense as including legal exposition, legal history and the science of legislation. The weakness is the ambiguity of "civil law" and "first principles".
- **G.W. Keeton**: "the study and systematic arrangement of the general principles of law". This is simple and neutral, but it does not say what principles or what method.

**3. Realist and American definitions**
- **John Chipman Gray**: the science of law, that is, "the statement and systematic arrangement of the rules followed by the courts and the principles involved in those rules". It is court-centred. It leaves out the social setting, and Julius Stone criticised it for reducing jurisprudence to mere arrangement of rules.
- **Roscoe Pound**: "the science of law", using "law" in the juridical sense of the body of principles recognised or enforced by public and regular tribunals in the administration of justice. Pound also treated law as a tool of social engineering, so his jurisprudence has a social purpose.

**4. Modern and sociological definitions**
- **Julius Stone**: jurisprudence is "the lawyer's extroversion", that is, the lawyer's examination of the precepts, ideals and techniques of law in the light of knowledge drawn from outside law. It gives jurisprudence its interdisciplinary character.
- **R.W.M. Dias**: jurisprudence is legal theory, the study of the nature of law and of the ideas lawyers use. This is the name used in the CCSU syllabus.
- **W. Friedmann** (*Legal Theory*): legal theory reflects the changing role of law in society.
- **Jerome Hall** (*integrative jurisprudence*, 1958): jurisprudence must unite three elements: law as form (rules), law as value (justice) and law as fact (social behaviour).

**5. The Indian tradition.** In the *Dharmashastra* (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada), law is part of *dharma*: it is the whole system of duty, custom, royal command (*rajashasana*) and adjudication (*vyavahara*). Kautilya's *Arthashastra* names four sources of law: *dharma*, *vyavahara*, *charitra* (custom) and royal edict. The Indian view is closer to Ulpian than to Austin, since it does not separate law from morality.

**6. Evaluation: what makes a good definition?** A good definition should (i) be neither too wide nor too narrow; (ii) cover what the subject actually studies today, namely the nature and sources of law, legal concepts, justice and the social working of law; (iii) be neutral between the schools; and (iv) be easy to apply. Judged by these tests:

| Definition | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Ulpian | Covers law, justice and ethics | Too wide; no separation of law and morals |
| Austin | Clear subject: positive law | Too narrow; ignores custom, ethics, society |
| Holland | Stresses structure and concepts | "Formal" excludes content and purpose |
| Salmond | Combines exposition, history and ethics | "Civil law" and "first principles" are vague |
| Gray | Realistic; focuses on what courts do | Ignores legislation and social setting |
| Pound | Adds social purpose | Stresses one function (engineering) |
| Stone | Shows its outward-looking character | Says what jurisprudence does, not what it studies |

**7. My choice.** No single classical definition is complete. The most appropriate is **Salmond's** as the starting point, because it states the field (the first principles of the legal system) and, in its wider form, joins analysis, history and ethics. But it should be read together with **Stone's** idea that jurisprudence looks outward to other disciplines, and with **Hall's** insistence that law is at once rule, value and fact. A working definition that I would offer is:

> *Jurisprudence is the systematic, theoretical and critical study of law: its nature, sources and concepts, the ends it should serve, its history and its working in society.*

The reasons are: it includes the analytical, historical, ethical and sociological branches; it does not exclude custom or international law; it does not assume that law is only a command; and it fits the title of the paper, "Legal Theory".

**Conclusion.** The variety of definitions shows that jurisprudence is a developing subject, not a fixed list of rules. Each definition captures one side of law: Ulpian its ethics, Austin its authority, Holland its form, Salmond its principles, Gray its judicial practice, Pound its social purpose and Stone its connection with other disciplines. A modern definition must hold these together, and that is why a composite definition is the most appropriate.

## C-02 · Definition, nature, scope and utility of jurisprudence

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q11 ("Discuss the definition, nature, scope and the utility of jurisprudence."). Related: Dec 2025 Q6 (B, need for the study of jurisprudence), Dec 2023 Q8 and Dec 2022 Q7 (B, "eye of law").
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** definition 4 · nature 4 · scope 4 · utility 6 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** *Jurisprudence* is derived from the Latin *juris prudentia*, "knowledge of law". It is the study of law at the level of general principles. It does not describe the rules of one statute; it asks what law is, why it binds, where it comes from, what it is for and how its basic ideas (right, duty, liability, ownership, possession, personality) work.

**1. Definition**
- *Ulpian*: knowledge of things divine and human; the science of the just and the unjust.
- *Austin*: the philosophy of positive law.
- *Salmond*: the science of the first principles of the civil law.
- *Holland*: the formal science of positive law.
- *Gray*: the statement and systematic arrangement of the rules followed by the courts and the principles behind them.
- *Pound*: the science of law, in the sense of the principles recognised and enforced by courts.
- *Keeton*: the study and systematic arrangement of the general principles of law.
- *Stone*: the lawyer's extroversion; *Dias*: legal theory.
In substance, jurisprudence is the theoretical, systematic and critical study of law. (For a detailed comparison see C-01.)

**2. Nature**
1. **Theoretical and general.** It studies general ideas and principles common to legal systems, not the details of one law. Austin called the general study "general jurisprudence".
2. **A social science.** It is organised knowledge, but it is not an experimental science; its conclusions are tested by reasoning, history and observation of society, and they are contested between schools.
3. **Both descriptive and normative.** Analytical jurisprudence describes law as it is; ethical or philosophical jurisprudence asks what it ought to be; sociological jurisprudence studies law at work.
4. **Dynamic.** Its theories follow the needs of the age: natural law in the age of religion and reason; positivism with the rise of the sovereign State; the historical school with nationalism; the sociological school with industrial society; realism with judicial law-making; Marxism with class conflict.
5. **Interdisciplinary.** It draws on philosophy, ethics, history, sociology, economics, politics and psychology. This is Stone's "lawyer's extroversion".
6. **Different from the study of a particular law.** Contract, tort and crime study rules of a subject; jurisprudence studies the concepts behind all of them. It is often called the "grammar" or "philosophy" of law, and, in a phrase commonly attributed to Harold Laski, "the eye of law".

**3. Scope**
Jurisprudence has no fixed boundary. Its principal heads are:
1. The **nature, definition and purpose of law**, and the relation of law to morality, justice and sovereignty.
2. The **sources of law**: legislation, precedent, custom, equity, juristic writing.
3. **Fundamental legal concepts**: rights and duties, ownership and possession, personality, liability, obligation, property, title.
4. **Justice and the administration of justice**, including theories of punishment.
5. **Legal reasoning and interpretation**.
6. The **schools of thought**: natural law, analytical, historical, sociological, realist and economic.
Different writers divide the subject differently:
- *Salmond*: legal exposition (analytical), legal history (historical) and the science of legislation (ethical).
- *Bentham*: expositorial (what the law is) and censorial (what it ought to be, tested by utility).
- *Austin*: general jurisprudence (principles common to mature legal systems) and particular jurisprudence (the science of one actual system).
**Relation to other subjects.** With *sociology* (law is a social institution; Ehrlich's living law); *ethics* (law is backed by the State and governs external conduct, morality rests on conscience; positivists separate them, natural-law thinkers join them); *history* (Savigny, Maine); *political science* (State, sovereignty, constitution); *economics* (Marx; law and economics); *psychology* (intention, motive, judicial behaviour; Jerome Frank); *anthropology* (early law and custom).

**4. Utility (importance of the study)**
1. **Foundation of legal study.** The concepts used in contract, tort, crime and constitutional law are explained by jurisprudence.
2. **Training in legal reasoning.** It teaches the student to analyse concepts and to argue from principle.
3. **Interpretation of statutes and the Constitution.** The choice between literal and purposive readings is a jurisprudential choice.
4. **Guidance for judges.** Indian courts use jurisprudential ideas openly. *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950) read "procedure established by law" narrowly (a positivist reading); *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) required the procedure to be fair, just and reasonable (a natural-law and rights-based reading). *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) held that Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure. *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) held that life and liberty are natural rights that existed before the Constitution and overruled *ADM Jabalpur* (1976). *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) shows a court filling a gap by looking to the social purpose of law.
5. **Law reform.** Codification (Bentham's idea), the Law Commission's reports and the replacement of the Indian Penal Code by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 rest on theories about what law should be.
6. **Comparison of legal systems.** It helps explain why different societies have different laws.
7. **Links law with justice and society.** It stops law becoming a set of technical rules.
8. **Preparation for new problems.** Artificial intelligence, data privacy and climate change are being met by extending old ideas of rights, personality, harm and State power.

**5. Branches of jurisprudence in outline.** For revision it helps to remember the main branches: *analytical* (Austin, Kelsen, Hart: the structure and concepts of law); *historical* (Savigny, Maine: law as a growth of society); *ethical or philosophical* (natural law, theories of justice); *sociological* (Jhering, Ehrlich, Duguit, Pound: law in its social setting); *realist* (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank: law as what courts do); and *economic or Marxist* (law as a product of economic relations). Each answers a different question: what law is, how it grew, what it ought to be, what it does to society, how judges really decide, and whose interests it serves. A complete study needs all six.

**Conclusion.** Jurisprudence is wide in scope and general in character. Its nature is theoretical, critical and interdisciplinary, and its value lies in explaining why law is what it is, how it can be understood and how it should change. For the lawyer it is the "eye of law": it shows the purpose behind the rules.

## C-03 · Austin's division into general and particular jurisprudence: how far do Holland and Salmond agree?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q9 ("According to Austin, Jurisprudence can be divided into 'General and Particular.' To what extent do Holland and Salmond agree with this view?"). Related: A-11, B-04 (kinds of jurisprudence).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Austin's division 6 · Holland's view 5 · Salmond's view 5 · extent of agreement and evaluation 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** John Austin (1790–1859), the first Professor of Jurisprudence at University College London (from 1826), fixed the subject-matter of jurisprudence in *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) and in his lectures (published after his death). His view that jurisprudence has a general and a particular part has been accepted by some writers and criticised by others. The question asks how far T.E. Holland and Sir John Salmond agree. The answer is: they agree on the central idea that jurisprudence is about positive law and its principles, but each rejects or reshapes Austin's way of dividing it.

**1. Austin's view**
- **Subject-matter.** The proper province of jurisprudence is *positive law*: law "properly so called", which is set by a sovereign to subjects, and which can be separated from the question whether it is good or bad. Jurisprudence is "the philosophy of positive law".
- **General jurisprudence.** It is the science that expounds the principles, notions and distinctions common to all systems of law, at least to the more mature systems. Examples are the ideas of right, duty, injury, sanction, punishment, ownership. It is a study of the "necessary" ideas of law.
- **Particular jurisprudence.** It is the science of a particular, actual system of positive law, such as English law, Hindu law or the law of a modern State. It states the contents and rules of that single system.
- **Legislation.** Austin distinguished both from the science of legislation, or ethics, which asks what law ought to be. That belongs to a different science, not to jurisprudence.
- **Why the division.** For Austin, there are things common to all legal systems, and a student can study them without studying the detailed rules of any one of them. The difference is therefore one of *scope*, not of *kind*: both parts deal with positive law.

**2. Holland's view**
- T.E. Holland (*Elements of Jurisprudence*, 1880) defined jurisprudence as "the formal science of positive law".
- **What he accepts from Austin.** (i) The subject is positive law, law as it is. (ii) The study is not about the merits of law, so ethics and the science of legislation are outside it. (iii) It deals with general ideas.
- **What he rejects.** Holland says the word "formal" shows that jurisprudence studies the *relations* regulated by law, and the ideas of those relations, not the particular rules of a system or their content. If so, jurisprudence is *by nature* general. The study of the rules of a particular country is the study of that country's law, not a separate branch of jurisprudence. So for Holland the division into general and particular is not a division within jurisprudence. The difference between systems is a difference of subject, not of science.
- **Result.** Holland agrees with Austin about the field (positive law) and the exclusion of ethics, but disagrees about the division.

**3. Salmond's view**
- Sir John Salmond (*Jurisprudence*, 1902) defined jurisprudence as "the science of the first principles of the civil law" (the law of the land administered by the courts).
- **What he accepts.** The subject is a body of law that is actually in force (the civil law), and the study seeks first principles. He also recognises that the study can be carried on in a *general* way (the principles of law as such) and in a *particular* way (the first principles of a given system, such as English law).
- **What he adds and rejects.** Salmond does not treat jurisprudence as only the analysis of positive law. In a wider sense jurisprudence includes three parts: *legal exposition* (the analysis of the content of a legal system), *legal history* (how the system and its ideas arose) and the *science of legislation* (the ends law ought to serve). So he includes the very ethical element that Austin and Holland exclude.
- He criticises Holland for rejecting the idea that a study of the principles of a particular system is a part of jurisprudence. He keeps a place for particular jurisprudence, though he does not use Austin's categories as his main plan.

**4. Comparing the three**

| Point | Austin | Holland | Salmond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | Philosophy of positive law | Formal science of positive law | Science of the first principles of the civil law |
| Subject-matter | Positive law (command of the sovereign) | Positive law (the relations it governs) | Civil law in force, with its history and ends |
| Ethics / legislation | Excluded (a separate science) | Excluded | Included (science of legislation) |
| General v. particular | Two parts of jurisprudence | Rejects the division; jurisprudence is general by nature | Accepts that both studies exist; keeps a place for particular jurisprudence |
| Method | Analysis of concepts | Analysis of form and relations | Analysis, history and ethics |

**5. To what extent do they agree?**
1. **Agreement on the object.** All three treat jurisprudence as the study of law that actually exists and not of the moral ideal.
2. **Agreement that it is principle-centred.** All three look for general ideas and principles, not just rules.
3. **Partial disagreement on the division.** Holland rejects it. Salmond accepts that the study can be general or particular but does not rest his plan on it.
4. **Disagreement on the boundary.** Austin and Holland exclude ethics and history; Salmond brings them in.
5. **Result.** Holland agrees with Austin on the object but not on the division; Salmond agrees on the existence of the two levels of study but widens the field. Neither adopts Austin's division as it stands.

**6. Evaluation**
- Austin's division has a practical use: a student can learn the general concepts of law (right, duty, ownership, possession) and then apply them to one system. It still has value as the distinction between *theory of law* and *the law of a country*.
- Holland's criticism is sound as a matter of logic: the difference lies in the object studied, not in the science.
- Salmond's wider scheme is closer to what jurisprudence is today, since modern jurisprudence includes history, sociology and ethics. This is why Austin's narrow "province" was later enlarged by the historical, sociological and realist schools.
- Austin's idea that general jurisprudence deals with ideas common to mature systems is the ancestor of **comparative law** and of the modern idea of "general theory of law".

**7. Indian illustration.** The concept of "legal right" and the concept of "ownership" are general ideas, studied in jurisprudence. Their application to the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 or to the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 is particular. An Indian student must master the general idea first, then apply it.

**Conclusion.** Holland and Salmond agree with Austin that jurisprudence is concerned with positive law and its principles, but neither accepts his division as stated. Holland denies that jurisprudence has two kinds; Salmond keeps both levels but includes history and ethics. The question therefore calls for the answer "to a limited extent": agreement on the field, disagreement on the division and on the boundaries of the subject.

> *Study note.* The account of how Holland and Salmond treat Austin's division is built from textbook and lecture-note summaries, not from a fresh reading of the original pages. Before the exam, check the opening chapter of Salmond's *Jurisprudence* and of Holland's *Elements of Jurisprudence* (the passages on "general" and "particular" jurisprudence) if your teacher gives you a different statement. ## C-04 · "Law is the body of principles recognised and applied by the State in the administration of justice." Discuss.

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q12 ("'Law is the body of principles, recognized and applied by the state in the administration of Justice.' Discuss."). Related: Dec 2023 Q6 (B, law and morals); C-08 (Austin's command theory).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** Salmond's definition and its elements 6 · compared with other definitions 5 · merits and criticism 5 · law and morals; Indian illustration; conclusion 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** This is Sir John Salmond's definition of law. It appears in *Jurisprudence* (1902) and is the basis of his idea that law is a matter of what courts actually enforce. Salmond's definition differs from Austin's. Austin defined law by its *source* (the command of the sovereign) and its *sanction*; Salmond defined it by its *function* (the use made of it by the State in the administration of justice). This answer explains the definition, tests it against others and assesses it.

**1. The elements of the definition**
1. **"Body of principles."** Law is not a single rule or command. It is a body of principles, that is, general standards that can be applied to many cases. This allows custom, precedent and legislation alike to count as law, so long as they contain principles.
2. **"Recognised and applied."** A principle is law only if the courts recognise and apply it. Merely being printed in a statute book is not enough, and a rule that the courts never apply is not law in Salmond's sense. This is close to the realist idea that law is what courts do (Holmes, Gray).
3. **"By the State."** Law is the law of a politically organised society. Its force comes from the State, which has the power to compel. Salmond distinguishes the **State's coercive power** from private rules of a club or a church, which are not law even if people obey them.
4. **"In the administration of justice."** The purpose is to do justice between persons and between the individual and the State. The words link law with justice, but they do not say that an unjust rule loses its character as law.
5. **Law and the courts.** Salmond calls the courts the point at which law is found. He holds that the "sources" of law are the facts by which courts are guided: legislation, precedent, custom, agreement and professional opinion.

**2. Salmond compared with other definitions**

| Writer | Definition of law | Contrast with Salmond |
|---|---|---|
| **Austin** | Command of the sovereign backed by sanction | Austin stresses source and sanction; Salmond stresses application by courts. Salmond's definition can include custom and judge-made law, which Austin finds hard to explain |
| **Kelsen** | A system of coercive norms, valid if derived from the basic norm | Kelsen defines law by validity within a hierarchy; Salmond defines it by what courts apply |
| **Hart** | A union of primary and secondary rules | Hart explains why courts apply rules (the rule of recognition); Salmond takes judicial recognition as the test |
| **Holmes** | "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact" | Closest to Salmond: both look to the courts; Holmes is more sceptical about rules |
| **Gray** | Law is what judges declare | Even more court-centred than Salmond |
| **Savigny** | Law arises from the spirit of the people (*Volksgeist*) | Savigny looks at origin in the people; Salmond looks at the State's enforcement |
| **Cicero / Aquinas** | True law is right reason in agreement with nature | Natural law ties law to justice; Salmond's definition does not make justice a test of validity |

**3. Merits of the definition**
1. **It is practical.** The test is easy to apply: ask what the courts recognise and enforce.
2. **It covers all sources.** Legislation, precedent and custom all count if the courts apply them.
3. **It separates law from morals and religion.** Moral and religious principles are not law unless the State recognises and applies them.
4. **It links law with the State and with justice.** It explains why law has authority.
5. **It is neutral between theories.** It does not require that law be a command or that it follow a basic norm.

**4. Criticism**
1. **Circular.** It defines law as principles applied by courts, but the courts apply law. The definition assumes what it wants to explain.
2. **It ignores law that is not litigated.** Much of the law (constitutional conventions, rules of contract that are never disputed, international law) is obeyed without any court applying it. On Salmond's test it is not law until a court has applied it.
3. **It does not explain the law-making process.** The legislature makes law before any court applies it. A statute passed but not yet applied is surely law.
4. **It makes validity depend on the court's action.** A new statute would not be law until applied; this is the objection also made to Holmes and to Gray.
5. **It excludes unjust rules only by silence.** The reference to "the administration of justice" is vague: does an unjust law that courts apply remain law? Salmond's answer is yes; natural-law critics reject that answer.
6. **International law.** Because it is not applied by the courts of any single State, it falls outside the definition.
7. **It stresses the State.** Customary law of tribes and religious law are law for those who obey them even before the State recognises them (Ehrlich's "living law").

**5. Law and morals.** Salmond's definition separates law from morals. Both regulate conduct, but law is backed by the State and applied by courts, and it concerns external conduct; morality rests on conscience and social opinion. They overlap (murder is both illegal and immoral), and many legal rules are morally neutral (the rule of the road). Natural-law writers say a gravely unjust rule is not law; the positivists say it remains law, though it may be bad law (Hart).

**6. Indian illustration**
- The Indian Constitution and statutes are law because the courts apply them. Under Art. 141 the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts. This accords with Salmond's stress on judicial recognition.
- Custom is law only if proved to be ancient, certain, reasonable and not opposed to statute (*Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy*, 1868, Privy Council). This is Salmond's idea of recognition by courts.
- The decision in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), that Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure, shows the courts defining the limits of legislative power, so that the "body of principles" includes principles found by judges themselves.
- Panchayat decisions and religious bodies' rules are not law unless the courts or statute give them effect.

**7. Assessment.** The definition is a useful statement of the working side of law, and it is the best for answering "what will a court do?" It is incomplete as a theory of law, because it neither explains how law gets its authority (Kelsen, Hart) nor gives a place to justice (natural law).

**Conclusion.** Salmond's definition describes law as a body of principles that courts recognise and apply through the machinery of the State in order to do justice. It is practical and wide, but it is circular and does not explain unlitigated law or the creation of law. It is best taken together with the definitions of Austin (the authority of the sovereign), Kelsen and Hart (the structure of a legal system) and the natural-law view (the link with justice).

## C-05 · Past theories of the natural law school

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q9 ("Discuss fully the past theories of natural law school of Jurisprudence."). Related: Dec 2025 Q13 (C-06), Dec 2024 Q7 (B, revival of natural law; B-08).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and basic idea 3 · Greek and Roman 4 · medieval 4 · modern (social contract, Grotius, Kant) 5 · criticism 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Natural law is the idea that there is a body of law, higher than the law made by rulers, that is derived from nature, reason or God, and by which man-made (positive) law is to be judged. It is the oldest school of jurisprudence. Its history has four periods: the Greek and Roman period, the medieval period, the period of the social contract and rationalism, and the revival in the twentieth century (see C-07). The "past theories" asked for in the question are the first three.

**1. Basic features of natural law**
1. Law is discovered by reason, not made by will.
2. It is universal and unchanging, the same for all people.
3. It is superior to positive law: a rule that conflicts with it is invalid or at least defective.
4. It is the source of justice and of natural rights.

**2. Greek period**
- **Sophists.** They distinguished *physis* (nature) from *nomos* (convention). Some held that law is merely convention; others that natural justice is above law.
- **Socrates, Plato.** Plato's *Republic* and *Laws* stress that law should aim at justice and the good of the community.
- **Aristotle** (384–322 BC, *Nicomachean Ethics*). He distinguished *natural justice*, which has the same force everywhere, from *legal justice*, which is a matter of convention. He drew the distinction between *distributive* and *corrective* justice.
- **Sophocles'** *Antigone* is often cited as an early appeal from the king's decree to the unwritten law of the gods.

**3. Stoics and Roman period**
- **Stoics** (Zeno, 3rd century BC): the universe is governed by reason (*logos*); men, as rational beings, share in it, and the law of nature is the law of reason.
- **Cicero** (106–43 BC, *De Republica*, *De Legibus*): "True law is right reason in agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting." He added that it cannot be repealed or changed by the Senate or the people.
- **Roman jurists.** Ulpian, Gaius and Justinian's *Institutes* distinguish *jus naturale* (natural law), *jus gentium* (the law of nations, common to all peoples) and *jus civile* (the law of Rome). The idea of the *jus gentium* helped to develop Roman law from a narrow civil law into a law for foreigners as well.

**4. Medieval period: the Christian doctrine**
- **St Augustine** (354–430): an unjust law is no law (*lex iniusta non est lex*), a phrase later associated with Aquinas.
- **St Thomas Aquinas** (1225–74, *Summa Theologiae*): he gave the classic Christian statement and distinguished four kinds of law:
  1. **Eternal law**: the reason of God governing the universe.
  2. **Divine law**: God's law revealed in scripture.
  3. **Natural law**: the part of the eternal law that man can know by reason.
  4. **Human (positive) law**: rules made by rulers for the common good.
  A human law is valid only so far as it follows the natural law; if it contradicts it, it is not law but "a perversion of law".
- **Result.** In the medieval period natural law rested on God's authority, and the Church used it to limit the power of kings.

**5. The modern period: rationalism and the social contract**
- **Hugo Grotius** (1583–1645, *De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, 1625): the "father of international law". He held that natural law comes from human reason and the social nature of man and "would have some validity even if we were to concede that there is no God". This separated natural law from theology.
- **Thomas Hobbes** (1588–1679, *Leviathan*, 1651): in the state of nature life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short"; men make a contract and surrender their rights to an absolute sovereign for the sake of peace.
- **John Locke** (1632–1704, *Second Treatise of Government*, 1689): the natural rights of life, liberty and property exist before government; government rests on consent and may be resisted if it breaks its trust. His ideas shaped the American and French Declarations.
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (1712–78, *The Social Contract*, 1762): the "general will" is the source of legitimate law; man is born free.
- **Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804): the moral law, the "categorical imperative", is a law of reason; right is the harmony of the freedom of each with the freedom of all.
- **Practical effect.** These ideas inspired the American Declaration of Independence (1776), the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) and, in the twentieth century, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948).

**6. Decline in the nineteenth century.** Natural law was criticised by Bentham ("nonsense upon stilts"), by Austin, and by the historical school. The age of codification and legislation made the law of the State the main object of study. Positivism replaced natural law until the horrors of the twentieth century led to its revival.

**7. Criticism of the past theories**
1. **Vague and uncertain content.** There is no agreement on what the law of nature contains; it has been used for opposite purposes (to defend slavery and to attack it, to defend absolute monarchy and to attack it).
2. **Confuses law and morals.** It blurs the difference between what law is and what it should be (Austin, Bentham).
3. **Unscientific.** Its principles are assumed, not proved.
4. **Shows no way of enforcing itself.** It depends on the conscience of rulers or the pressure of revolution.
5. **Unhistorical.** Savigny and Maine showed that law develops in history and varies with each society; it is not the same everywhere.
6. **Abuse.** American courts in the *Lochner* era used natural-law ideas of "liberty of contract" to strike down social legislation.

**8. Contribution.** Despite its weaknesses, natural law has: (i) provided a standard for criticising bad law; (ii) given rise to ideas of human rights and constitutional limits on power; (iii) created international law (Grotius); (iv) inspired the American and French revolutions; and (v) supplied a test for the Nuremberg trials after 1945.

**9. India.** In ancient India, *dharma* (Manu, the Mahabharata) was a higher law that bound even the king; Manu names the Veda, the *smriti*, good custom (*sadachara*) and one's own conscience (*atmatushti*) as the sources of dharma. In modern India, the Constitution's Preamble, Part III and the doctrine of the basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) and the decision in *Puttaswamy* (2017) carry a natural-law spirit.

**Conclusion.** The past theories of natural law moved from nature (Greeks), to universal reason (Stoics and Romans), to God (Aquinas), and then to human reason and the social contract (Grotius to Kant). Each period used natural law to limit the power of the ruler. The theory has been criticised for vagueness, but its main idea, that law must meet a standard of justice, remains alive in human rights law and constitutional review.

## C-06 · "The basis of natural law was man's reason and wisdom, and not the spiritual power of God." Discuss.

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q13 ("The basis of natural law was the man's reason and wisdom and not the spiritual power of God. Discuss.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** the thesis and the two bases 3 · reason-based theories (Stoics, Cicero, Grotius, Kant) 7 · God-based theories (Aquinas) 4 · evaluation 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Natural law has been grounded on two different foundations. One is the *will of God* (the theological basis), found in the medieval Christian writers, above all St Thomas Aquinas. The other is *human reason* (the rational basis), found in the Stoics, Cicero and, after the Reformation, Grotius and the writers of the social contract. The statement in the question says that natural law was based on reason and wisdom, not on God. This is true of some periods and false of others. The answer should therefore be: the statement is correct for the Greek, Roman and modern periods but not for the medieval period, and in the modern period the separation was not complete.

**1. The meaning of the statement.** To say that natural law rests on reason is to say that its rules can be discovered by man, using his own rational and moral faculties, without revelation. To say that it rests on God is to say that its authority comes from God's eternal law, and that reason is only the means by which man knows it.

**2. The rational basis: Greek and Roman thought**
- **Aristotle.** Natural justice is what is right by nature and has the same force everywhere; it is known by the practical wisdom of the reasonable man.
- **The Stoics.** The universe is ordered by reason (*logos*); man, being rational, shares in it; to live according to nature is to live according to reason. For them natural law was a law of reason, the same for all rational beings.
- **Cicero** (*De Republica*, III): "True law is right reason in agreement with nature, of universal application, unchanging and eternal." He added that God is its author and promulgator in the sense that he is the source of reason in all men, but the law is known by reason. Roman jurists applied this reasoning in their idea of *jus gentium*.

**3. The theological basis: the medieval period**
- **St Thomas Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae*): natural law is man's participation in the eternal law of God through reason. It is "the rational creature's participation in the eternal law". The ruler's laws bind in conscience only if they are consistent with it.
- Here reason is the means and God is the source. A positive law is just if it follows natural law, and natural law derives its force from the eternal law. So the statement in the question is not true of the medieval period.
- **Church and State.** The theory served the Church in its claim to limit secular rulers.

**4. The return to reason: the modern period**
- **Hugo Grotius** (*De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, 1625): natural law is "the dictate of right reason", derived from man's rational and social nature. He added that it would hold even if there were no God, which is the key statement for this question. He still treated God as its ultimate author, but the content of the law no longer depended on divine revelation.
- **Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau.** They found the basis of law and government in a social contract made by rational individuals in a state of nature. Hobbes: the law of nature is a precept found out by reason (*Leviathan*, ch. 14). Locke: "Reason, which is that law, teaches all mankind who will but consult it" (*Second Treatise*), though he also spoke of God as maker of man. Rousseau: law rests on the general will.
- **Kant** (1724–1804): the basis of morality and right is practical reason alone; the categorical imperative.
- **Effect.** After Grotius, natural law became a secular philosophy: the "Age of Reason" or Enlightenment replaced the authority of the Church by the authority of reason, and natural rights became the basis of the American and French Declarations.

**5. The revival: reason with variable content.** In the twentieth century, writers such as **Stammler** (natural law with a variable content), **Kohler** (law serves civilisation) and **Fuller** (the inner morality of law) revived natural law on rational and social grounds, not on religion. **John Finnis** (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980) bases natural law on basic human goods known by practical reason. Neo-Thomists such as Maritain, however, still base it on God.

**6. Evaluation**
1. **True of the Greek, Roman and Enlightenment writers.** For them reason is the basis, and God is at most the author of reason.
2. **Not true of the medieval period.** Aquinas ties natural law to eternal law.
3. **Even the rationalists did not break completely with God.** Grotius, Locke and Kant spoke of God as creator, though their arguments do not depend on him.
4. **Importance of the shift.** Placing the basis in reason allowed natural law to be argued by lawyers and philosophers, to be applied to international relations, and to be used against both kings and the Church. It also made it possible to defend rights in a society of many religions, as in the Indian Constitution.
5. **Weakness.** Reason has led different thinkers to different conclusions. Hobbes derived absolute government and Locke limited government from the same state of nature.

**7. India.** Indian thought treats *dharma* as a law of cosmic order, known through the Vedas, *smriti*, custom and conscience; it is not only reason and not only revelation. In modern India, the courts base fundamental rights on reason and on constitutional text, not on religion (*Puttaswamy*, 2017: life and liberty are natural rights that exist before the Constitution; *Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973: the basic structure).

**Conclusion.** The statement is partly correct. Natural law began as a law of reason (Greeks, Stoics, Cicero), was linked to God in the medieval Church (Aquinas), and was freed from theology by the rationalists from Grotius onwards. The modern theories base it on human reason, wisdom and the social needs of people. The truth is that natural law was never based on reason alone nor on God alone: in each age the source was the dominant idea of that age, and the permanent element was the belief that law must conform to a higher standard of justice.

## C-07 · The revival of natural law: Stammler, Kohler and later writers

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("revival of natural law — Rudolf Stammler and Kohler"). Related: Dec 2024 Q7 (B, "Write a note on revival of natural law"; B-08), Dec 2025 Q13 (C-06).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** why natural law declined and revived 4 · Stammler 5 · Kohler 5 · other revivalists (Fuller, Finnis, Rawls) 3 · criticism 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Natural law declined in the nineteenth century under the attacks of Bentham, Austin and the historical school, and under the dominance of legislation and codification. It came back in the late nineteenth and the twentieth century. The causes were (i) the failure of pure positivism to give a standard to criticise unjust law; (ii) the abuses of power in the two World Wars and under totalitarian regimes, in particular Nazi Germany; (iii) the rise of the idea of human rights; and (iv) the need to find a link between law and social ideals in an age of rapid social change. The revived natural law differs from the old one: it does not claim a fixed code valid for all time, but ideals that guide the making and applying of law.

**1. Rudolf Stammler (1856–1938)**
- A German neo-Kantian philosopher of law. Main work: *Die Lehre von dem richtigen Rechte* (1902), translated as *The Theory of Justice* (1925).
- **"Natural law with a variable content."** Stammler said that no fixed legal rules can be valid for all times and places. What is constant is the *form* or method of just law; its content varies with time and place. Natural law is not a code, but a method of testing law.
- **The social ideal.** The ideal of law is a "community of free-willing men": a society in which each person's will is respected as an end, not as a means to another's ends.
- **Four principles of just law.** Stammler turned the ideal into formal tests (wording varies slightly between textbooks):
  - *Principles of respecting:* (1) the content of one person's will must not be subject to the arbitrary wishes of another; (2) every legal demand must be such that the person bound by it can remain his own "neighbour", that is, be treated as an end and not merely as a means.
  - *Principles of participating:* (1) a person who is legally bound must not be arbitrarily excluded from the community; (2) a power of exclusion given by law may be exercised only in a way that leaves the excluded person still a "neighbour".
  These tests do not give ready-made rules; they are a method for judging whether a particular rule is just.
- **Method.** Law is the "form" of social life, and economics is its "matter". Stammler held against the Marxists that form gives shape to matter.
- **Criticism.** The principles are too formal and give no clear answers in difficult cases; the "community of free-willing men" is an ideal not a rule; and the theory does not say how to decide between competing claims.

**2. Josef Kohler (1849–1919)**
- A German jurist and Hegelian philosopher. Main works: *Lehrbuch der Rechtsphilosophie* (1909, translated as *Philosophy of Law*, 1914).
- **Law and civilisation.** Kohler said that law is a product of civilisation and must be judged by how far it helps the growth of civilisation. The task of law is to preserve existing civilisation and to develop it. A legal rule is good if it helps the fullest development of human powers and the greatest possible control over nature (a summary of Kohler's idea, as it is usually stated in textbooks).
- **Relativity.** Like Stammler, he held that law changes: "the law of each age must be suited to the civilisation of that age". He rejected fixed natural law but kept the idea of an ideal.
- **A "jurisprudence of civilisation."** Jurists must study the social conditions and values of their time and adapt law to them. The judge must not merely apply the letter of law but must interpret it so as to help civilisation. Kohler thus stood close to the sociological school.
- **Criticism.** "Civilisation" is vague and can justify different policies; it was criticised as unclear about whose civilisation is meant.

**3. Stammler and Kohler compared**

| Point | Stammler | Kohler |
|---|---|---|
| Philosophical roots | Kant (formal method) | Hegel (historical development) |
| Ideal | Community of free-willing men | Advance of civilisation |
| Natural law | Form is constant, content varies | Law relative to the stage of civilisation |
| Relation to social science | Separates form and matter | Wants jurists to study social facts |
| Weakness | Too formal | Too vague |

**4. Later revivalists (for completeness)**
- **Lon L. Fuller** (*The Morality of Law*, 1964): the "inner morality of law" consists of eight requirements of lawmaking: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy and congruence between declared rule and official action. A system that fails these altogether does not produce law. This is a procedural natural law. See the **Hart–Fuller debate** (1958), in which Hart defended the separation of law and morals and Fuller replied in *Positivism and Fidelity to Law*.
- **Gustav Radbruch** (1878–1949), after the Nazi regime, argued that extreme injustice is not law (the "Radbruch formula"), a principle used in German trials after 1945.
- **John Finnis** (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, 1980): natural law rests on basic human goods (life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability, practical reasonableness, religion) identified by practical reason.
- **John Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971): the principles of justice are those that rational people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance". This is a secular, contractarian form of natural-law thinking.
- **Ronald Dworkin** (*Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977): judges apply principles of rights and morality as well as rules.
- **Neo-Thomists** (Jacques Maritain) still ground natural law in God.

**5. Why the revival matters.** It has (i) produced a standard for criticising unjust law; (ii) supported the international law of human rights (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948) and the **Nuremberg trials**, where "following orders" was rejected as a defence for crimes against humanity; (iii) supported the doctrine of the basic structure and the expansion of Art. 21 in India.

**6. India.** The Preamble's ideals of justice, liberty, equality and fraternity, Part III and Part IV, and the basic structure (*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*, 1973) carry the idea that law is subject to higher principles. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) read natural-justice and reasonableness into Art. 21. *Puttaswamy* (2017) held that life and liberty are inalienable natural rights that pre-exist the Constitution and overruled *ADM Jabalpur* (1976), which had treated them as dependent on the Constitution.

**7. Criticism of the revival**
1. Variable content makes natural law vague, and it can be used to support any political view.
2. Positivists (Hart) say the separation of law and morals is still necessary for clear thinking: an immoral law is still law, and it should be criticised and repealed.
3. The "morality" of law is often only procedural (Fuller) and cannot rule out an evil but orderly regime.
4. The revival is a family of different theories, not one school.

**Conclusion.** The revival of natural law showed that no theory of law can ignore justice. Stammler offered a formal test (the community of free-willing men), Kohler an evolutionary test (the advance of civilisation); later writers added procedural morality (Fuller), basic goods (Finnis) and fairness (Rawls). The revived theories give up the old claim of a fixed code, but they keep the central idea that law should be tested by a standard of justice.
