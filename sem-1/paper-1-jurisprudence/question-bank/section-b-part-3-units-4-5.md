## B-25 · Discuss in short the *Volksgeist* theory of law

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q6 ("Discuss in short the 'Volksgeist Theory' of law."). 20-mark form: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · main propositions 4 · criticism 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

*Volksgeist* means the spirit or common consciousness of the people. The theory is that of Friedrich Carl von Savigny (1779–1861), the founder of the historical school, in *Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft* (1814).

**Propositions**
1. Law is not made by a legislator but arises, like language, from the common life and convictions of a people, growing organically and unconsciously.
2. It is national, not universal: each people has its own law.
3. It is found, not made: the legislator only declares what already lives in the people's consciousness.
4. Law passes through stages: custom first; then technical law developed by jurists, who represent the people's consciousness; legislation last.

**Criticism**
1. The *Volksgeist* is vague and mystical; a society has many groups with conflicting practices.
2. It undervalues legislation, which in modern societies is used deliberately for reform.
3. It glorifies custom, though some customs are unjust (sati, untouchability).
4. Savigny favoured Roman law, which Germany had borrowed.

**Contribution.** It stressed history and custom and prepared the way for sociological jurisprudence.

**India.** Customary and personal laws illustrate it; *Shayara Bano* (2017) and *Navtej Johar* (2018) show where custom yields to constitutional rights.

## B-26 · "Law grows with the growth, strengthens with the strength of the people and finally dies away as a nation loses its nationality." Explain

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q6 (same words). Related: C-13, A-58.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** source and meaning 3 · explanation in three parts 4 · assessment 3

### Answer

The statement is from Savigny, the leader of the historical school, and expresses his view that law is the product of a people's life.

**Meaning**
1. *Law grows with the growth of the people.* Like language, law develops gradually and unconsciously as the people's customs, beliefs and needs develop. It is not invented by a legislator.
2. *It strengthens with the strength of the people.* When a nation is vigorous and its common consciousness (*Volksgeist*) is strong, its law is also vigorous and well developed.
3. *It dies away as the nation loses its nationality.* Law is bound up with national identity; when a people loses its distinct existence or character, its law loses its force and is replaced by another.

**Implications.** Law is national and not universal; it is found, not made; hasty codification is dangerous (Thibaut–Savigny, 1814).

**Assessment.** The statement is useful in showing law's link with society and culture, and in the persistence of customary law. But it is exaggerated: laws can be borrowed (the Indian Contract Act drew on English law), survive the loss of national independence, and be changed deliberately. Modern law combines custom with legislation.

## B-27 · Savigny and the codification controversy (Thibaut v. Savigny)

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Savigny"). Related: C-13.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · Thibaut 2 · Savigny 4 · outcome and India 2

### Answer

**Background.** After the Napoleonic Code (1804) France had a unified civil law, while Germany was a patchwork of states with different laws.

**Thibaut.** In 1814 A.F.J. Thibaut, a Heidelberg professor, published *On the Necessity of a General Civil Law for Germany*. He urged one rational code to unite the country and remove confusion.

**Savigny's reply.** In the same year, Savigny published *Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft*. He argued that: (1) law grows out of the *Volksgeist* and cannot be created by a legislator; (2) the German legal science was not ready, as the historical knowledge of Roman and Germanic law was incomplete; (3) a code made now would be defective and, once enacted, would stop natural development; (4) history first, a code later.

**Outcome.** The German Civil Code (*Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch*) came into force in 1900, built by Savigny's followers (the Pandectists Puchta and Windscheid).

**Assessment.** Savigny was right to demand careful preparation but wrong to oppose codes as such; codes have worked in Germany, Switzerland and India (the Indian Penal Code 1860, the Contract Act 1872, and the BNS 2023).

The same debate recurs in India over the Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44).

## B-28 · Maine's stages of legal development

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Maine"). Related: C-15, A-63.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** method 1 · four stages 5 · static and progressive societies 2 · criticism 2

### Answer

Sir Henry Maine (1822–88), in *Ancient Law* (1861), used the historical-comparative method to find stages through which progressive societies pass.

1. **Themistes.** The earliest judgments were thought to be inspired by the gods and delivered by the king (Homer's *themistes*). There was no body of law, only isolated judgments.
2. **Customary law.** The judgments hardened into customs, kept and applied by a privileged class (a priestly or aristocratic class).
3. **Codes.** With writing, custom was recorded to end the monopoly of that class: the Twelve Tables of Rome, the Greek codes and the Code of Manu.
4. **Modern growth.** After codes, a progressive society changes its law by *legal fictions*, *equity* and *legislation* (see B-29).

**Static and progressive societies.** Most societies, Maine said, stop developing after the code stage; only a few, mainly in Western Europe, continue. The movement of progressive societies is from status to contract.

**Criticism.** The evidence was narrow (Roman, Hindu, Hebrew); the stages are not universal; the division into static and progressive societies is Eurocentric; and he underrated economic factors and conscious law-making.

**Merit.** Maine founded comparative legal history and applied it in India as Law Member (1862–69).

## B-29 · Legal fictions, equity and legislation as instruments of legal change (Maine)

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Maine"). Related: C-15, A-64.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** introduction 1 · each of the three 2 or 3 · evaluation 1

### Answer

In *Ancient Law* (1861) Maine said that once a society has its law in a code, it can adapt law to new needs only in three ways, which he called the instrumentalities of legal change.

1. **Legal fictions.** An assumption, not open to challenge, that a fact is so when it is not, by which the letter of the law is kept but its operation changed. Examples: adoption, by which a stranger is treated as the natural son (in Roman and Hindu law); and in English law, the fictitious *John Doe* and *Richard Roe* in the action of ejectment.
2. **Equity.** A body of rules claiming a higher justice, standing beside the civil law and overriding it where it is harsh or defective: the Roman *praetor's edict* and the English Court of Chancery.
3. **Legislation.** Conscious law-making by an authority recognised by the society. It is the last and most powerful instrument.

**Order.** Fictions work secretly, equity openly beside the old law, and legislation changes the law directly.

**Evaluation.** The scheme shows how law reconciles stability with change, but it overstresses the Roman and English experience.

## B-30 · The historical school: assessment, and its place in India

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Historical School"). Related: C-12, C-13, C-15.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** features 3 · merits 2 · demerits 3 · India 2

### Answer

**Features.** The historical school (Hugo, Savigny, Puchta, Maine) holds that law is not made by a sovereign but is found in a people's life and grows with it; it is national and not universal; custom is the primary form of law and legislation is secondary; the method is historical.

**Merits**
1. It introduced the historical method and respect for custom.
2. It showed that law is rooted in social life, preparing the way for the sociological school and anthropology.
3. It gave a lasting warning against copying foreign law.
4. Maine founded comparative legal history.

**Demerits**
1. The *Volksgeist* is vague and mystical.
2. It undervalues legislation and overvalues custom (some customs are unjust).
3. Borrowing of law, such as the German reception of Roman law, contradicts its premise.
4. It is conservative.
5. Status to contract is not a straight line.

**India.** Custom and personal laws are central; Art. 13(3)(a) counts "custom or usage having the force of law" as law; courts apply proved custom (*Collector of Madura*, 1868; *Hurpurshad*, 1876). But custom is tested against fundamental rights (*Shayara Bano*, 2017), and harmful practices are removed by statute (the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987; Art. 17).

## B-31 · Write a short note on Duguit's doctrine of social solidarity

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q8 ("Write short note on Duguit's doctrine of 'Social Solidarity'."). Related: Dec 2025 Q5, Dec 2024 Q5, Dec 2023 Q4 (Section A); 20-mark form: C-18; 4-mark forms: A-75, A-76.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · law and State 3 · property and public service 2 · criticism 2

### Answer

Léon Duguit (1859–1928), French jurist at Bordeaux, wrote *Les transformations du droit public* (1913; English *Law in the Modern State*, 1919), drawing on Comte and Durkheim.

**Social solidarity.** Human beings live in society and are interdependent. This interdependence is a social fact. It takes two forms: solidarity by similarity and solidarity by division of labour. The rule of conduct that arises from it is the *objective law* (*droit objectif*): a rule is law when the community feels that its breach is a breach of social solidarity.

**Consequences**
1. *No sovereign State will:* sovereignty is a fiction; the State is the strongest group, limited by objective law, and justified only so far as it serves solidarity.
2. *No individual rights:* people have social functions and duties, not natural rights; "rights" are powers to perform functions.
3. *Property is a social function*, not an absolute right.
4. *Public service:* the State is a cooperative of public services (*service public*).

**Criticism.** "Solidarity" is vague; the denial of rights is dangerous to liberty; it confuses "is" and "ought"; it ignores conflict and class.

**India.** Arts. 39(b)–(c), 51A (duties) and Art. 300A reflect the ideas of social function and duty.

## B-32 · Rudolf von Jhering: law as a means to an end

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Sociological School: background"). Related: C-17, A-73.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** life and works 2 · purpose of law 3 · struggle for law 3 · criticism 2

### Answer

Rudolf von Jhering (1818–92), a German jurist, began as a follower of the historical school but turned to a purposive view of law. Works: *Der Kampf ums Recht* (*The Struggle for Law*, 1872) and *Der Zweck im Recht* (*Law as a Means to an End*, 1877–83).

**Law has a purpose.** Law is neither the will of a sovereign nor the spirit of a people; it is a means to a social end, the securing of the conditions of social life. He defined law as the sum of the conditions of social life, in the widest sense, secured by the State through external compulsion.

**Interests.** A right is a legally protected interest. Law has to balance the interests of the individual with those of society (social utilitarianism).

**Struggle for law.** Law is won through struggle, and every person has a duty to assert his rights, for the defence of rights is also the defence of law and benefits the community.

**Influence.** He is a forerunner of Pound's theory of interests and of sociological jurisprudence.

**Criticism.** The idea of "social ends" is vague; it is utilitarian; it relies on State compulsion; the "struggle" may encourage litigation.

## B-33 · Eugen Ehrlich's "living law"

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Sociological School: background"). Related: C-17, A-74.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · living law v. State law 4 · implications 2 · criticism and India 2

### Answer

Eugen Ehrlich (1862–1922), an Austrian jurist, taught at Czernowitz in Bukovina, where he studied the customs of the many peoples of the region. His work is *Grundlegung der Soziologie des Rechts* (*Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law*, 1913), and he is regarded as the founder of the sociology of law.

**Central idea.** "The centre of gravity of legal development lies not in legislation, nor in juristic science, nor in judicial decision, but in society itself."

**Living law.** The law that actually governs social life, found in the practices of families, associations, trade and villages, whether or not it is written or enforced by courts. It differs from **State law**, the norms used by courts to decide disputes.

**Implications.** Legislators and judges should study and follow the living law; a code or a decision that departs from it will not work.

**Criticism**
1. Living law is hard to identify and may merge into social custom.
2. It undervalues the role of the State and legislation.

**India.** Lok Adalats (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987), village panchayats and recognition of tribal customary law (Arts. 371A, 371G) reflect living law.

## B-34 · Pound's classification of interests

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Roscoe Pound"). Related: C-16, A-80.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · three classes 5 · use 2 · criticism 1

### Answer

Roscoe Pound defined an *interest* as a claim, demand or desire that people, individually or in groups, seek to satisfy, and which law must therefore consider. Law recognises interests, defines the limits of their legal effect, secures them within those limits, and weighs them when they conflict.

**Classification**
1. **Individual interests**: claims of the individual as such. (a) *Personality*: body, reputation, privacy, freedom of will, belief; (b) *domestic relations*: marriage and family; (c) *interests of substance*: property, contract, association, employment.
2. **Public interests**: claims of the State as a legal person, or as guardian of social interests.
3. **Social interests**: claims of society as a whole: (a) general security; (b) security of social institutions; (c) general morals; (d) conservation of social resources; (e) general progress; (f) individual life (self-assertion, opportunity and conditions of life).

**Use.** Conflicts are solved by comparing all claims as claims of society, using the jural postulates.

**Criticism.** The classes overlap, since the same interest may be put in several classes (Julius Stone); there is no scale of values; the list reflects early twentieth-century America.

**India.** Courts weigh individual and social interests under Arts. 19(2)–(6) and in proportionality review (*Puttaswamy*, 2017).

## B-35 · Pound's jural postulates

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Roscoe Pound"). Related: C-16, A-81.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · five postulates 5 · evaluation 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** Jural postulates are the assumptions about what members of a civilised society may expect of each other, which the law should secure. They give the judge and legislator a standard to weigh competing interests. They are relative to time and place, and change with civilisation.

**Pound's postulates for the society of his time**
1. People must be able to assume that others will not commit intentional aggression upon them.
2. They must be able to control, for beneficial purposes, what they have discovered, created or acquired.
3. They must be able to assume that those with whom they deal will act in good faith.
4. They must be able to assume that those who act will use due care to avoid injury.
5. They must be able to assume that those who keep things likely to escape and do damage will keep them within bounds (compare *Rylands v. Fletcher*).

Pound later added others, such as spreading the burden of enterprise risks on those who benefit (compare *M.C. Mehta*, 1987).

**Evaluation.** The postulates reflect Anglo-American conditions of Pound's time and are not a complete scale of values.

## B-36 · Social engineering: criticism and application in India

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q11; Dec 2024 Q10; Dec 2023 Q9; Dec 2022 Q12 (20-mark C-16). This is the 10-mark form.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 3 · criticism 3 · Indian application 4

### Answer

**Meaning.** Roscoe Pound saw law as social engineering: building as efficient a structure of society as possible by securing the greatest number of competing claims with the least friction and waste. The jurist, like an engineer, identifies the interests involved, balances them and shapes rules and institutions.

**Criticism**
1. The classification of interests overlaps and is arbitrary (Stone).
2. There is no scale of values to decide which interest prevails.
3. "Engineering" suggests control by an elite.
4. It is tied to America in the early twentieth century.
5. It blurs law and policy and may let judges invade the legislature's role.

**India**
1. The Constitution is itself an instrument of social engineering: the Preamble's justice; Part IV directives.
2. *Reasonable restrictions* (Art. 19) and *proportionality* balance rights and social interests (*Modern Dental College*, 2016; *Puttaswamy*, 2017).
3. Public interest litigation (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984), *Olga Tellis* (1985), and *Vishaka* (1997) are examples of the Court serving social needs.
4. Absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987) shifts industrial risk to enterprises.
5. Reservation (*Indra Sawhney*, 1992) balances equality and social justice.

**Conclusion.** The theory underlies Indian welfare legislation and constitutional adjudication.

## B-37 · Characteristics of the sociological school

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Background and Characteristics"). Related: C-17.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · characteristics 5 · merits and criticism 3

### Answer

The sociological school arose in the late nineteenth century, out of dissatisfaction with the analytical school (law as command), the historical school (law as growth) and natural law, and out of the new social sciences (Comte, Spencer, Durkheim). Its leaders are Jhering, Ehrlich, Pound and Duguit.

**Characteristics**
1. Law is a social phenomenon, to be studied through its function and effect, not only its form.
2. Emphasis on *law in action* rather than *law in books*.
3. Law is an instrument of social control and social change.
4. The preference for interests and social purposes over abstract rights.
5. It is interdisciplinary: law with sociology, economics and psychology.
6. It is empirical, using facts and statistics (the "Brandeis brief" in *Muller v. Oregon*, 1908).
7. It favours individualised justice and purposive interpretation.
8. It is pragmatic and reform-minded.

**Merits.** It brought law into touch with society and encouraged welfare legislation.

**Criticism.** It lacks a single theory and a scale of values; "social interest" is vague; it may undervalue certainty and individual rights.

**India.** The Constitution's Preamble and Part IV, public interest litigation, and welfare statutes are expressions of the approach.

*Word count: 190*
