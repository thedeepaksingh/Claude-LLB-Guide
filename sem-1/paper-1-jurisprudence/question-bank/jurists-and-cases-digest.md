# Paper I · Jurists at a Glance and Cases Digest (facts · issue · holding · principle · what happened next)

> **Last updated:** 2026-10-28 (IST) · **Law stated as on:** 2026-10-28 · **Branch:** `paper/01-jurisprudence`
> **What this is.** Part 1 is a one-page-per-school table of the thinkers (dates, works, core idea, main criticism, where each is used). Part 2 is a digest of every case the model answers rely on: **material facts** (where I could confirm them), **issue**, **holding**, **principle** to quote, **subsequent developments** (where there are any), and the answers that use it. Part 3 matches each school to its best cases. Use it to write a thinker in 2 lines (4 marks), 4–5 lines (10 marks) or a paragraph (20 marks).
> **How certain is each entry?** Three levels.
> - **‡ checked**: confirmed this session against a published source (see the [source-verification log](source-verification-log.md)).
> - **†**: written from memory and I am less sure of it. Obtain it from the report or a textbook before you rely on it. In the exam, write only what you are sure of.
> - **Unmarked**: standard textbook knowledge. Name, year and holding are reliable, but I did not re-check the detail this session.
> Dates of thinkers are the usual ones in textbooks; a few (Manu, Kautilya) are only scholarly estimates.
> **Key to "used in".** `A-nn` = [Section A](section-a-part-1-units-1-2.md) (parts [1](section-a-part-1-units-1-2.md) · [2](section-a-part-2-unit-3.md) · [3](section-a-part-3-units-4-5.md) · [4](section-a-part-4-units-6-7.md)); `B-nn` = Section B (parts [1](section-b-part-1-units-1-2.md) · [2](section-b-part-2-unit-3.md) · [3](section-b-part-3-units-4-5.md) · [4](section-b-part-4-units-6-7.md)); `C-nn` = Section C (parts [1](section-c-part-1-units-1-2.md) · [2](section-c-part-2-unit-3.md) · [3](section-c-part-3-units-4-5.md) · [4](section-c-part-4-units-6-7-cross.md)).

## 2A · Morality, natural law and the limits of law (Units 1–2)

### 1. Nuremberg Trials (1945–46) · International Military Tribunal
- **Facts.** After the Second World War, the leaders of Nazi Germany were tried at Nuremberg under the London Charter (8 August 1945) for crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity. Many pleaded that they had only obeyed orders and German law.
- **Issue.** Can a person escape liability by saying that his act was lawful under the law of his own State?
- **Held.** The Tribunal rejected the defences of superior orders and State law. The Charter (Art. 8) said that acting under orders is no defence, though it may reduce the punishment.
- **Principle.** Some acts are wrong whatever the positive law says; the natural-law case against pure positivism.
- **After.** The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the Rome Statute of the ICC (1998) carry the idea forward. The trials revived natural-law thought after 1945 (see Radbruch).
- **Used in:** A-30, B-08, C-07, C-24.

### 2. The grudge-informer case (Oberlandesgericht Bamberg, 1949) †
- **Facts.** In 1944 a German woman, wanting to be rid of her husband, denounced him to the authorities for insulting remarks about Hitler that he had made on leave from the army. Under Nazi statutes he was sentenced to death, but he was sent to the front instead of being executed. After the war she was prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty. She argued that she had only reported an offence under the laws of the time.
- **Issue.** Was the Nazi statute "law" that justified her act?
- **Held.** The court convicted her: the statute was contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent people.
- **Principle.** The core test case of the **Hart–Fuller debate** (1958). Hart said the statute was valid law, though too evil to be obeyed, and that a retroactive punishment statute would have been the honest way to deal with her; Fuller said that a wicked system loses the character of law.
- **After.** Radbruch's formula (1946) is the best-known statement of the court's approach; German courts used it after 1990 against Berlin Wall guards.
- **Used in:** A-30, A-53, B-12, C-11, C-24.

### 3. *Riggs v. Palmer* 115 NY 506 (1889) · Court of Appeals of New York
- **Facts.** Elmer Palmer poisoned his grandfather, Francis Palmer, whose will left him the bulk of the estate, because he feared that his grandfather would change the will. The daughters, who would otherwise take, sued to stop him from inheriting.
- **Issue.** The statute of wills had no exception for a murderer. Could he still inherit?
- **Held.** No. The statute had to be read subject to the maxim that no one shall profit from his own wrong.
- **Principle.** Dworkin used this case (*Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977) to show that law contains **principles** as well as rules, so Hart's rule-based positivism is incomplete.
- **After.** The principle is in many statutes today (for example, s. 25 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, disqualifies a murderer from inheriting from his victim).
- **Used in:** A-55, C-11, C-24.

### 4. *R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884) 14 QBD 273 · Queen's Bench Division
- **Facts.** After the yacht *Mignonette* sank, four men were adrift in an open boat for many days. Dudley and Stephens killed and ate the cabin boy, Parker, who was weakest, to survive.
- **Issue.** Is necessity a defence to murder?
- **Held.** No. Lord Coleridge C.J. held that the killing was murder and that necessity does not justify taking an innocent life. (The sentence of death was commuted to six months.)
- **Principle.** A moral limit that the court would not bend, even for a sympathetic accused. Used to show how law and morality meet.
- **After.** The English rule is cited as persuasive authority in India. The Indian provision on necessity is s. 81 IPC (now BNS s. 19 †), which applies where an act is done without criminal intent to prevent a greater harm.
- **Used in:** C-24.

### 5. *Shaw v. DPP* [1962] AC 220 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Shaw published the *Ladies' Directory*, which listed prostitutes with their addresses and details. He was convicted of living on the earnings of prostitutes, and of conspiracy to corrupt public morals.
- **Issue.** Is there an offence of conspiracy to corrupt public morals when no statute creates it?
- **Held.** Yes. Viscount Simonds held that the courts retain a residual power to protect the moral welfare of the State.
- **Principle.** It supports **Devlin's** view in the Hart–Devlin debate (courts and criminal law may enforce shared morality). Hart criticised the decision.
- **After.** *Knuller v. DPP* [1973] AC 435 accepted the offence but with caution. In *Navtej* and *Joseph Shine* the Indian Supreme Court preferred constitutional morality to prevailing social morality when it tested criminal laws.
- **Used in:** A-54, C-24.

### 6. *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 · 5 judges, unanimous (6 September 2018)
- **Facts.** Navtej Singh Johar (a dancer) and others challenged s. 377 IPC, which criminalised "carnal intercourse against the order of nature", as applied to consensual acts between adults. The Delhi High Court had read it down in *Naz Foundation* (2009), but *Suresh Kumar Koushal v. Naz Foundation* (2013, reported (2014) 1 SCC 1) had set that decision aside.
- **Issue.** Does s. 377, applied to consensual adult conduct, violate Arts. 14, 15, 19 and 21?
- **Held.** Yes. Section 377 was read down to the extent that it criminalised consensual sexual conduct between adults in private. *Koushal* was overruled. Dipak Misra C.J. and the other judges preferred **constitutional morality** to prevailing social morality.
- **Principle.** Constitutional morality prevails over majority social morality; dignity and autonomy are part of Art. 21.
- **After.** The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (in force from 1 July 2024) has no provision corresponding to s. 377 †.
- **Used in:** A-54, C-08, C-11, C-13, C-24.

### 7. *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) · 5 judges (27 September 2018; reported (2019) 3 SCC 39 ‡)
- **Facts.** Joseph Shine, a non-resident Indian, challenged s. 497 IPC and s. 198(2) CrPC. Under s. 497 only a man could commit adultery, with another man's wife; the wife was not punishable and the husband could prosecute, but not the other way round.
- **Issue.** Does the offence of adultery violate Arts. 14, 15 and 21?
- **Held.** Section 497 was struck down. It treated a married woman as her husband's property and denied her autonomy and dignity. Adultery remains a ground for divorce, but not a crime.
- **Principle.** The criminal law cannot be used to enforce a moral view that treats a woman as property.
- **After.** The BNS, 2023 does not make adultery an offence †.
- **Used in:** C-14, C-24.

### 8. *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1 · 5 judges (9 March 2018)
- **Facts.** The NGO Common Cause asked the Court to declare the right to die with dignity a part of Art. 21 and to recognise living wills (advance directives) for terminally ill patients.
- **Issue.** Is the right to die with dignity (passive euthanasia and advance directives) a part of the right to life?
- **Held.** Yes. The Court allowed passive euthanasia and recognised advance directives (living wills), with guidelines for their making and execution. Active euthanasia remains illegal. It followed *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011) † on passive euthanasia, which had allowed it in a limited way.
- **Principle.** Dignity includes dying with dignity; an example of the law bending to a moral claim.
- **After.** The Court modified the guidelines in January 2023 † to make advance directives easier to execute and give effect to.
- **Used in:** C-24.

### 9. *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) 9 SCC 1 · 5 judges, 3:2 (22 August 2017)
- **Facts.** Shayara Bano was divorced by *talaq-e-biddat* (instant triple *talaq*) delivered by letter. She challenged the practice and the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 to the extent that it recognised it.
- **Issue.** Is instant triple *talaq* valid under Art. 25 and Art. 14, or arbitrary?
- **Held.** By 3:2 the practice was set aside. Nariman J. (with Lalit J.) held it manifestly arbitrary and violating Art. 14; Kurian Joseph J. held that it is not part of Islamic law; Khehar C.J. and Nazeer J. dissented.
- **Principle.** Personal law is subject to the Fundamental Rights; a practice may be struck down as manifestly arbitrary.
- **After.** The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019 made instant triple *talaq* void and a criminal offence.
- **Used in:** C-08, C-13, C-24.

### 10. *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 1 · 9 judges, unanimous (24 August 2017)
- **Facts.** Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (retd.) challenged the Aadhaar scheme. The Union argued that privacy is not a fundamental right, relying on *M.P. Sharma* (1954) and *Kharak Singh* (1962). A nine-judge bench was set up to decide the point.
- **Issue.** Is the right to privacy a fundamental right?
- **Held.** Yes. Privacy is intrinsic to life and liberty (Art. 21) and part of Part III. The Court overruled *M.P. Sharma* and *Kharak Singh* to the extent that they said otherwise, and **overruled *ADM Jabalpur*** ‡. A restriction must meet three tests: legality, a legitimate aim and proportionality.
- **Principle.** Life and liberty are natural rights that exist before the Constitution and are not given by the State.
- **After.** *Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)* (2018) upheld Aadhaar with limits. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 now regulates personal data.
- **Used in:** C-02, C-09, C-16, C-24.

### 11. *Manoj Narula v. Union of India* (2014) 9 SCC 1 † · 5 judges
- **Facts.** The petitioner asked the Court to bar persons facing criminal charges from being appointed ministers.
- **Issue.** Can a person facing criminal charges be barred from appointment as a minister beyond the disqualifications in the Constitution itself?
- **Held.** The Court declined to add a disqualification not in the Constitution but said that the Prime Minister and Chief Ministers are expected to act in keeping with **constitutional morality** and constitutional trust.
- **Principle.** Constitutional morality binds holders of high office, though the Court cannot add disqualifications that the Constitution does not contain.
- **Used in:** C-24.

## 2B · The analytical school, sovereignty and the basic norm (Unit 3)

### 12. *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* AIR 1950 SC 27 · 6 judges (19 May 1950)
- **Facts.** A.K. Gopalan, a Communist leader, was detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950. He challenged the Act under Arts. 13, 14, 19, 21 and 22.
- **Issue.** What does "procedure established by law" mean in Art. 21? Are Arts. 19, 21 and 22 separate codes?
- **Held.** "Law" meant the law enacted by the State, not natural-justice principles; the Articles were separate codes. The Act was valid except s. 14.
- **Principle.** A positivist reading of Art. 21: any validly enacted procedure will do.
- **After.** *R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (1970) † held that the effect of a law on a right must be tested under all the relevant Articles. *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) overruled the narrow view.
- **Used in:** C-02, C-08, C-09, C-24.

### 13. *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) 2 SCC 521 · 5 judges, 4:1 (28 April 1976)
- **Facts.** During the Emergency (declared June 1975), the President suspended the right to move any court to enforce Arts. 14, 21 and 22 under Art. 359. Many persons were detained under MISA. High Courts had held that habeas corpus was still available.
- **Issue.** Can a detainee seek habeas corpus during the Emergency when Art. 21 is suspended?
- **Held.** By 4:1 (Khanna J. dissenting) no writ could be maintained.
- **Principle.** A positivist view: when the law says there is no remedy, there is none. Khanna J. said that the rule of law survives the Emergency and that life and liberty do not depend on the Constitution alone.
- **After.** The 44th Amendment (1978) provided that Arts. 20 and 21 cannot be suspended. *Puttaswamy* (2017) overruled the decision.
- **Used in:** C-09, C-24.

### 14. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) 1 SCC 248 · 7 judges (25 January 1978)
- **Facts.** The Regional Passport Officer impounded Maneka Gandhi's passport in July 1977 "in the interests of the general public" under s. 10(3)(c) of the Passports Act, 1967, without giving reasons or a hearing.
- **Issue.** Is "procedure established by law" in Art. 21 any procedure laid down by law?
- **Held.** No. The procedure must be **just, fair and reasonable**; Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are linked, and a law that affects a right must satisfy all of them. *Gopalan* was overruled in effect.
- **Principle.** Substantive due process entered Art. 21; the natural-law idea of fairness is read into positive law.
- **After.** Opened the way for the wide reading of Art. 21 (livelihood, dignity, clean environment, speedy trial, privacy).
- **Used in:** C-02, C-07, C-08, C-09, C-24.

### 15. *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973) 4 SCC 225 · 13 judges, 7:6 (24 April 1973)
- **Facts.** The head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala challenged the Kerala Land Reforms Act, 1963 (as amended). During the hearing the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments were also challenged.
- **Issue.** Can Parliament, under Art. 368, amend the Constitution to abridge or take away Fundamental Rights? Is the power unlimited?
- **Held.** *Golaknath* was overruled. Parliament may amend any provision, including Part III, but **cannot alter the basic structure or framework** of the Constitution. The list of features is open: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secular character, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review and others.
- **Principle.** The amending power is limited by the basic structure. Parliament is not an Austinian sovereign.
- **After.** *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975); *Minerva Mills* (1980); *I.R. Coelho* (2007); *SCAORA* (2015) (the NJAC amendment struck down).
- **Used in:** C-02, C-07, C-08.

### 16. *Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India* (1980) 3 SCC 625 · 5 judges, 4:1 (31 July 1980)
- **Facts.** The Government took over Minerva Mills. The owners challenged ss. 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment, which had widened Art. 31C to all Directive Principles and declared that amendments could not be questioned in any court.
- **Issue.** Can Parliament, by the 42nd Amendment, widen Art. 31C to all Directive Principles and bar judicial review of constitutional amendments?
- **Held.** Both sections were struck down. A limited amending power is itself part of the basic structure. The **balance between Parts III and IV** is also basic structure.
- **Principle.** Parliament cannot give itself unlimited power to amend.
- **Used in:** C-08.

### 17. *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* AIR 1967 SC 1643 · 11 judges, 6:5 (27 February 1967)
- **Facts.** The Golaknath family held more than 500 acres in Punjab; the Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953 declared much of it surplus. The Act had been placed in the Ninth Schedule by the 17th Amendment (1964).
- **Issue.** Is a constitutional amendment "law" within Art. 13(2), so that it cannot abridge Fundamental Rights?
- **Held.** Fundamental Rights cannot be amended under Art. 368. The Court applied **prospective overruling** (the earlier amendments stayed valid), which it drew from American practice (*Linkletter v. Walker*).
- **Principle.** As then held, Fundamental Rights are beyond the amending power; prospective overruling avoids disturbing past amendments.
- **After.** The 24th Amendment (1971) reversed it; *Kesavananda* overruled *Golaknath*.
- **Used in:** C-19.

### 18. *State v. Dosso* PLD 1958 SC 533 ‡ · Pakistan Supreme Court
- **Facts.** On 7 October 1958 President Iskander Mirza abrogated the 1956 Constitution and declared martial law. The case arose from a murder conviction under the Frontier Crimes Regulation, which the High Court had held unconstitutional.
- **Issue.** Was the abrogation of the Constitution a valid legal act? Did the old Constitution still govern?
- **Held.** Chief Justice Munir used **Kelsen's** theory to hold that a successful revolution is a law-creating fact. The new order (including the Laws (Continuance in Force) Order, 1958) was valid because it was effective.
- **Principle.** Validity follows efficacy; the *Grundnorm* changes with a successful revolution.
- **After.** *Asma Jilani* (1972) overruled it. Later courts still used the doctrine of necessity to validate coups (for example *Begum Nusrat Bhutto v. Chief of Army Staff*, PLD 1977 SC 657 †).
- **Used in:** A-47, B-15, B-18, B-23, C-10.

### 19. *Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab* PLD 1972 SC 139 ‡ · Pakistan Supreme Court (20 April 1972)
- **Facts †.** Malik Ghulam Jilani was detained under martial law during General Yahya Khan's rule; his daughter, Asma Jilani, challenged the detention. The Court decided the case after Yahya Khan had fallen.
- **Issue.** Was *Dosso* correctly decided? Can a successful usurper's regime be treated as a valid legal order?
- **Held.** Hamoodur Rahman C.J. held *Dosso* unsustainable: Kelsen's theory was not a universally accepted doctrine and had been misapplied to a temporary martial law, and a usurper cannot be legitimate by reason of success alone. The doctrine of necessity can save only some acts of the usurper's regime.
- **Principle.** Efficacy alone does not confer legitimacy; courts are not bound to accept a usurper.
- **Used in:** A-47, B-23, C-10.

### 20. *Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex p. Matovu* [1966] EA 514 · High Court of Uganda †
- **Facts.** In 1966 the Prime Minister suspended the 1962 Constitution and a new Constitution was adopted by a process not provided in the old one. A detainee, Matovu, challenged his detention.
- **Issue.** Was the 1966 Constitution valid, given that it replaced the 1962 Constitution in a way the old Constitution did not provide?
- **Held.** The High Court held that the old Constitution had been abrogated by a revolution and that the new one, being effective, was the valid legal order (applying Kelsen).
- **Note.** I have not confirmed the facts or the bench; state only the holding.
- **Principle.** The effectiveness of the new order decides its validity (Kelsen's view).
- **Used in:** B-23, C-10.

### 21. *Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke* [1969] 1 AC 645 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** After the Unilateral Declaration of Independence by Rhodesia on 11 November 1965, a detention order against Madzimbamuto was renewed under emergency regulations made by the illegal regime. His wife challenged it.
- **Issue.** Were detentions under regulations made by the illegal Rhodesian regime valid?
- **Held.** The Privy Council (Lord Reid) refused to treat the regime as lawful. The UK Parliament remained the lawful sovereign and the regime's acts could not be validated by efficacy or necessity in the circumstances.
- **Principle.** Validity and efficacy are not the same; a regime may be effective in fact and still unlawful.
- **Used in:** B-23, C-10.

## 2C · The historical school: custom, status and contract (Unit 4)

### 22. *Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sathupathy* (1868) 12 MIA 397 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** I could not confirm the facts and have left them out.
- **Issue.** May clear proof of usage outweigh the written text of Hindu law?
- **Held.** Under the Hindu system, clear proof of usage outweighs the written text of the law.
- **Principle.** Custom is a source of law and can override the written texts (the Savigny/historical view that law grows from the people's practice).
- **Used in:** C-04, C-13, C-15.

### 23. *Ramalakshmi Ammal v. Sivanatha Perumal Sethuraya Thevar* (1872) 14 MIA 570 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** Not confirmed; left out.
- **Issue.** How must a custom be proved before it can override the text?
- **Held.** Repeated that clear proof of custom prevails over the text of Hindu law and that custom must be proved by satisfactory evidence.
- **Principle.** A custom must be established by clear and satisfactory evidence.
- **Used in:** C-15.

### 24. *Hurpurshad v. Sheo Dayal* (1876) 3 IA 259 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** Not confirmed; left out.
- **Issue.** What are the requisites of a valid custom, and who must prove it?
- **Held.** A custom must be proved to be **ancient, certain and reasonable**, and the burden lies on the person who asserts it.
- **Principle.** These are the tests of a valid custom in India.
- **Used in:** C-13, C-15.

### 25. *Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma* (2020) 9 SCC 1 † · 3 judges (11 August 2020)
- **Facts.** The question was whether a daughter is a coparcener by birth under the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 as amended in 2005, if her father was not alive on 9 September 2005.
- **Issue.** Is a daughter a coparcener by birth, whether or not her father was alive on 9 September 2005?
- **Held.** Daughters are coparceners by birth, with the same rights as sons, whether or not the father was alive on that date. Earlier contrary decisions were overruled.
- **Principle.** Law moves from status (birth, sex) toward equality; a modern example of Maine's theme and of its limits.
- **Used in:** C-14.

### 26. *Central Inland Water Transport Corporation v. Brojo Nath Ganguly* (1986) 3 SCC 156 †
- **Facts.** The Corporation's service rule allowed it to end the service of a permanent employee by three months' notice, without reasons.
- **Issue.** Is a rule allowing termination of a permanent employee on notice, without reasons, valid?
- **Held.** The rule was unconscionable and violated Art. 14 and s. 23 of the Contract Act, because of the unequal bargaining power between the parties.
- **Principle.** Freedom of contract is limited where parties are unequal: status-like protection returns within contract.
- **Used in:** C-14.

### 27. *LIC of India v. Consumer Education and Research Centre* (1995) 5 SCC 482 †
- **Facts.** Not confirmed; left out.
- **Issue.** Can a State instrumentality such as LIC use unequal bargaining power in standard-form contracts?
- **Held.** I could not confirm the operative holding; only the principle below is stated †.
- **Principle.** A State instrumentality such as LIC must not use its bargaining power unfairly in standard-form contracts (Art. 14); the Preamble and the Directive Principles guide interpretation. The decision also treated the Preamble as part of the Constitution, departing from *Berubari* (1960).
- **Used in:** C-14.

## 2D · The sociological school and social engineering (Unit 5)

### 28. *Muller v. Oregon* 208 US 412 (1908) · US Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Curt Muller, a laundry owner in Portland, was fined for making a woman employee work more than ten hours in a day, which an Oregon law forbade.
- **Issue.** Did the law violate liberty of contract under the Fourteenth Amendment, as in *Lochner*?
- **Held.** The law was upheld unanimously. Louis Brandeis, who argued for Oregon, filed a **"Brandeis brief"** with only two pages of law and over a hundred pages of social and medical data on the effect of long hours.
- **Principle.** Social facts, and not logic alone, decide the reasonableness of legislation. This is the model of the sociological method.
- **Used in:** B-37, C-16, C-17, C-20.

### 29. *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) LR 3 HL 330
- **Facts.** Rylands built a reservoir on his land; water from it flowed through old mine shafts into Fletcher's colliery.
- **Issue.** Is a landowner liable, without negligence, for damage caused by the escape of a dangerous thing from his land?
- **Held.** A person who brings and keeps on his land something likely to do mischief if it escapes is liable for the damage if it escapes, without proof of negligence (Blackburn J.'s rule).
- **Principle.** Pound's jural postulate that those who create risks must bear the loss. Developed further in India as **absolute liability** (the next case).
- **Used in:** B-35, C-16.

### 30. *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (Oleum gas leak) (1987) 1 SCC 395 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** In December 1985 oleum gas leaked from a unit of Shriram Foods and Fertilizer Industries in Delhi. A public interest petition by the lawyer M.C. Mehta asked for the closure of the plant and for compensation.
- **Issue.** What is the rule of liability for harm from hazardous industry?
- **Held.** Bhagwati C.J. refused to follow the exceptions to *Rylands v. Fletcher* and laid down the rule of **absolute liability** for an enterprise engaged in a hazardous or inherently dangerous activity, with compensation correlated to its size and capacity (the "deep pocket" rule).
- **Principle.** A court crafts a new rule suited to Indian conditions: law as social engineering.
- **After.** The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, and the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 31. *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997) 6 SCC 241 · 3 judges (13 August 1997)
- **Facts.** Bhanwari Devi, a village-level worker in a Rajasthan women's development programme, was gang-raped in 1992 after she tried to stop a child marriage. Women's groups, led by Vishaka, filed a petition under Art. 32.
- **Issue.** In the absence of a statute, can the Court lay down binding rules against sexual harassment at the workplace?
- **Held.** Sexual harassment violates Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. In the absence of a law the Court laid down the **Vishaka Guidelines**, relying on CEDAW and Art. 51(c), to apply until Parliament legislated.
- **Principle.** Judicial law-making to fill a gap.
- **After.** The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.
- **Used in:** C-02, C-08, C-16.

### 32. *Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation* (1985) 3 SCC 545 · 5 judges
- **Facts.** Pavement and slum dwellers in Bombay faced eviction after the State announced a plan to remove them in 1981. A journalist, Olga Tellis, and others petitioned.
- **Issue.** Is the right to livelihood part of the right to life, and may pavement dwellers be evicted without notice?
- **Held.** The **right to livelihood** is part of the right to life (Art. 21), because a person cannot live without the means of living. Eviction was allowed after a fair procedure; the Court also directed alternative sites for eligible dwellers. There is no estoppel against a Fundamental Right.
- **Principle.** Law serves social needs.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 33. *Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India* (1984) 3 SCC 161 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** The Bandhua Mukti Morcha, led by Swami Agnivesh, wrote to the Court about bonded labourers in stone quarries near Faridabad (Haryana). The Court treated the letter as a writ petition and appointed commissions to visit the quarries.
- **Issue.** Can the Court act on a letter, and what must the State do for bonded labourers?
- **Held.** The right to live with human dignity covers freedom from bondage. The Court issued directions for release and rehabilitation; and treated the proceedings as non-adversarial.
- **Principle.** Public interest litigation as a tool for social change.
- **After.** The Court's directions pushed enforcement of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976.
- **Used in:** C-14, C-16.

### 34. *Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar* (1980) 1 SCC 81 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Newspaper reports, and a petition by the advocate Kapila Hingorani, showed thousands of undertrial prisoners in Bihar jails, some held longer than the maximum sentence for their alleged offence.
- **Issue.** Is a speedy trial part of Art. 21, and what follows for undertrials held beyond the likely sentence?
- **Held.** A **speedy trial** is part of Art. 21, and free legal aid is part of a fair procedure. The Court ordered release of many undertrials.
- **Principle.** Speedy trial is part of the fair procedure that Art. 21 requires.
- **Used in:** C-17.

### 35. *Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar* (1983) 4 SCC 141 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Rudul Sah was acquitted in 1968 but was kept in jail until 1982.
- **Issue.** Can the Court award money compensation under Art. 32 for unlawful detention?
- **Held.** The Court awarded **compensation** under Art. 32 for the violation of Art. 21, an early and leading example of such an award.
- **Principle.** The Court created a remedy to give effect to a right.
- **Used in:** C-18.

### 36. *Indra Sawhney v. Union of India* 1992 Supp (3) SCC 217 · 9 judges (16 November 1992)
- **Facts.** The challenge was to the Government's 1990 decision to reserve 27 per cent of Central posts for socially and educationally backward classes (the Mandal Commission's recommendation).
- **Issue.** Is reservation for backward classes valid, and within what limits (the 50 per cent ceiling, the creamy layer, promotions)?
- **Held.** Reservation for backward classes under Art. 16(4) was upheld; the **creamy layer** must be excluded; the total reservation should not exceed **50 per cent** except in extraordinary cases; there is no reservation in promotions (a point later changed for SC/ST by the 77th Amendment, Art. 16(4A)).
- **Principle.** Compensatory discrimination is a means of social engineering, balanced against equality.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 37. *Modern Dental College and Research Centre v. State of M.P.* (2016) 7 SCC 353 †
- **Facts.** Private professional colleges challenged state admission and fee rules.
- **Issue.** What test applies to a restriction on the right to carry on a profession, trade or business (Art. 19(1)(g))?
- **Held.** The Court applied the structured test of **proportionality** to restrictions on the right under Art. 19(1)(g): a legitimate aim, a rational connection, necessity and a balance between the right and the restriction.
- **Principle.** Proportionality is a judicial method for balancing interests.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 38. *Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India* (2016) 7 SCC 221 · 2 judges (13 May 2016)
- **Facts.** Petitions by Subramanian Swamy, Rahul Gandhi, Arvind Kejriwal and others challenged ss. 499–500 IPC and ss. 199(1)–(4) CrPC (criminal defamation).
- **Issue.** Is criminal defamation a reasonable restriction on free speech (Art. 19(2)) and consistent with Art. 21?
- **Held.** The provisions were upheld as reasonable restrictions on free speech under Art. 19(2); reputation is a part of Art. 21.
- **Principle.** The Court weighs an individual's interest in speech against another individual's reputation.
- **After.** Criminal defamation is now s. 356 BNS.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 39. *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637 · 3 judges (10 January 2020)
- **Facts.** After 4–5 August 2019, internet and communication services were shut down in Jammu and Kashmir and s. 144 CrPC orders were passed. Anuradha Bhasin, editor of *Kashmir Times*, challenged them.
- **Issue.** Are an internet shutdown and prohibitory orders valid restrictions, and what safeguards apply?
- **Held.** Free speech and the right to carry on trade through the internet are protected under Art. 19(1)(a) and (g). An indefinite shutdown is impermissible; orders must be published, reasoned and subject to proportionality and judicial review.
- **Principle.** Restrictions on speech must be proportionate, reasoned and open to judicial review.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 40. *Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India* (1996) 5 SCC 647 · 3 judges
- **Facts.** Tanneries in North Arcot (Tamil Nadu) discharged untreated effluent into the Palar river and polluted drinking water.
- **Issue.** What principles govern pollution by industry, and who must bear the cost?
- **Held.** The **precautionary principle** and the **polluter-pays principle** are part of the environmental law of the country, as part of sustainable development; the burden of proving that an activity is safe lies on the developer.
- **Principle.** The law serves the social interest in a clean environment.
- **Used in:** C-16.

## 2E · American realism (Unit 6)

### 41. *Lochner v. New York* 198 US 45 (1905) · US Supreme Court, 5:4
- **Facts.** Joseph Lochner, a bakery owner, was convicted of letting an employee work more than sixty hours in a week, which the New York Bakeshop Act forbade.
- **Issue.** May a State limit the hours of bakery workers without violating liberty of contract?
- **Held.** The Act was struck down as an interference with liberty of contract under the Fourteenth Amendment.
- **Holmes's dissent.** "The Constitution does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics": the Court was enforcing its own economic theory.
- **Principle.** The classic example of mechanical (formalist) reasoning that realists and sociologists attacked.
- **Used in:** A-88, B-41, C-19.

### 42. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen* 244 US 205 (1917) · US Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A stevedore was killed while unloading a ship in New York harbour; the question was whether the New York workmen's compensation law could apply.
- **Issue.** Could a State workmen's compensation law apply to maritime work?
- **Held.** The majority held the state law inapplicable to maritime work. **Holmes dissented**: "The common law is not a brooding omnipresence in the sky". Law is made by judges and is the law of some State.
- **Principle.** The realist denial that law exists apart from the courts.
- **Used in:** C-19.

### 43. The collegium cases: *SCAORA v. Union of India* (1993) 4 SCC 441 (Second Judges Case); Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (Third Judges Case); *SCAORA v. Union of India* (2016) 5 SCC 1 (Fourth Judges Case, decided 16 October 2015)
- **Facts.** Arts. 124 and 217 say that the President appoints judges after "consultation" with the Chief Justice. The Second Judges Case (1993) read "consultation" as concurrence and gave primacy to the Chief Justice acting with a collegium of senior judges; the Third (1998, on a Presidential reference) † fixed the collegium for the Supreme Court as the Chief Justice and his four senior-most colleagues. In 2014 Parliament replaced the collegium with a National Judicial Appointments Commission (99th Amendment and NJAC Act).
- **Issue.** Who has primacy in appointing judges, and may Parliament replace the collegium by the NJAC?
- **Held.** The 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act were struck down (4:1, Chelameswar J. dissenting) because they violated the independence of the judiciary, a basic feature.
- **Principle.** A realist example: judges themselves, by interpretation, created the rule on their own appointment.
- **Used in:** B-43, C-19.

## 2F · Marxist and economic approaches (Unit 7)

### 44. *People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India* (Asiad Workers' case) (1982) 3 SCC 235
- **Facts.** The PUDR's investigation showed that workers building the 1982 Asian Games projects in Delhi were paid less than the minimum wage and lived in poor conditions.
- **Issue.** Is non-payment of the minimum wage forced labour under Art. 23, and may a public interest group sue?
- **Held.** Non-payment of the minimum wage is **forced labour** under Art. 23; the Court gave directions to enforce labour laws and held that a public interest petition is maintainable.
- **Principle.** Protection of the weaker class against economic power, a point Marxists make about law.
- **Used in:** C-21.

### 45. *State of Karnataka v. Ranganatha Reddy* (1977) 4 SCC 471 † and *Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd.* (1983) 1 SCC 147
- **Facts.** Both concern Art. 31C and Art. 39(b) ("ownership and control of the material resources of the community are so distributed as best to subserve the common good"). *Sanjeev Coke* upheld the nationalisation of coking-coal mines.
- **Issue.** Do privately owned resources fall within "material resources of the community" in Art. 39(b) and so within Art. 31C?
- **Held.** In *Ranganatha Reddy* the minority opinion of Krishna Iyer J. read "material resources of the community" to include privately owned resources; *Sanjeev Coke* followed that reading.
- **Principle.** Art. 39(b) was read widely in *Sanjeev Coke*; the 2024 decision narrowed the reading.
- **After.** *Property Owners Association* (2024) did not accept that broad view.
- **Used in:** C-18.

### 46. *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (decided 5 November 2024) ‡ · 9 judges
- **Facts.** Owners of old "cessed" buildings in Mumbai challenged Chapter VIII-A of the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Act, 1976, which allowed the State to acquire their buildings to give effect to Art. 39(b). The question went to a nine-judge bench.
- **Issue.** Do privately owned resources count as "material resources of the community" under Art. 39(b)?
- **Held.** Chandrachud C.J., for himself and six judges, held that private resources **can** be treated as material resources in some cases, but not every private resource is one automatically. Factors include the nature of the resource, its effect on the community's welfare, its scarcity and who holds it. Nagarathna J. agreed in part and dissented in part; Dhulia J. dissented.
- **Principle.** The Constitution is not tied to any one economic theory; Art. 39(b) does not mean a socialist economy.
- **Check the split on each issue** in the report before relying on the numbers.
- **Used in:** C-18, C-22.

### 47. *Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India* (decided 25 November 2024) ‡ · 2 judges
- **Facts.** Subramanian Swamy and others challenged the insertion of the words "socialist" and "secular" in the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976), arguing that the Preamble's adoption date (26 November 1949) cannot be altered.
- **Issue.** Could the 42nd Amendment add "socialist" and "secular" to the Preamble, and does the Preamble's adoption date bar this?
- **Held.** Khanna C.J. and Sanjay Kumar J. dismissed the petitions. Parliament's power under Art. 368 extends to the Preamble. "Socialist" in India means a welfare State, not a bar on private enterprise. Secularism is part of the basic structure.
- **Principle.** Parliament's amending power extends to the Preamble, subject to the basic structure.
- **Used in:** B-46, C-21.

### 48. *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.* 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947) · Learned Hand J.
- **Facts.** A barge, the *Anna C*, broke away from its mooring in New York harbour after a tug's crew carelessly shifted the lines. It drifted, collided with a tanker and sank. The bargee was away.
- **Issue.** Was the barge owner negligent in leaving the barge without a bargee, and how is negligence measured?
- **Held.** The owner was partly negligent for leaving the barge without a bargee in working hours.
- **Principle.** The **Hand formula**: liability arises if the burden of precaution (B) is less than the probability of harm (P) times the loss (L); B < P × L. The economic analysis of negligence.
- **Used in:** A-109, B-47, C-22.

### 49. *Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India* (1996) 3 SCC 212 †
- **Facts.** Chemical industries at Bichhri village in Rajasthan discharged toxic sludge that polluted the land and groundwater.
- **Issue.** Who must pay to remedy the harm caused by the industries' toxic waste?
- **Held.** The **polluter-pays principle**, and absolute liability, apply; the industries had to bear the cost of remedial action.
- **Principle.** Economic analysis: the cost of harm falls on the party that caused it (internalising externalities).
- **Used in:** C-22.

### 50. *Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India* (2019) 4 SCC 17 †
- **Facts.** The petitioners challenged provisions of the Code, including those on the classification of creditors and the composition of the committee of creditors †.
- **Issue.** Is the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 constitutionally valid?
- **Held.** The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 was upheld. The Court accepted that the Code's purpose is timely resolution and maximising the value of assets, and that the legislature's economic policy deserves deference.
- **Principle.** Courts defer to economic legislation that passes Arts. 14 and 19(1)(g).
- **Used in:** C-22.
