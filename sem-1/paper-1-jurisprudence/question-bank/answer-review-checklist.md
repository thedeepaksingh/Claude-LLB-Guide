# Paper I · Answer-Review Checklist (use after every mock and every practice answer)

> **Last updated:** 2026-10-28 (IST) · **Law stated as on:** 2026-10-28 · **Branch:** `paper/01-jurisprudence`
> **What this is.** A checklist for marking your own answers against the [model answers](README.md) and for finding what to fix before the exam. **The mark splits are mine.** The university publishes no marking scheme, and I could not find one (see the [gaps document](../../../docs/01-gaps-and-open-questions.md) and the [source-verification log](source-verification-log.md)). Treat them as a guide to what a careful examiner would look for.

---

## 1. Before you review: set the answer beside the question

1. Did you **answer the question that was asked**? Compare your first paragraph with the verb in the question (*explain, discuss, comment, examine critically, distinguish, write a note*).
2. Did you keep to the **exam limit**? Section A ≤ 75 words, Section B ≤ 200 words, Section C: no limit printed ("answer is required in detail"), but the models run 950–1,550 words; 700–1,000 handwritten words (my estimate) with the same headings and the key thinkers fits a 30–35 minute answer.
3. Did you write the **right number of answers**? A: all five. B: two of three. C: three of five. An extra answer in B or C does not earn extra marks.
4. If the question quotes a statement ("Law is the command of the sovereign"; "Jurisprudence is the eye of law"), did you **say whose it is** and **test it** (agree, disagree, or agree in part)?

## 2. Section A (4 marks) — tick each box

| Check | Marks (mine) |
|---|---|
| Definition or meaning in the first sentence | 1 |
| Two or three main points (not a list of ten) | 2 |
| The thinker's name, the work and the year, or one criticism | 1 |
| ≤ 75 words | required |

**Typical losses:** starting with a long introduction; no name or work; a work given the wrong year; writing 120 words and running out of time; for a name question (Manu, Bentham, Duguit, Holmes) giving only the idea and no book.

## 3. Section B (10 marks) — tick each box

| Check | Marks (mine) |
|---|---|
| One-line introduction that answers the question | 1 |
| The theory or concept explained correctly | 3 |
| Thinker, work and year; one example or case | 3 |
| A criticism or an Indian illustration | 2 |
| One-line conclusion | 1 |
| ≤ 200 words | required |

## 4. Section C (20 marks) — tick each box

| Check | Marks (mine) |
|---|---|
| Introduction: meaning, background, the school or thinker | 3 |
| Five or six headings in a logical order | 5 |
| Thinkers, works and years named correctly (at least four) | 5 |
| At least two cases or Indian illustrations | 2 |
| Criticism, comparison with another school, or a balanced view | 3 |
| Conclusion that answers the question set | 2 |

**Typical losses:** a good summary of a theory with no criticism; thinkers listed without their ideas; a quoted statement left untested; an answer that stops after the first half of a two-part question (for example, "features of the analytical school *and* distinguish it from the historical school"; "Austin's division *and* how far Holland and Salmond agree"; "status to contract *and* how far it applies in India"); wrong attribution of a statement.

## 5. Accuracy check

### 5.1 Mistakes I found in my own drafts and corrected

Check that you have **not** made these mistakes. I found them while checking the model answers.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| Austin's positive morality includes a master's orders to a servant | Such orders can be positive law when given under legal rights. Positive morality is law set by general opinion (the law of fashion, rules of honour, international law as Austin saw it) |
| Hart's "no vehicles in the park" example mentions an army lorry on a war memorial | Hart's examples are a bicycle, roller-skates and a toy car. The war-memorial lorry is **Fuller's** reply |
| Hart's "nightmare" and "noble dream" belong to *The Concept of Law* | They come from Hart's 1977 essay "American Jurisprudence through English Eyes: The Nightmare and the Noble Dream" |
| Bentham "added rewards" to sanctions | Bentham recognised rewards as *remunerative* sanctions, alongside punishments |
| Kelsen's basic norm of international law is *pacta sunt servanda* | In his later view it is that states ought to behave as they have customarily behaved |
| Stammler's principles were stated as "two and two" with three items | Use the standard four (two of respecting, two of participating) and say that textbooks word them differently |
| A verbatim quotation was attributed to Kohler | Kohler's idea is that law is judged by how far it advances civilisation; do not put words in his mouth |
| Bentham's *Of Laws in General* was published in 1945 | It was written about 1782 and published in 1970; part of it appeared in 1945 as *The Limits of Jurisprudence Defined* † |

I also expanded and corrected my account of *Dosso* and *Asma Jilani* after checking it against published accounts (see the [log](source-verification-log.md)), and I fixed several cross-references between answers.

### 5.2 Common confusions to avoid

These are standard slips, not errors that I found in my drafts.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience" is from *The Path of the Law* | It is from Holmes's *The Common Law* (1881). The "prophecy" passage is in *The Path of the Law* (1897) |
| Brandeis dissented in *Lochner* | Holmes dissented. Brandeis's contribution is the *brief* in *Muller v. Oregon* (1908) |
| Holland and Salmond define jurisprudence in the same words | Holland: "the formal science of positive law". Salmond: the science of the first principles of the civil law |
| Austin treated international law as law | He called it positive morality (he said it lacks a sovereign and a sanction) |
| Maine's "from status to contract" is in *Early Law and Custom* | It is in *Ancient Law* (1861), chapter V |
| Savigny wanted codification | He opposed an immediate code (against Thibaut, 1814) |
| The Grundnorm is an enacted rule | It is presupposed (a hypothesis of juristic thought); it is not enacted |
| *Madzimbamuto* applied Kelsen's efficacy as *Matovu* did | *Matovu* (Uganda) accepted the new order as effective; the Privy Council in *Madzimbamuto* refused to treat the Rhodesian regime as lawful |
| *Common Cause* (2018) allowed active euthanasia | It allowed **passive** euthanasia and advance directives (living wills) |
| *Dosso* (1958) upheld the coup because of necessity alone | *Dosso* applied Kelsen's efficacy: a successful revolution creates a new legal order. *Asma Jilani* (1972) held it wrongly decided and that Kelsen had been misapplied; necessity was kept only to save some acts |
| *Joseph Shine* is a 2019 decision | It was decided on 27 September 2018 (reported (2019) 3 SCC 39) |
| *Gopalan* is still good law on Art. 21 | *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) overruled its narrow reading; *Puttaswamy* (2017) overruled *ADM Jabalpur* |
