<a id="a-57"></a>
**A-57 · The historical school** · *Related: C-13, C-15*

The historical school, led by Savigny in Germany and Maine in England, holds that law is not made by a sovereign but grows out of the life and history of a people. It is national, not universal; custom is its primary form and legislation secondary; its method is historical. It arose as a reaction against natural law and the codifying rationalism of the Napoleonic Code.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-58"></a>
**A-58 · *Volksgeist*** · *Related: B-25, B-26*

*Volksgeist* is the spirit or common consciousness of the people. In Savigny's theory (*Vom Beruf*, 1814), law is its product: like language, it grows organically and unconsciously from the life and convictions of a people, and is found, not made. Each people has its own law. The legislator only declares what already lives in the people's consciousness.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-59"></a>
**A-59 · Books of Maine and Savigny** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q4*

*Sir Henry Maine:* *Ancient Law* (1861) and *Village Communities in the East and West* (1871) (also *Early History of Institutions*, 1875; *Dissertations on Early Law and Custom*, 1883).

*Savigny:* *Vom Beruf unserer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft* (*Of the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence*, 1814) and *System des heutigen römischen Rechts* (*System of Modern Roman Law*, 1840–49).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-60"></a>
**A-60 · Thibaut–Savigny controversy** · *Related: B-27*

In 1814 A.F.J. Thibaut urged Germany to adopt a general civil code. Savigny replied in *Vom Beruf* that Germany was not ready: legal science was insufficiently developed, and a hasty code would be defective and would freeze the natural growth of law. He asked for historical study first. The German Civil Code came into force in 1900, built by his followers.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-61"></a>
**A-61 · Montesquieu and Hugo as precursors** · *Related: C-13*

Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) held that laws must suit the climate, religion, customs and character of the people for whom they are made. Gustav Hugo (1764–1844) held that positive law grows historically and is not made by a legislator's will; he is regarded as the founder of the historical school. Both influenced Savigny.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-62"></a>
**A-62 · Status to contract** · *Related: C-14*

Maine (*Ancient Law*, 1861): "the movement of the progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract". In early law a person's rights depend on position in the family; in progressive societies the individual makes his own legal relations by free agreement. Criticism: modern welfare law protects classes (workers, consumers, tenants), so some say the movement is back to status.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-63"></a>
**A-63 · Maine's stages of legal development** · *Related: B-28*

Maine described four stages: (1) *themistes*, divine judgments given by the king; (2) customary law, kept by a privileged class; (3) codes (Twelve Tables, Code of Manu); and (4) modern growth through legal fictions, equity and legislation. Most societies, he said, stop at the code stage ("static"); only a few "progressive" societies develop further.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-64"></a>
**A-64 · Legal fictions, equity and legislation** · *Related: B-29*

Maine's three instrumentalities of legal change after the code stage. *Fiction*: an assumption, not open to challenge, that something is so when it is not (adoption treated as natural birth). *Equity*: principles claiming a higher justice beside the civil law (the Roman praetor's edict; the Court of Chancery). *Legislation*: conscious law-making by a recognised authority, the last and most powerful.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-65"></a>
**A-65 · Maine's patriarchal theory** · *Related: C-15*

Maine held that the earliest social unit was the patriarchal family under the eldest male, with absolute power over its members, and that larger groups (clan, tribe, State) grew by extension of the family, with kinship traced through males. McLennan (*Primitive Marriage*, 1865) and Morgan (*Ancient Society*, 1877) argued that early societies were matriarchal. Later anthropology shows many early forms.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-66"></a>
**A-66 · Maine on village communities** · *Related: C-15*

In *Village Communities in the East and West* (1871) Maine studied the Indian village community as a body of related families holding land jointly and administering justice by custom, a surviving picture of early institutions. It was a foundation of legal anthropology. Later research showed that the Indian village was less static and less equal than he supposed.

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-67"></a>
**A-67 · Criticism of Savigny** · *Related: B-25*

The *Volksgeist* is vague and mystical; a society has many groups with conflicting practices. The theory undervalues legislation and glorifies custom, though some customs are unjust (sati, untouchability). Savigny favoured Roman law, which Germany had borrowed. Codes have worked (the German, Swiss and Indian codes). It is conservative and gives no standard for reform.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-68"></a>
**A-68 · Criticism of Maine** · *Related: B-28*

Maine's evidence was narrow (Roman, Hindu, Hebrew). The stages are not universal, and the division into "static" and "progressive" societies is Eurocentric. His patriarchal theory has been challenged by anthropology. Status to contract is not a straight line; welfare States show a reverse movement. He underrated economic factors and the conscious role of legislation.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-69"></a>
**A-69 · Historical and analytical schools: main difference** · *Related: C-12*

The analytical school sees law as the command of the sovereign, studies law as it is by analysis, and favours legislation and codification. The historical school sees law as an organic growth from the life of the people, studies its origin and development, treats custom as the primary form of law, and distrusts hasty codification.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-70"></a>
**A-70 · The historical school in India** · *Related: B-30*

Custom and personal laws are central in India. Art. 13(3)(a) counts "custom or usage having the force of law" as law; courts apply proved custom (*Collector of Madura*, 1868; *Hurpurshad*, 1876). But custom is tested against fundamental rights (*Shayara Bano*, 2017), and harmful practices are removed by statute (Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act, 1987; Art. 17).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-71"></a>
**A-71 · The sociological school** · *Related: C-17*

The sociological school studies law as a social phenomenon, by its function and effect in society. It stresses law in action, treats law as an instrument of social control and change, prefers interests and social purposes to abstract rights, is interdisciplinary and empirical, and is reform-minded. Main thinkers: Jhering, Ehrlich, Pound and Duguit.

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-72"></a>
**A-72 · Comte and Durkheim as background** · *Related: C-17*

Auguste Comte (1798–1857) coined "sociology" and urged the scientific study of society. Émile Durkheim (*The Division of Labour in Society*, 1893) distinguished mechanical solidarity (similarity, in simple societies, with repressive law) from organic solidarity (interdependence by division of labour, with restitutive law). Duguit drew on Durkheim's idea of social solidarity.

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-73"></a>
**A-73 · Jhering** · *Related: B-32*

Rudolf von Jhering (1818–92), in *Der Zweck im Recht* (*Law as a Means to an End*, 1877–83), held that law is a means to a social end and balances individual and social interests (social utilitarianism). In *Der Kampf ums Recht* (*The Struggle for Law*, 1872) he said it is a person's duty to assert his legal rights. He is a forerunner of Pound.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-74"></a>
**A-74 · Ehrlich: living law** · *Related: B-33*

Eugen Ehrlich (*Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law*, 1913) said the centre of gravity of legal development lies not in legislation, juristic science or judicial decision, but in society itself. The "living law" is the law that actually governs social life, as distinct from State law used by courts. Lok Adalats and tribal customary law illustrate it in India.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-75"></a>
**A-75 · Social solidarity** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q5*

Social solidarity is Duguit's basis of law. Human beings live in society and are interdependent, either by similarity of needs or by division of labour. The rule of conduct that arises from this interdependence is objective law (*droit objectif*): a rule is law when the community feels that its breach is a breach of solidarity. The State and legislation are valid only so far as they serve it.

*Word count: 68*

<a id="a-76"></a>
**A-76 · Contribution of Leon Duguit** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q5; Dec 2023 Q4*

Léon Duguit (1859–1928), French jurist (*Les transformations du droit public*, 1913; English *Law in the Modern State*, 1919), grounded law in social solidarity, rejected sovereignty as a fiction and denied individual natural rights in favour of social functions and duties. He treated property as a social function and the State as a cooperative of public services (*service public*). Criticism: his ideas are vague and endanger liberty.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-77"></a>
**A-77 · Duguit: property as a social function** · *Related: C-18*

For Duguit, property is not an absolute right but a social function: the owner holds it for the benefit of society and must use it productively and consistently with social needs, failing which the law may intervene. Echoes in India: Art. 39(b)–(c) (distribution of resources to serve the common good) and Art. 300A; the nine-Judge Bench in *Property Owners Association* (2024) held that not all private resources are community resources under Art. 39(b).

*Word count: 73*

<a id="a-78"></a>
**A-78 · Duguit on the State and sovereignty** · *Related: C-18*

Duguit denied that the State is a sovereign legal person with a will above the law; sovereignty is a metaphysical fiction. The State is the strongest group of persons in society, and its power is limited by objective law. Its authority is justified only so far as it serves social solidarity. He saw the State as a cooperative of public services.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-79"></a>
**A-79 · Social engineering (Pound)** · *Related: B-36, C-16*

Roscoe Pound saw law as social engineering: building as efficient a structure of society as possible by securing the greatest number of competing claims with the least friction and waste. The jurist, like an engineer, studies the social facts, identifies the interests involved, balances them and shapes rules and institutions accordingly.

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-80"></a>
**A-80 · Pound's classification of interests** · *Related: B-34*

An interest is a claim or demand that people seek to satisfy and law must consider. Pound classified interests as *individual* (personality, domestic relations, substance), *public* (of the State as a legal person and as guardian of social interests) and *social* (general security, social institutions, general morals, conservation of social resources, general progress, and individual life).

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-81"></a>
**A-81 · Jural postulates** · *Related: B-35*

Jural postulates are assumptions about what members of a civilised society may expect of each other, which law should secure. Pound's five: no intentional aggression; control of what one has discovered, created or acquired; good faith in dealings; due care to avoid injury; and keeping within bounds things likely to escape and do damage. They change with time and place.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-82"></a>
**A-82 · Pound's stages of legal development** · *Related: C-16*

Pound described five stages: (1) primitive law (custom and blood-feud); (2) strict law (formal rules); (3) equity and natural law (moral standards enter law); (4) maturity of law (equality and certainty); and (5) socialisation of law (law turns to social interests and welfare). The modern law is moving to the fifth stage.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-83"></a>
**A-83 · Law in books and law in action** · *Related: C-16*

Pound's article "Law in Books and Law in Action" (1910) criticised "mechanical jurisprudence", in which courts apply rules logically without regard to results. He asked that the law in action, the actual working and effects of rules, be the test, and that the gap between it and the law in books be reduced by reform and by judges' awareness of social facts.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-84"></a>
**A-84 · Criticism of Pound** · *Related: B-36*

The classification of interests is arbitrary and overlapping (Stone). There is no scale of values for deciding between conflicting interests. "Engineering" suggests control by an elite. The theory reflects early twentieth-century America. "Interest" is vague. Realists said he gave too little empirical work; positivists said he blurs law and policy.

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-85"></a>
**A-85 · Social engineering in India** · *Related: B-36*

The Constitution is an instrument of social engineering: the Preamble's justice and Part IV directives. Courts balance competing interests through reasonable restrictions (Art. 19) and proportionality (*Modern Dental College*, 2016; *Puttaswamy*, 2017), public interest litigation (*Bandhua Mukti Morcha*, 1984), absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*, 1987) and guidelines on sexual harassment (*Vishaka*, 1997).

*Word count: 51*

<a id="a-86"></a>
**A-86 · Pound's works** · *Related: C-16*

*Law in Books and Law in Action* (1910); *The Scope and Purpose of Sociological Jurisprudence* (1911–12); *An Introduction to the Philosophy of Law* (1922); *Interpretations of Legal History* (1923); *Social Control Through Law* (1942); and the five-volume *Jurisprudence* (1959). Pound was Dean of Harvard Law School from 1916 to 1936.

*Word count: 50*

<a id="a-87"></a>
**A-87 · Sociological and analytical schools: difference** · *Related: C-17*

The analytical school studies the form of law (command or norm) by analysis, treats legislation as central and separates law from social facts. The sociological school studies the function and effect of law in society, treats law as an instrument of social control and change, and uses social facts and interests. Pound's "law in action" answers Austin's "law in books".

*Word count: 60*
