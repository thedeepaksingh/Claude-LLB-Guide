## C-19 · Write a detailed essay on the Realist movement of jurisprudence

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q12 ("Write detailed essay on 'Realist Movement' of jurisprudence."). Related: Dec 2024 Q9 (C-20), Dec 2025 Q8 (B-38, Holmes' prophecies), Dec 2022 A2 (A-88, Justice Holmes).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and background 4 · characteristics (Llewellyn's points) 4 · exponents (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank) 7 · criticism 3 · merits, India and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** American Realism, or the Realist movement, was a school of thought in American jurisprudence between about 1900 and 1940. Its central claim is that law must be studied as it actually operates in the courts and in society, not as it appears in books of rules. Its most-quoted starting point is the remark of Justice Holmes that "the prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law". Karl Llewellyn insisted that realism was a **movement**, not a school: it was a shared method and attitude.

**1. Background**
1. **Reaction against formalism.** The late nineteenth-century American law, influenced by Langdell's case method at Harvard and by faith in logic, treated law as a closed system from which results followed by deduction ("mechanical jurisprudence"). Courts used this approach to strike down social legislation, for example in *Lochner v. New York* (198 US 45, 1905), where a maximum-hours law for bakers was held to violate liberty of contract; Holmes dissented.
2. **Pragmatism** (William James, John Dewey): ideas are to be judged by their practical consequences.
3. **Sociological jurisprudence.** Pound's call to study law in action (see C-16).
4. **Holmes's influence.** His writings fixed attention on what courts actually do.
5. **Social upheaval.** The Great Depression and the New Deal showed that law must answer economic needs, and that judges' views on policy mattered.
6. **Behavioural sciences.** Psychology (Freud) and social science encouraged the study of how judges decide.
The movement had its centres at Columbia (Llewellyn, Walter Wheeler Cook, Underhill Moore) and Yale (Arthur Corbin, Thurman Arnold, Felix Cohen, Charles Clark, and later Jerome Frank).

**2. Characteristics: Llewellyn's points.** In *Some Realism About Realism* (44 Harvard Law Review 1222, 1931) Karl Llewellyn listed what realists shared:
1. Law is in flux, and judicial creation of law is a constant fact.
2. Law is a means to social ends, not an end in itself.
3. Society changes faster than law, so the fit of law must be checked often.
4. A temporary separation of "is" and "ought" for the purposes of study.
5. Distrust of traditional legal rules and concepts as descriptions of what courts or people actually do.
6. Distrust of the theory that traditional rule-formulations are the main cause of decisions.
7. Grouping cases and legal situations into narrower categories than the traditional ones.
8. Evaluating any part of law by its effects.
9. A sustained, programmatic attack on legal problems along these lines.
In short: **rule-scepticism**, **judge-centred study**, **empirical and functional method**, and **anti-formalism**.

**3. The main exponents**

*(a) Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (1841–1935)*, Justice of the US Supreme Court (1902–32): the intellectual father of the movement. Works: *The Common Law* (1881); *The Path of the Law* (10 Harvard Law Review 457, 1897).
- **Experience, not logic:** "the life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience". The felt necessities of the time, moral and political theories, public policy and even the prejudices of judges have shaped legal rules more than syllogisms.
- **Prediction theory:** law is a prediction of what courts will do in fact.
- **The "bad man":** to know the law, look at it as a bad man does, caring only for the material consequences (damages, imprisonment) that the law will bring, not for morality.
- **Dissents:** in *Lochner* (the Constitution "does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics") and in *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen* (244 US 205, 1917) (the common law is not "a brooding omnipresence in the sky": it is the articulate voice of some sovereign).

*(b) John Chipman Gray (1839–1915)*, Harvard professor, a forerunner of the movement. Work: *The Nature and Sources of the Law* (1909).
- The law of the State consists of the **rules the courts lay down** for determining legal rights and duties.
- **Statutes are sources of law, not law itself**, until a court interprets and applies them. Gray approved the remark of Bishop Hoadly that whoever has absolute authority to interpret a law is the true lawgiver, not the person who first wrote it. All law is therefore judge-made law.

*(c) Karl N. Llewellyn (1893–1962)*, Columbia professor and chief reporter of the Uniform Commercial Code. Works: *The Bramble Bush* (1930); *Some Realism About Realism* (1931); *The Cheyenne Way* (1941, with E.A. Hoebel); *The Common Law Tradition: Deciding Appeals* (1960).
- **Law is what officials do** about disputes.
- **Paper rules and real rules:** the rules in books are "paper rules"; what courts and officials actually do are the real rules.
- **Rule-scepticism:** rules do not by themselves produce decisions.
- **Law-jobs theory:** every group needs law to (i) clear up trouble cases, (ii) prevent trouble by channelling conduct, (iii) allocate authority and decision procedures, (iv) give direction and incentive to the group, and (v) provide the juristic method, the craft of handling law.
- In his later work (1960) he spoke of the **Grand Style** (judges reasoning openly from the purpose of rules and the "situation sense") as against the Formal Style, and conceded that rules and precedent do constrain judges so that decisions are reasonably predictable.

*(d) Jerome Frank (1889–1957)*, judge of the US Court of Appeals, Second Circuit. Works: *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930); *Courts on Trial* (1949).
- **Fact-scepticism:** the real uncertainty of law lies at the trial, in the facts: witnesses are unreliable, juries unpredictable, and the judge's personality and "hunch" influence the finding of fact. Rule-sceptics (Llewellyn) doubt the rules; fact-sceptics (Frank) doubt the facts.
- **The myth of certainty:** the longing for fixed law is, he said, a childish wish for a father-figure.
- **Actual law and probable law:** actual law is a past decision in a particular case; probable law is a guess at future decisions.
- **Reforms:** better fact-finding, judges trained in psychology and social science, and open acknowledgement of discretion.

*(e) Others:* Joseph Hutcheson ("The Judgment Intuitive", 1929: the "hunch"); Felix Cohen ("Transcendental Nonsense and the Functional Approach", 1935); Thurman Arnold (*Symbols of Government*, 1935).

*(f) Scandinavian realism (a separate European movement).* Axel Hägerström, Karl Olivecrona (*Law as Fact*), A.V. Lundstedt and Alf Ross (*On Law and Justice*, 1953) rejected metaphysical ideas such as "rights", "duties" and "validity" and treated law as psychological and social fact; Ross defined valid law as the rules judges will actually apply and feel bound by. Scandinavian realism is psychological; American realism is behavioural and predictive.

**4. Hart's critique.** In *The Concept of Law*, ch. VII, Hart criticised rule-scepticism: (i) most cases are decided under clear rules; (ii) the prediction theory cannot explain the judge's own position, because a judge applies a rule he accepts and does not predict his own decision (the internal point of view); (iii) the theory is circular, because if law is what courts decide, one must still say what rule courts follow in deciding. Hart's concept of open texture offers a middle way between formalism and rule-scepticism.

**5. Criticism**
1. It overstates uncertainty. Most disputes are routine and predictable, and rules do constrain judges.
2. Law reduced to prediction is of no use to the judge, or to the citizen who needs to know what the law requires.
3. It is circular (law is what judges decide, but on what basis do they decide?).
4. It neglects legislation and constitutional text, which clearly are law.
5. The stress on judges' personality threatens certainty and the rule of law.
6. It gives no normative guidance, since it separates "is" from "ought" and does not say what judges should do.
7. Its empirical basis is weak, and Frank's psychoanalytic claims were speculative.
8. Realists differ among themselves (Llewellyn against Frank).
9. It is mainly about American appellate courts and the common-law system.

**6. Merits.** It exposed the real role of judicial discretion, policy and social context; made law purposive and effects-oriented; encouraged empirical legal research; changed legal education (social science in law schools); shaped New Deal jurisprudence; and influenced the drafting of the Uniform Commercial Code. It was a forerunner of law and economics and Critical Legal Studies.

**7. Illustrations from India.** The Indian legal system shows that judges make law:
- Art. 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts; Art. 142 lets it do "complete justice".
- The basic-structure doctrine (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973) and the expansion of Art. 21 (*Maneka Gandhi*, 1978; livelihood, privacy, dignity, clean environment) are judge-made.
- Absolute liability (*M.C. Mehta*, 1986/1987) and the *Vishaka* guidelines (1997, until the 2013 Act) were rules made by the Court.
- The collegium system of appointing judges was created by the Court's reading of "consultation" in Arts. 124 and 217 (the *Second Judges Case*, 1993; the *Third Judges Case*, 1998), and the Constitution (99th Amendment) Act and the NJAC Act were struck down in the *Fourth Judges Case* (2015).
- Prospective overruling was adopted in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (1967), borrowing from American practice.
The exam caution is that Indian courts do not call themselves "realist"; the point is that their practice illustrates realist claims.

**Conclusion.** Realism redirected jurisprudence from rules to the behaviour of courts and officials, and from logic to experience. Its exaggerations have been corrected by Hart and others, but its basic lesson, that law in action may differ from law in books and that judges do make law, is now generally accepted.

## C-20 · "American modern realism is a combination of the analytical positivists and sociological approaches." Comment

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q9 ("American modern realism is a combination of the analytical positivists and sociological approaches. Comment.")
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** the three approaches in brief 5 · what realism shares with the analytical school 5 · what it shares with the sociological school 5 · differences and verdict 5

### Answer

**Introduction.** The statement says that American Realism has two parents: it takes the analytical positivists' interest in law *as it is* and the sociological school's interest in law *as it works in society*. There is much truth in this. But realism also differs from both, and it is better described as a movement that grew out of dissatisfaction with both formalism and abstract social theory. This answer first states the three approaches, then shows the common ground and the differences, and gives a verdict.

**1. The three approaches in brief**
- **Analytical positivism** (Bentham, Austin, Kelsen, Hart): law is a human creation, "posited" by authority; jurisprudence studies law *as it is*, separately from morality; its method is the analysis of concepts and rules.
- **Sociological jurisprudence** (Ehrlich, Pound, Duguit): law is a social institution; it must be judged by its effect in society; "law in action" matters more than "law in books"; its method is the study of social facts and interests.
- **American realism** (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank): law is what courts and officials actually do; rules are not the sole determinants of decisions; its method is empirical and predictive.

**2. What realism shares with the analytical positivists**
1. **Law as it is.** Realists, like positivists, study actual law, not the ideal of natural law. Holmes's "bad man" wants to know what the law will do to him, not what morality demands.
2. **Separation of "is" and "ought" (for purposes of study).** Llewellyn's fourth point is a temporary separation of "is" and "ought". This is the positivist separation thesis in an empirical form.
3. **Law is posited by human authority.** Gray said law consists of the rules the courts lay down; for him the judge takes the place of Austin's sovereign. Austin looked to the legislator, Gray to the judge.
4. **Rejection of natural law and metaphysics.** Holmes attacked the idea of the common law as "a brooding omnipresence in the sky"; law is the articulate voice of some sovereign.
5. **Sanction and power.** Holmes's prediction theory rests on the force of the State exercised through courts, which resembles Austin's stress on sanctions.
6. **Concern with clarity.** Both reject vague moralising in legal analysis.

**3. What realism shares with the sociological school**
1. **Law in action.** Pound's contrast between law in books and law in action was taken up by the realists as the starting point of their study.
2. **Law as a means to social ends.** Llewellyn's second point says law is a means to social ends and not an end in itself.
3. **Evaluation by effects.** The eighth point says that any part of law should be judged by its effects, as Pound's engineers judged rules by results.
4. **Interdisciplinary approach.** Realists drew on psychology, economics and anthropology (Llewellyn's *The Cheyenne Way*, with the anthropologist E.A. Hoebel), as the sociological school drew on sociology.
5. **Social change.** Both observe that society changes faster than law and demand that law be re-examined.
6. **Rejection of mechanical jurisprudence.** Both attacked the formal, deductive use of rules (as in *Lochner*), and both admired Brandeis's use of social data in *Muller v. Oregon* (1908).
7. **Common ancestors.** Holmes (*The Common Law*, 1881) and Brandeis influenced both.

**4. Differences: why realism is not merely a combination**
1. **Focus on the judge.** The realists concentrate on the behaviour of the judge and official, while sociological jurists focus on social interests and the social effects of rules. Pound himself criticised realists for exaggerating judicial subjectivity.
2. **Rule-scepticism.** Realists doubt that rules determine decisions. Analytical positivists (Austin, Kelsen, Hart) take rules as central, and Hart's *The Concept of Law* (ch. VII) argued that rule-scepticism is untenable. Sociological jurists, in turn, accept rules but want to adjust them.
3. **Fact-scepticism.** Frank's stress on the uncertainty of facts and on psychological factors is peculiar to realism.
4. **Value-neutrality.** The realists professed to study what courts do, not what they should do; Pound's sociological jurisprudence was openly purposive and offered a scheme of social values (interests, jural postulates).
5. **Prediction.** For the realist, the test is predictability of decisions, which neither Austin (command and sanction) nor Pound (social ends) used as a definition.
6. **Method.** Realism is more empirical and behavioural than either parent.
7. **Pragmatism.** Realism has its own philosophical root in pragmatism (James, Dewey), which is neither positivist nor sociological.

**5. The Scandinavian parallel.** Scandinavian realists (Hägerström, Olivecrona, Ross) also combine a positivist rejection of metaphysics with a psychological and social account of law, which shows that this combination is a general feature of realist thought.

**6. Indian illustration.** The Supreme Court's practice shows both strands. The positivist side appears in the stress on the text of the Constitution and in Art. 141 (the law declared by the Court binds). The sociological side appears in public interest litigation, in *Vishaka* and in decisions on livelihood and environment, where the Court looked to the social effect of rules. The realist element appears in the judge-made doctrines, such as the basic structure, absolute liability and the collegium.

**7. Verdict.** The statement is partly right. Realism combines the positivist concern with law as it is and a rejection of natural law with the sociological concern with law's social working and effects. But realism is not a simple sum of the two. It has its own distinctive claims: the centrality of the judge, rule-scepticism and fact-scepticism, and the prediction theory. It is best described as an empirical, judge-centred and sceptical development that borrowed from both, and then went beyond them.

**Conclusion.** American Realism stands between the analytical and sociological schools: it is positivist in its refusal to mix law and morals and sociological in its attention to law in action. Yet it adds a sceptical view of rules and a focus on judicial behaviour, which makes it a separate movement.

## C-21 · The Marxist approach to law: base and superstructure, class struggle, law as the will of the ruling class, and the withering away of law

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 ("Write an essay on economic approach to law."); Dec 2025 Q1 (A-101, "Write the economic definition of Law given by Karl Marx."). Syllabus line: "Economic Approach of Law: Marxist Economic Approach on Law."
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** background and works 4 · historical materialism, base and superstructure 5 · law as class instrument 5 · withering away and later Marxists 3 · criticism and India 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** The economic approach to law holds that law cannot be understood apart from the economic structure of society. Its classical form is the Marxist theory of law, developed from the writings of Karl Marx (1818–83) and Friedrich Engels (1820–95). Marx studied law at the universities of Bonn and Berlin and began as a critic of Prussian law. He did not write a systematic theory of law: the Marxist theory is reconstructed from his works and from later writers (Lenin, Pashukanis and others).

**1. Background and sources**
Marx's thought arose from (i) the Industrial Revolution and its extreme inequality, child labour and long hours; (ii) classical political economy (Adam Smith, David Ricardo); (iii) utopian socialists (Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen); and (iv) Hegel's dialectic, which Marx turned from idealism to materialism. Important works are:
- *On the Jewish Question* (1843): the "rights of man" as the rights of the egoistic individual of bourgeois society.
- *The German Ideology* (with Engels, 1845–46): the statement of historical materialism; law as part of ideology.
- *The Communist Manifesto* (with Engels, 1848): "Your jurisprudence is but the will of your class made into a law for all."
- *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), Preface: the classic statement of base and superstructure.
- *Das Kapital*, vol. I (1867); *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875).
- Engels: *Anti-Dühring* (1878); *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884); letters to Bloch and Schmidt (1890).

**2. The economic definition of law.** In the *Manifesto* (part II), addressing the bourgeoisie, Marx and Engels wrote that their jurisprudence is "but the will of your class made into a law for all, a will whose essential character and direction are determined by the economic conditions of existence of your class". This is the Marxist definition: law is the will of the ruling economic class, made binding on the whole society, and its content is determined by the economic conditions of that class.

**3. Basic ideas of Marxism**
1. **Dialectical materialism.** Change comes through the conflict of opposites (thesis, antithesis, synthesis); material conditions, the way people produce their living, drive history, not ideas.
2. **Historical materialism.** History is the history of changing modes of production: primitive communism, slave society, feudalism, capitalism, socialism, and communism. Each stage ends when the *forces of production* (technology, labour) outgrow the *relations of production* (ownership and class relations), which brings revolution.
3. **Base and superstructure.** The economic structure is the real foundation (the *base*), on which a legal and political *superstructure* rises. Law, the State, politics, religion, morality and ideology belong to the superstructure. Law reflects and protects the economic base; when the base changes, law changes. In the 1859 Preface Marx wrote that it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.
4. **Class struggle.** "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles" (*Manifesto*). In capitalism, the owners of the means of production (the bourgeoisie) confront the wage-labourers (the proletariat).

**4. Law as an instrument of class domination**
1. **Law expresses the will of the ruling class.** The content of law is determined by the economic conditions of that class.
2. **The State is the machinery of the ruling class.** In the *Manifesto*'s phrase, the executive of the modern State is a committee for managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie.
3. **Property, contract and criminal law protect the owners.** Private property and capitalist exchange appear as "natural" rights.
4. **Formal equality hides substantive inequality.** A worker is "free" to make a contract, but must sell his labour to live; equality before the law benefits those who can afford to use it.
5. **Law as ideology.** Law presents class rule as neutral, universal justice, and so secures the consent of the ruled.
6. **Critique of rights.** In *On the Jewish Question*, Marx said that the rights of man are the rights of the egoistic member of civil society, separated from the community.
7. **Origin of law.** Engels (1884) traced the State and law to the rise of private property and the division of society into classes; in a classless society, they would not exist.

**5. Engels' qualification.** In his letters of 1890, Engels explained that the economy is decisive only "in the last instance". Law, politics and ideas have **relative autonomy** and can react on the economic base. This corrected the crude economic determinism of simple versions of Marxism.

**6. Law in the transition; the withering away of law**
1. **Revolution and the dictatorship of the proletariat.** The workers seize power and use the State and law against the former ruling class. In the *Critique of the Gotha Programme*, Marx said that even in the first stage of communism a "bourgeois right" remains: an equal standard is applied to unequal people.
2. **Higher communism.** With abundance and no classes, coercion is unnecessary. The State "withers away" (Engels, *Anti-Dühring*) and law with it, replaced by the administration of things and voluntary cooperation. The principle is: "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs".

**7. Later Marxist developments**

| Thinker | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Lenin** (*The State and Revolution*, 1917) | The State is a machine of class oppression; it must be smashed and replaced by a proletarian State, which then withers away |
| **Evgeny Pashukanis** (*The General Theory of Law and Marxism*, 1924) | The commodity-exchange theory: the legal form (equal legal subjects, rights, contract) arises from the exchange of commodities in capitalism; as commodity exchange disappears, law will wither. He was purged and executed in 1937 |
| **Andrey Vyshinsky** | Law is the will of the ruling class expressed in legislation; "socialist legality" serves the Party-State |
| **Antonio Gramsci** | Hegemony: the ruling class rules by consent, shaped through culture and law, as well as by force |
| **Louis Althusser** | Law as part of the ideological State apparatus, with relative autonomy |
| **E.P. Thompson** (*Whigs and Hunters*, 1975) | The rule of law is "an unqualified human good" because it restrains rulers too |

**8. Criticism**
1. **Economic determinism.** Law is also shaped by religion, culture, politics and morality, and itself shapes the economy (property, contract and company law).
2. **Law is not only a class tool.** Labour laws, factory laws, welfare and consumer laws protect the weak, and the rule of law restrains the powerful (Thompson).
3. **Failed prediction.** The State and law did not wither away in socialist States; they expanded.
4. **Ignores universal functions** of law: criminal law, family law, procedure, dispute settlement.
5. **Class is not the only source of conflict.** Gender, caste, race and religion matter, especially in India.
6. **The stages of history are not universal** and are Eurocentric.
7. **Unfalsifiable.** "The will of the ruling class" can explain any law.
8. **Rights have value.** To call them merely "bourgeois" overlooks that they protect individuals against the State, including socialist States.

**9. Merits.** The approach exposes economic inequality behind formal legal equality; shows the link between law and power; explains the ideological role of law; contributed to labour and welfare legislation, to the sociology of law and to Critical Legal Studies; and influenced socialist and welfare constitutions, including the Directive Principles in India.

**10. India**
- The Preamble describes India as "socialist" (42nd Amendment, 1976), and promises social, economic and political justice. Arts. 38(2), 39(b)–(d), 41, 43 and 43A direct the State to reduce inequality, avoid concentration of wealth and protect workers.
- Land reform (abolition of zamindari, ceilings), bank nationalisation (1969), abolition of privy purses (1971) and the removal of property from fundamental rights (44th Amendment, 1978; Art. 300A) show a movement towards economic restructuring.
- The Supreme Court has said that Indian socialism is not Marxist: it means economic and social justice and does not bar private enterprise (*Dr. Balram Singh v. Union of India*, 25 November 2024).
- Cases: *R.C. Cooper* (1970), *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973), *Minerva Mills* (1980), *Randhir Singh* (1982, equal pay for equal work), *PUDR v. Union of India* (1982, non-payment of minimum wage as forced labour), *D.S. Nakara* (1983), *Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984).

**Conclusion.** The Marxist approach treats law as a part of the superstructure of society, shaped by the economic base and used by the ruling class. It is valuable in showing how law reflects power and economic interest, but it is too narrow and deterministic as a general theory. India has taken from it the idea of economic justice without adopting its class-war and withering-away doctrines.

## C-22 · The economic approach to law beyond Marx: law and economics, with a critical assessment of both approaches

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 ("Write an essay on economic approach to law."). Related: C-21, B-47 (law and economics), A-109, A-110.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning of the economic approach 3 · Marxist approach in outline 4 · law and economics (Coase, Calabresi, Posner) 7 · comparison and criticism 4 · India and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** "The economic approach to law" is a term for any theory that explains law by economic factors or that uses economic reasoning to analyse law. There are two main forms. The **Marxist** approach (C-21) says that law is determined by the economic base of society and serves the ruling class. The **law and economics** approach (the Chicago school) applies economic analysis to legal rules and asks whether they promote efficiency. The syllabus names the Marxist approach; a full essay on the "economic approach" should add the second as a comparison.

**1. The Marxist approach in outline.** Law belongs to the superstructure and reflects the economic base; it is the will of the ruling class made binding on all (*Communist Manifesto*, 1848); formal equality hides real inequality; and in a classless society law will wither away. (For details see C-21.)

**2. Law and economics**
1. **Meaning.** It applies the concepts of microeconomics (scarcity, cost, incentive, rational choice, efficiency) to the study of legal rules and institutions. It is both *positive* (what effect do legal rules have?) and *normative* (what rules would be efficient?).
2. **Roots.** Adam Smith and Jeremy Bentham (utility), and the economists of the Chicago school in the twentieth century.
3. **Ronald Coase**, "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960). The **Coase theorem** says that, if transaction costs are zero, the parties will bargain to the efficient outcome whatever the initial legal rule of liability; where transaction costs are high, the law's allocation of rights matters, and it should be to the party who can avoid the harm at the lowest cost.
4. **Guido Calabresi**, *The Costs of Accidents* (1970): the aims of accident law are to reduce the cost of accidents and the cost of avoiding them; liability should fall on the "cheapest cost avoider". With A. Douglas Melamed (1972) he distinguished property rules, liability rules and inalienability rules.
5. **Richard Posner**, *Economic Analysis of Law* (1973). He argued that the common law tends to produce efficient rules, and he proposed **wealth maximisation** as the normative goal of law. The negligence test of Judge Learned Hand in *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.* (159 F.2d 169, 2d Cir. 1947) is explained in economic terms: a person is negligent if the burden of precautions (B) is less than the probability of harm (P) multiplied by the gravity of the loss (L), that is, B < P × L.
6. **Applications.** Contract (efficient breach), tort (cost of accidents), property (clear titles and transaction costs), crime (deterrence and the cost of punishment), competition law, company law and bankruptcy law.

**3. Marxist approach and law and economics compared**

| Basis | Marxist approach | Law and economics |
|---|---|---|
| Thinkers | Marx, Engels, Lenin, Pashukanis | Coase, Calabresi, Posner |
| Main question | Whose class interest does law serve? | Does law promote efficiency? |
| Role of law | Instrument of class domination and ideology | Instrument for allocating resources efficiently |
| View of capitalism | To be overthrown | Accepted as the framework; markets allocate efficiently |
| Key ideas | Base and superstructure; class struggle; withering away | Coase theorem; cost-benefit; Hand formula; wealth maximisation |
| Method | Dialectical and historical materialism | Neoclassical economics |
| Main works | *Manifesto*; *Capital* | "Problem of Social Cost" (1960); *Costs of Accidents* (1970); *Economic Analysis of Law* (1973) |
| Criticism | Determinism; failed predictions | Ignores distributive justice and values; assumes rational actors |

**4. Criticism of the law and economics approach**
1. **Efficiency is not justice.** It neglects distribution: a rule can be efficient and yet unfair to the poor (Dworkin, "Is Wealth a Value?", 1980).
2. **Unrealistic assumptions.** People are not always rational; behavioural economics shows systematic biases.
3. **Transaction costs are never zero**, so the Coase theorem is a benchmark, not a description.
4. **Values cannot be priced.** Human dignity, liberty and equality cannot be reduced to a cost-benefit calculation.
5. **The common-law efficiency claim** is empirically doubtful.
6. **It takes existing wealth distribution as given**, which Marxists and others say shows a bias towards the status quo.

**5. Criticism of the Marxist approach (summary).** Determinism and reductionism; law is not only a class tool; the failed prediction of the withering away of the State; class is not the only source of conflict; the stages of history are not universal; and the theory is unfalsifiable (see C-21).

**6. The economic approach in India**
1. **Constitutional text.** The Preamble ("socialist"; social, economic and political justice) and Directive Principles (Arts. 38, 39, 41, 43) express a welfare-oriented economic approach.
2. **Judicial decisions.** The Supreme Court has used the Directive Principles to enrich fundamental rights (*Randhir Singh*, 1982; *D.S. Nakara*, 1983), has held that socialism in India does not bar private enterprise (*Dr. Balram Singh*, 2024), has upheld liberalisation and disinvestment as matters of economic policy (*Delhi Science Forum*, 1996; *BALCO Employees' Union*, 2002) and has restricted the reach of Art. 39(b) to resources that are material resources "of the community" (*Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra*, 5 November 2024).
3. **Efficiency-oriented statutes.** The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (upheld in *Swiss Ribbons Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India*, 2019) aims at timely resolution and maximising asset value; the Competition Act, 2002 uses economic analysis of market power; the "polluter pays" principle (*Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India*, 1996) places the cost of harm on the party causing it. These are examples of economic reasoning in Indian law.
4. **Labour law.** The four Labour Codes (the Code on Wages, 2019, and the Industrial Relations Code, the Code on Social Security and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code of 2020) were brought into force on 21 November 2025. They raise the Marxist question of whose interests labour law serves, and the efficiency question of flexibility and compliance costs.
5. **Gig work.** The treatment of platform workers revives the issue of formal freedom of contract against real bargaining power; the Code on Social Security, 2020 recognises gig and platform workers.

**Conclusion.** The economic approach shows that law is bound up with the economy, in two different ways: Marxists see law as an expression of economic power, while law and economics sees it as a means of allocating resources efficiently. Neither is a complete theory of law. But each has contributed lasting ideas, such as the link between law and class interest and the use of cost-benefit reasoning, and both are visible in Indian constitutional and statutory law.

## C-23 · Compare the main schools of jurisprudence: natural law, analytical, historical, sociological, realist and Marxist

**Asked as:** not asked as such in 2022–25; the comparison parts of Dec 2024 Q13 (analytical v. historical) and Dec 2023 Q7 (Austin v. Kelsen) are tested every year. This answer is the cross-unit revision essay; it is the likeliest "unseen" long question.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and approach 2 · table of comparison 8 · explanation of the differences 6 · assessment and Indian illustrations 4

### Answer

**Introduction.** Each school of jurisprudence answers a different question about law. Natural law asks what law *ought* to be; the analytical school asks what law *is*; the historical school asks how law *grew*; the sociological school asks what law *does* in society; the realists ask what courts *actually do*; and the Marxists ask *whose interests* law serves. The schools are therefore not so much rivals as partial views of one subject, and each arose as a reaction to the one before it.

**1. Chain of reaction**
- **Natural law** (Greeks, Stoics, Aquinas, Grotius, Locke) was attacked by **Bentham and Austin**, who founded the **analytical school** (law as command; separation of law and morals).
- **The historical school** (Savigny, Maine) reacted against both natural law's universal reason and the rationalist codifiers: law is the product of a people's history.
- **The sociological school** (Jhering, Ehrlich, Pound, Duguit) reacted against the formalism of the analytical school and the conservatism of the historical school, and asked what law does in society.
- **American realism** (Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank) reacted against formalism, and turned attention to judges.
- **Marxism** challenged all of them as ignoring economic power.
- **Revival of natural law** (Stammler, Kohler, Fuller, Finnis) and **Hart's** modern positivism are later restatements.

**2. Comparison table**

| Basis | Natural law | Analytical | Historical | Sociological | Realist | Marxist |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Question asked** | What ought law to be? | What is law? | How did law grow? | What does law do? | What do courts do? | Whose interest does law serve? |
| **Main thinkers** | Aristotle, Cicero, Aquinas, Grotius, Locke; Stammler, Kohler, Fuller, Finnis | Bentham, Austin, Kelsen, Hart | Savigny, Maine | Jhering, Ehrlich, Pound, Duguit | Holmes, Gray, Llewellyn, Frank | Marx, Engels, Lenin, Pashukanis |
| **Source of law** | Reason, nature or God | The sovereign (Austin); the Grundnorm (Kelsen); the rule of recognition (Hart) | Custom; the *Volksgeist* | Social needs and interests; living law | Judicial decision | The economic base and the ruling class |
| **Nature of law** | Higher moral order | Command, norm or rule | Organic growth of a people | Social control; social engineering | What courts and officials do | Superstructure; instrument of class power |
| **Law and morals** | United: an unjust law is not true law | Separate | Linked through the national spirit | Linked through social ends | Separate for study | Morality is itself class-determined |
| **Method** | Reason and philosophy | Analysis of concepts | Historical study | Study of social facts | Empirical and predictive | Dialectical and historical materialism |
| **Role of legislation** | Must conform to natural law | Chief source of law | Secondary; records custom | A tool of social engineering | Source; not law until applied by courts | Expresses ruling-class interest |
| **Role of the judge** | Applies and discovers natural law | Applies the law (discretion in the penumbra: Hart) | Declares custom | Balances interests | Makes law | Serves the ruling class |
| **Main strength** | Gives a standard to criticise unjust law; basis of human rights | Clarity; separation of "is" from "ought" | Respect for custom; historical perspective | Links law with society; purposive | Exposes judicial discretion | Exposes economic power behind law |
| **Main weakness** | Vague; content disputed | Narrow; ignores society and morality | Vague *Volksgeist*; undervalues legislation | No scale of values | Overstates uncertainty | Determinism; failed predictions |
| **Indian reflection** | Basic structure; Art. 21; *Puttaswamy* | Codes; Art. 141; legislative supremacy within limits | Custom; personal laws; Art. 13(3)(a) | Part IV; PIL; *Vishaka*; proportionality | Judge-made doctrines; collegium | Directive Principles; labour laws |

**3. Key contrasts**
1. **Natural law v. analytical.** The first joins law and morality; the second separates them. The Hart–Fuller debate (1958) is the modern form of the dispute (see C-24).
2. **Analytical v. historical.** The analytical school sees law as the command of the sovereign and favours codification; the historical school sees law as custom growing from the people and distrusts hasty codification (Thibaut–Savigny, 1814). (See C-12.)
3. **Analytical v. sociological.** The analytical school studies the form of law; the sociological school its function. Pound's "law in action" is the answer to Austin's "law in books".
4. **Sociological v. realist.** Both study law in action, but realists focus on the judge and doubt rules, while sociological jurists focus on interests and want to improve law (see C-20).
5. **Marxist v. all others.** Marxists treat law as secondary to the economy, whereas the other schools treat law as a more independent subject.
6. **Kelsen v. Austin.** Kelsen replaces the sovereign's command by a hierarchy of norms and a presupposed Grundnorm; Austin treats law as a fact of power (see B-14).
7. **Hart v. realists.** Hart answers rule-scepticism by arguing that rules are standards used from the internal point of view.

**3A. A chronology to fix the order in memory**
- Classical natural law: Aristotle (4th century BC), Cicero (1st century BC), Aquinas (13th century), Grotius (1625), Locke (1689).
- Bentham's *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789); Austin's *Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832).
- Savigny's *Vom Beruf* (1814); Maine's *Ancient Law* (1861).
- Marx and Engels, *Communist Manifesto* (1848); Marx's *Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859).
- Jhering's *Der Zweck im Recht* (1877–83); Holmes's *The Common Law* (1881) and *The Path of the Law* (1897).
- Stammler's *Theory of Just Law* (1902); Salmond's *Jurisprudence* (1902); Gray's *Nature and Sources of the Law* (1909); Kohler's *Philosophy of Law* (1909).
- Pound's sociological jurisprudence (1910–12); Ehrlich (1913); Duguit (1913).
- Realism: Frank (1930), Llewellyn (1930–31, 1941); Kelsen's *Pure Theory* (1934, 1960).
- Hart's *Concept of Law* (1961); Fuller's *Morality of Law* (1964); Rawls (1971); Posner (1973); Finnis (1980).
(Several of these dates are from memory and are marked for checking in the [source-verification log](source-verification-log.md).)

**4. Assessment.** No one school is complete. A modern definition of jurisprudence, as in Stone's "lawyer's extroversion" or Jerome Hall's integrative jurisprudence (law as form, value and fact), draws on all of them: the analytical school for structure, natural law for values, the historical school for roots, the sociological school for function, the realists for the working of courts and Marxism for the connection with power. The Indian Constitution reflects this mixture, since it has a written hierarchy of norms (Kelsen), enforceable rights with natural-law roots (*Kesavananda*, *Puttaswamy*), recognition of custom (Art. 13(3)(a)), social and economic directives (Part IV), and a judiciary that actively shapes law (realism).

**Conclusion.** The schools ask different questions and give different answers. Together they give a fuller picture of law as a system of rules, a product of history, an instrument of social purpose, the work of courts, a vehicle of justice and an expression of economic power.

## C-24 · Law and morality: their relation in natural law, positivism and modern debates, with Indian illustrations

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q6 (B-03, "Law and Morals"). Related: Dec 2025 Q12 (C-04), Dec 2024 Q7 (B-08, revival of natural law), Dec 2024 Q8 (B-16, Hart).
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 35 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning and differences 5 · theories of the relation (natural law, positivism) 6 · Hart–Fuller, Hart–Devlin and Radbruch 4 · Indian illustrations 3 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The relation between law and morality is one of the oldest questions in jurisprudence. Law is the body of rules enforced by the State; morality is the body of standards of right and wrong in conduct, which rest on conscience, religion and social opinion. Each school answers the question differently, and the answer affects how a judge decides, how a citizen may criticise a law, and whether an unjust law is binding.

**1. Differences between law and morality**

| Basis | Law | Morality |
|---|---|---|
| Source | State: legislation, precedent, custom recognised by courts | Conscience, religion, social opinion |
| Sanction | Organised and external (fine, imprisonment, damages) | Inner (guilt) and social disapproval |
| Subject matter | Mainly external conduct | Motives, intentions and inner life as well |
| Certainty | Usually written and definite | Less definite and varies from person to person |
| Enforcement | By courts and officials | No official machinery |
| Change | By deliberate amendment | Slowly with social attitudes |
| Scope | Does not cover all morality (ingratitude is not punishable) | Wider than law |

**2. Overlap.** Many legal rules have a moral basis (murder, theft, fraud are both wrong and illegal). Some laws are morally neutral (driving on the left). Some immoral conduct is lawful, and some lawful acts may be immoral. Law is often described as the "ethical minimum" (Jellinek), but not all legal rules fit this.

**3. Natural-law view: law and morality are united**
- Aquinas: a human law that conflicts with natural law is "a perversion of law" and has no binding force in conscience.
- Cicero: true law is right reason in agreement with nature.
- The revival (Stammler, Kohler, Fuller, Finnis, Radbruch) keeps the link in modern forms. **Fuller** (*The Morality of Law*, 1964) said law has an *inner morality* of eight requirements (generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy, congruence of rule and official action); a system that fails them altogether does not produce law. **Finnis** (1980) grounds law in basic human goods. **Radbruch** (1946) said that where the conflict of a statute with justice reaches an intolerable degree, the statute must yield to justice.

**4. Positivist view: law and morality are separate**
- **Bentham:** the existence of law is one thing, its merit another; law can be criticised by the test of utility.
- **Austin:** "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another." An unjust law is still law. The test of its goodness is utility.
- **Kelsen:** the pure theory excludes ethics and politics; a norm is valid because it was created in a manner authorised by a higher norm, not because it is just.
- **Hart:** the "separation thesis" is that there is no necessary connection between law and morals, though he accepts a **minimum content of natural law** (rules on violence, property and promises, based on the facts of human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources and limited understanding and strength of will). Hart's reason for the separation is practical and moral: if we say that wicked laws are not law, we blur the issue of whether to obey them.

**5. Historical and sociological views.** Savigny: law grows out of the common consciousness of the people, so it is connected with the morality of the people. Pound: morality is one of the means of social control and law must secure the social interest in general morals. Ehrlich: the living law is in great part the moral and customary order of society. Realists: they separate "is" and "ought" for study, but regard moral and social attitudes of judges as influencing decisions. Marxists: morality, like law, is part of the superstructure and class-determined.

**6. The modern debates**
1. **Hart–Fuller (1958).** Hart's "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" (71 Harvard Law Review 593) and Fuller's reply (71 HLR 630). The *grudge informer* case: after the Second World War a German woman who had denounced her husband for insulting Hitler was prosecuted. The Nazi statute under which she had acted was argued to be no law. Hart said it was law, though too evil to be obeyed, and that it is better to say so honestly. Fuller said that a system that has lost its inner morality is not law at all. The Radbruch formula and the Nuremberg trials show the other side.
2. **Hart–Devlin (1957–63).** After the Wolfenden Report (1957), Lord Devlin argued that society has the right to use the criminal law to protect its shared morality. Hart, relying on J.S. Mill's harm principle, argued that private immorality is not the business of the law. *Shaw v. DPP* ([1962] AC 220), in which the House of Lords recognised an offence of conspiracy to corrupt public morals, is usually cited as reflecting Devlin's view.
3. **Hart–Dworkin.** Dworkin argued that judges apply moral principles as part of law (*Riggs v. Palmer*, 115 NY 506, 1889). Hart's *Postscript* (1994) accepted that a rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria ("inclusive" or "soft" positivism); Raz denies this (the "sources thesis").
4. **Hard cases.** In *R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884) 14 QBD 273, shipwrecked sailors killed a cabin boy and ate him to survive, and the court held that necessity is no defence to murder. The case shows the court asserting a moral limit that the law would not bend.

**7. Indian illustrations**
1. **From positivism to a moral reading of Art. 21.** *A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras* (1950) treated "procedure established by law" as any procedure laid down by enacted law. *Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India* (1978) required that the procedure be fair, just and reasonable, thus bringing in a moral standard. *ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla* (1976) (a positivist outcome) was overruled in *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017), which treated life and liberty as natural rights that exist before the Constitution.
2. **Constitutional morality v. social morality.** *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) read down Section 377 IPC as regards consensual acts between adults, preferring constitutional morality to prevailing social morality. This reversed *Suresh Kumar Koushal v. Naz Foundation* (2014), which had upheld the section. In *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) the Court struck down the offence of adultery, saying that the criminal law cannot be used to enforce a moral view that treats a wife as the husband's property. The Court's concept of constitutional morality was also discussed in *Manoj Narula v. Union of India* (2014).
3. **Moral questions of life and death.** *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) recognised the right to die with dignity (passive euthanasia and living wills) as part of Art. 21.
4. **Custom and morals.** *Shayara Bano v. Union of India* (2017) set aside instant triple talaq (3:2).
5. **Law and morality in the statute book.** Offences against decency and morality (for example, obscenity) and the "public order, decency or morality" ground for restricting speech in Art. 19(2) incorporate moral standards into the law.

**8. Assessment.** The two sides each state part of the truth. Law cannot be identified with morality, because law may be unjust and a citizen must be able to say so. But law cannot be wholly cut off from morality, because the content of most law rests on moral ideas, because judges often have to decide hard cases by moral reasoning, and because most constitutions (including India's) make fundamental rights, which are moral claims, a test of validity. The modern Indian position is a blend: a positivist method for identifying law (enactment, hierarchy of norms) combined with natural-law-style limits (basic structure, fundamental rights, constitutional morality).

**Conclusion.** The relation between law and morality is one of overlap but not identity. Natural-law thinkers join them, positivists separate them, and modern writers such as Hart and Fuller argue about how far. In India, the courts have increasingly tested law by constitutional morality while keeping the distinction between what law is and what it should be.
