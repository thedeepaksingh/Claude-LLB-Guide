<a id="a-33"></a>
**A-33 · Meaning of legal positivism** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q3*

Legal positivism is the theory that law is law because it was "posited" (laid down) by human authority in a way recognised by the legal system, not because it is just. The existence of law is one thing, its merit another (Austin). Its main versions are Bentham and Austin (command), Kelsen (norms) and Hart (rules). Its opponents are natural-law writers, Fuller and Dworkin.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-34"></a>
**A-34 · Contribution of Bentham** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q4; Dec 2023 Q2; Dec 2022 Q4*

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) founded utilitarianism (the greatest happiness of the greatest number) and the analytical school. He separated expositorial from censorial jurisprudence, treated law as the command of the sovereign, rejected natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts", urged codification, and influenced Austin and the Indian codes. Works: *Fragment on Government* (1776); *Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789).

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-35"></a>
**A-35 · Sanction** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q1*

A sanction is the evil or compulsion that follows disobedience of a rule and gives it force. For Austin it is what turns a wish into a command; for Kelsen law is a coercive order that attaches sanctions to conduct; Hart treats it as a natural necessity, not a definition of law. Kinds: punishment (criminal law), compensation and enforcement (civil law), and nullity.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-36"></a>
**A-36 · Is international law law, according to Austin?** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q5*

No. For Austin law properly so called is the command of a sovereign. International law is not set by a sovereign to subjects, and no sovereign stands over states; its only sanction is the opinion of nations. He called it "positive international morality", law only by analogy. The criticism is that states regard it as binding and it has sanctions (reprisals, war and Security Council action). Kelsen and Hart treat it as law.

*Word count: 73*

<a id="a-37"></a>
**A-37 · Command (Austin)** · *Related: C-08*

A command is a wish expressed by a determinate person that another shall do or forbear, with an evil threatened if the wish is disobeyed. The power and purpose to inflict the evil makes a wish a command. Command, duty and sanction are correlative. A law is a general command; an occasional command (to shut the window now) is not law.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-38"></a>
**A-38 · Sovereign (Austin)** · *Related: B-17*

A determinate human superior, not in the habit of obedience to a like superior, who receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, is sovereign. Sovereignty is determinate, independent, indivisible and legally illimitable. In a democracy the sovereign is the electorate with the legislature. The theory fits poorly with federal States and written constitutions, as in India.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-39"></a>
**A-39 · Austin's classification of laws** · *Related: C-09*

Laws properly so called are commands: divine law (God to men) and human law. Human law is positive law (set by political superiors) or positive morality (set by others). Laws improperly so called are by analogy (fashion, honour, international law) or by metaphor (laws of science). Only positive law is the province of jurisprudence.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-40"></a>
**A-40 · Positive morality (Austin)** · *Related: C-09*

Positive morality is the aggregate of rules not set by political superiors acting as such: the rules of a club, of honour and of fashion, and (for Austin) international law and constitutional law. Some, like a club's rules, are commands and so laws properly so called; others are laws by analogy. None is positive law, so none lies within jurisprudence.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-41"></a>
**A-41 · How was Kelsen influenced by Austin?** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q2*

Kelsen followed Austin in being a positivist: he studied law as it is, separated it from morality, and treated sanction (coercion) as essential to law. He shared Austin's aim of a general theory of law. But he replaced the sovereign's psychological command with a valid norm, and sovereignty with a hierarchy of norms resting on the Grundnorm, so he improved on Austin.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-42"></a>
**A-42 · Ground norm (Grundnorm)** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q3*

The Grundnorm is Kelsen's basic norm: the highest norm of a legal system from which all other norms derive their validity. It is not enacted but presupposed: "the historically first constitution ought to be obeyed". It gives the system unity and is presupposed only for a legal order that is by and large effective. Critics call it a fiction.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-43"></a>
**A-43 · Pure theory of law** · *Related: C-10*

Kelsen's Pure Theory (*Reine Rechtslehre*, 1934; second edition 1960) seeks a science of law free from politics, ethics, sociology, psychology and natural science. Law is studied as it is, in terms of norms ("ought"), not as it ought to be or as it works in society. Its main ideas are the hierarchy of norms and the Grundnorm.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-44"></a>
**A-44 · Hierarchy of norms (*Stufenbau*)** · *Related: C-10*

A norm is valid because it was created in the way authorised by a higher norm. The legal order is therefore a pyramid: Grundnorm, Constitution, statutes, delegated legislation, judicial and administrative decisions and individual acts. In India: Constitution, statutes, rules and notifications, orders. Judicial review under Art. 13 and Arts. 32 and 226 checks each lower norm against a higher one.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-45"></a>
**A-45 · *Sein* and *Sollen* ("is" and "ought")** · *Related: C-10*

Kelsen distinguished the world of *Sein* ("is"), studied by natural science through causal laws (if A, then B will follow), from the world of *Sollen* ("ought"), studied by legal science through norms (if A, then B ought to follow). A legal norm is typically: "if a person commits theft, a sanction ought to be applied". The link in law is imputation, not causation.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-46"></a>
**A-46 · Validity and efficacy (Kelsen)** · *Related: B-18*

A norm is *valid* if it was created in accordance with a higher norm; it is *efficacious* if it is in fact obeyed and applied. For Kelsen, a legal order must be by and large efficacious for its Grundnorm to be presupposed, so efficacy is a condition of validity but not its source. When a revolution succeeds, a new Grundnorm is presupposed.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-47"></a>
**A-47 · *State v. Dosso*** · *Related: B-23*

*State v. Dosso* (PLD 1958 SC 533, Pakistan): after the President abrogated the 1956 Constitution and martial law was declared, Chief Justice Munir, relying on Kelsen, held that a successful revolution is a law-creating fact. The Supreme Court overruled *Dosso* in *Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab* (1972), holding that Kelsen had been misapplied and that a usurper is not made lawful by success alone.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-48"></a>
**A-48 · Primary and secondary rules (Hart)** · *Related: B-20*

*Primary rules* impose duties to do or abstain from acts. *Secondary rules* confer powers and concern the primary rules: the rule of recognition (identifies valid rules, curing uncertainty), the rule of change (makes and alters rules, curing static character) and the rule of adjudication (decides breaches, curing inefficiency). Hart called the union of the two "the key to the science of jurisprudence" (*Concept of Law*, 1961).

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-49"></a>
**A-49 · Rule of recognition** · *Related: B-20*

The rule of recognition is Hart's ultimate criterion of validity in a legal system, by which officials identify the rules of the system (for example, "what the Queen in Parliament enacts is law"). It is a social rule that exists as a fact in the practice of officials who accept it from the internal point of view. Unlike Kelsen's Grundnorm, it is not presupposed.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-50"></a>
**A-50 · Internal point of view** · *Related: B-24*

The internal point of view is that of a participant who accepts a rule as a standard for his own conduct and for criticising others. The external point of view is that of an observer who merely notes regularities and threats. Hart held that law cannot be understood without the internal point of view; a legal system exists where officials accept its secondary rules from it.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-51"></a>
**A-51 · Open texture of law** · *Related: B-24*

Every general rule has a core of settled meaning and a penumbra of doubt. Hart's example: "No vehicles in the park" plainly bans cars but is uncertain for bicycles or roller-skates. In the penumbra the judge must use discretion and so makes law within limits. Open texture is Hart's middle path between formalism and rule-scepticism.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-52"></a>
**A-52 · Minimum content of natural law (Hart)** · *Related: C-11*

Hart accepts that, given five facts about human beings (vulnerability, approximate equality, limited altruism, limited resources, and limited understanding and strength of will), any workable legal system will contain basic rules restricting violence, protecting property and enforcing promises. This is a modest concession to natural law, which leaves the separation of law and morals untouched.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-53"></a>
**A-53 · Hart–Fuller debate** · *Related: B-12*

In 71 Harvard Law Review 593 and 630 (1958), Hart defended the separation of law and morals; Fuller replied. In the *grudge informer* case a woman who denounced her husband under a Nazi statute was prosecuted after the war. Hart said the statute was law but too evil to obey; Fuller said a system that lacks the "inner morality" of law is not law.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-54"></a>
**A-54 · Hart–Devlin debate** · *Related: C-11*

After the Wolfenden Report (1957), Lord Devlin argued that society may use criminal law to protect its shared morality. Hart, following J.S. Mill's harm principle, said private immorality is not the law's concern (*Law, Liberty and Morality*, 1963). The Indian echo is *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018), which preferred constitutional morality to social morality.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-55"></a>
**A-55 · Hart–Dworkin debate** · *Related: C-11*

Dworkin (*Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977) argued that law includes principles as well as rules (as in *Riggs v. Palmer*, 1889) and that judges have no strong discretion; they must find the right answer. Hart's *Postscript* (1994) defended "soft" or inclusive positivism: the rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria, and discretion exists in hard cases.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-56"></a>
**A-56 · The gunman situation (obliged and obligation)** · *Related: C-11*

Hart's criticism of Austin: a gunman who orders a clerk to hand over money *obliges* him, but does not give him an *obligation*. Austin's law, a command backed by threat, explains being obliged, not having an obligation, which implies acceptance of a rule as a standard. People obey law also because they regard it as binding.

*Word count: 56*
