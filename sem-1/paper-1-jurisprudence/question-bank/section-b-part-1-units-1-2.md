## B-01 · Evaluate the need for the study of jurisprudence

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q6 ("Evaluate the need for the study of Jurisprudence."). 20-mark form: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 1 · five or six reasons with examples 6 · Indian illustration 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

Jurisprudence is the systematic study of the nature, sources and concepts of law and the ends it serves. Reasons for its study:

1. **Foundation.** The ideas used in every branch of law (right, duty, ownership, possession, liability, personality) are explained in jurisprudence.
2. **Legal reasoning.** It trains the student to analyse concepts and argue from principle.
3. **Interpretation.** The choice between a literal and a purposive reading of a statute or the Constitution is a jurisprudential choice.
4. **Guidance for judges.** *Gopalan* (1950) read "procedure established by law" as enacted procedure (a positivist reading), but *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) required fair, just and reasonable procedure. *Puttaswamy* (2017) treated life and liberty as natural rights and overruled *ADM Jabalpur* (1976).
5. **Law reform.** Codification and the new criminal codes (BNS, BNSS and BSA, 2023) rest on theories of what law should be.
6. **Link with society and justice.** The sociological, realist and Marxist approaches keep law tied to social purpose.
7. **New problems.** Data privacy and artificial intelligence are met by extending old ideas of right and harm.

It is, as Laski is said to have called it, the "eye of law": it shows the purpose behind the rules.

## B-02 · "Jurisprudence is the eye of law." Discuss

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q8; Dec 2022 Q7 (both: "'Jurisprudence is the eye of law.' Discuss."). 20-mark form: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning of the statement and its source 2 · what the "eye" sees 4 · examples 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

The saying is commonly credited to Harold J. Laski. It means that jurisprudence gives the lawyer a vision of law as a whole: as the eye lets a person see, jurisprudence lets a lawyer see the purpose, principles and working of rules that would otherwise be mere text.

**What the "eye" shows**
1. *What law is:* its nature, sources and basic concepts (Austin, Kelsen, Hart).
2. *How law grew:* the historical view (Savigny, Maine).
3. *What law ought to be:* justice and natural-law standards.
4. *How law works:* its social effect (Pound, Ehrlich) and what courts do (Holmes, Llewellyn).
5. *Whom law serves:* the economic view (Marx).

**Examples.** A lawyer who knows only the Indian Penal Code or Contract Act sees rules, but one who knows jurisprudence sees why liability, consent and intention matter. In *Gopalan* (1950) the Supreme Court looked only at the words of Art. 21; in *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) it looked at the purpose of the right to life and liberty and read in fairness.

Without jurisprudence, the study of law is mechanical; with it, law is understood, criticised and improved.

## B-03 · Law and morals

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q6 ("Law and Morals"). 20-mark form: C-24.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · differences 3 · schools' views 3 · Indian illustration 2

### Answer

*Law* is the body of rules enforced by the State; *morality* is the body of standards of right and wrong resting on conscience, religion and social opinion.

**Differences.** Law has State sanctions (fine, imprisonment, damages); morality has inner and social sanctions. Law governs mainly external conduct; morality also governs motive. Law is generally written and definite; morality varies. Law changes by enactment, morality slowly. Law is enforced by courts.

**Overlap.** Murder and theft are both illegal and immoral; but the rule of the road is morally neutral, and ingratitude is immoral but not unlawful.

**Views.** *Natural law* (Aquinas, Fuller) joins them: an unjust law is a perversion of law. *Positivists* separate them: Austin said the existence of law is one thing, its merit another; Hart accepted a minimum content of natural law but kept the separation. *Hart–Fuller* (1958) and *Hart–Devlin* (1957–63) debated the limits.

**India.** *Gopalan* (1950) took a positivist reading; *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) brought in fairness. *Navtej Singh Johar* (2018) preferred constitutional morality to social morality in decriminalising consensual same-sex acts between adults.

Law and morality overlap but are not identical.

## B-04 · Kinds (divisions) of jurisprudence

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("definition, nature and scope"). Related: C-03, A-10, A-11.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Bentham 2 · Austin 2 · Salmond 3 · Stone and modern 3

### Answer

Writers divide jurisprudence differently.

1. **Bentham:** *expositorial* jurisprudence (what the law is) and *censorial* jurisprudence (what it ought to be, tested by utility). Expositorial may be *authoritative* (by the legislator) or *unauthoritative* (by writers).
2. **Austin:** *general jurisprudence* (principles, notions and distinctions common to mature legal systems) and *particular jurisprudence* (the science of a given actual system). Holland rejected the division; Salmond kept both levels of study (see C-03).
3. **Salmond:** in the wide sense, *legal exposition* (analysis of the content of a legal system), *legal history* (the growth of its ideas) and *science of legislation* (the ends law ought to serve). In the narrow sense, the science of the first principles of the civil law.
4. **Julius Stone:** *analytical* jurisprudence (concepts and structure), *sociological* jurisprudence (law's social working) and *theories of justice* (ethical).
5. **By school:** analytical, historical, ethical (natural law), sociological, realist and economic.
6. **Jerome Hall:** *integrative* jurisprudence, uniting law as form (rule), value (justice) and fact (behaviour).

All divisions aim at covering the same field: what law is, how it grew, what it should be and how it works.

## B-05 · Jurisprudence and the other social sciences

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("nature and scope"). Related: C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** statement of the relation 2 · six disciplines 6 · conclusion 2

### Answer

Jurisprudence is interdisciplinary; Stone called it "the lawyer's extroversion", the study of law in the light of knowledge drawn from other fields.

1. **Sociology.** Law is a social institution: Ehrlich's "living law", Pound's social engineering and Duguit's social solidarity.
2. **Ethics.** Both guide conduct, but law is backed by the State and morality by conscience. Natural law joins them; positivism separates them.
3. **History.** Savigny and Maine explain law as a growth of society; legal history shows how concepts developed.
4. **Political science.** State, sovereignty, constitution and separation of powers are common ground (Austin, Kelsen, Duguit).
5. **Economics.** The Marxist theory treats law as shaped by the economic base; law and economics (Coase, Posner) applies the logic of efficiency to legal rules.
6. **Psychology.** Intention, motive, responsibility and judicial behaviour (Jerome Frank's fact-scepticism).
7. **Anthropology.** Early law and custom (Maine; Llewellyn and Hoebel, *The Cheyenne Way*, 1941).

Jurisprudence differs from them in that it studies law itself at the level of principle and uses them to explain it. A student of law who ignores them will see only rules, not the society they serve.

## B-06 · Compare the definitions of jurisprudence given by Holland, Salmond and Gray

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("definition"). Related: C-01, A-04, A-05, A-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** each definition 2 · comparison 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Holland** (*Elements of Jurisprudence*, 1880): "the formal science of positive law". It studies the relations regulated by law and their ideas, not the content or justice of law. *Defect:* "formal" excludes content, purpose and history.

**Salmond** (*Jurisprudence*, 1902): "the science of the first principles of the civil law", that is, the law of the land applied by courts. In a wider sense it includes legal exposition, legal history and the science of legislation. *Defect:* "civil law" and "first principles" are vague.

**Gray** (*The Nature and Sources of the Law*, 1909): "the statement and systematic arrangement of the rules followed by the courts and the principles involved in those rules". It is court-centred. *Defect:* it ignores legislation and the social setting.

| Point | Holland | Salmond | Gray |
|---|---|---|---|
| Method | Analysis of form | Analysis, history and ethics | Statement of court rules |
| Ethics | Excluded | Included (legislation) | Excluded |
| Centre | Positive law | Civil law | Courts |

All three treat jurisprudence as concerned with actual law. Salmond's is the widest and the most commonly preferred, but Stone's view of jurisprudence as the lawyer's extroversion adds the link with other disciplines.

## B-07 · Natural law: meaning, features and criticism

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Natural Law"). Related: C-05, C-06, C-07, A-16.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · features 3 · phases 2 · criticism 3

### Answer

**Meaning.** Natural law is a body of principles of right, derived from nature, reason or God, which is higher than man-made (positive) law and by which it is judged.

**Features**
1. It is discovered by reason, not made by will.
2. It is universal and unchanging.
3. It is superior to positive law; a gravely unjust law is not true law (Aquinas, Cicero).
4. It is the source of justice and natural rights.

**Phases.** Greek (Aristotle, Stoics) and Roman (Cicero, *jus naturale*); medieval (Aquinas: eternal, divine, natural and human law); modern rationalist (Grotius, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant); twentieth-century revival (Stammler, Kohler, Fuller, Finnis, Rawls).

**Criticism**
1. Its content is vague and disputed; it has defended opposite causes.
2. It confuses what law is with what it ought to be (Bentham, Austin).
3. Its principles are assumed, not proved.
4. It has no machinery of enforcement.
5. The historical school showed that law varies with each society.

**Contribution.** It supplies a standard to criticise bad law and underlies human rights, the Universal Declaration (1948), the Nuremberg trials and, in India, the basic-structure doctrine (*Kesavananda*, 1973) and *Puttaswamy* (2017).

## B-08 · Write a note on the revival of natural law

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q7 ("Write a note on revival of natural law."). 20-mark form: C-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** causes 3 · Stammler and Kohler 3 · later writers 3 · evaluation 1

### Answer

Natural law declined in the nineteenth century under the attacks of Bentham, Austin and the historical school. It revived in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries because positivism offered no standard to criticise unjust law, because of the abuses of power in the World Wars and under Nazi rule, and because of the rise of human rights.

**Stammler** (*Theory of Just Law*, 1902): "natural law with a variable content". The form of just law is constant, its content changes; the ideal is a community of free-willing men.

**Kohler** (*Philosophy of Law*, 1909): law serves the advance of civilisation, and each age's law must suit its stage.

**Later writers.** *Fuller* (*Morality of Law*, 1964): eight requirements of the inner morality of law. *Radbruch* (1946): extreme injustice is not law. *Finnis* (1980): basic human goods known by practical reason. *Rawls* (1971): principles of justice chosen behind a "veil of ignorance".

**India.** The basic structure (*Kesavananda*, 1973), *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) and *Puttaswamy* (2017) reflect the revived idea.

The revival drops the old claim of a fixed code but keeps the central idea that law should be tested by justice.

## B-09 · Rudolf Stammler's theory of natural law with a variable content

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Revival of Natural Law: Rudolf Stammler"). Related: C-07, A-25.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · variable content and form-matter 3 · social ideal and principles 4 · criticism 1

### Answer

Rudolf Stammler (1856–1938), a German neo-Kantian, wrote *Die Lehre von dem richtigen Rechte* (1902; English *The Theory of Just Law*).

**Variable content.** No fixed rules are valid for all time. What is constant is the *form* or method of just law; its content varies with time and place. Natural law is therefore a method of testing law, not a code.

**Form and matter.** Law is the form of social life and economics its matter; form is logically prior (against the Marxists).

**Social ideal.** A "community of free-willing men", in which each person's will is respected as an end, not as a mere means.

**Principles.** *Respecting:* (1) one person's will must not be subject to the arbitrary wishes of another; (2) every legal demand must leave the person bound able to remain his own neighbour. *Participating:* (1) no one legally bound may be arbitrarily excluded from the community; (2) a power of exclusion may be used only so that the excluded person is still a neighbour. (Wording varies between textbooks.)

**Criticism.** The principles are too formal to decide hard cases, and the ideal is an aim, not a rule.

## B-10 · Josef Kohler's theory: law and civilisation

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Kohler"). Related: C-07, A-26.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · central idea 4 · role of jurists and judges 2 · criticism 2

### Answer

Josef Kohler (1849–1919), a German jurist and Hegelian, wrote *Lehrbuch der Rechtsphilosophie* (1909; English *Philosophy of Law*, 1914).

**Law and civilisation.** Kohler held that law is a product of civilisation and must be judged by how far it helps civilisation to grow. Its task is to preserve existing civilisation and to develop it. In the usual summary, a rule is good if it helps the fullest development of human powers and control over nature.

**Relativity.** Like Stammler, he rejected a fixed natural law: the law of each age must suit the civilisation of that age. He kept the idea of an ideal but made it relative.

**Jurists and judges.** Jurists should study the social conditions and values of their time, and judges should interpret law in the light of the needs of civilisation, not merely by its letter. In this he stands close to the sociological school.

**Criticism.** "Civilisation" is vague and can justify different policies; it is unclear whose civilisation is meant.

**Comparison.** Stammler is Kantian and formal; Kohler is Hegelian and historical.

Both men show that revived natural law is relative to time and place.

*Word count: 187*

## B-11 · Aquinas and the four kinds of law

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Natural Law"). Related: C-05, A-22.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · four laws 5 · significance and criticism 3

### Answer

St Thomas Aquinas (1225–74) gave the classic Christian account of natural law in the *Summa Theologiae*, combining Aristotle's philosophy with Christian doctrine.

**The four kinds of law**
1. **Eternal law:** the reason of God governing the whole universe.
2. **Divine law:** God's law revealed in scripture, to guide man to his final end.
3. **Natural law:** the part of the eternal law that rational creatures can know by reason; it is their participation in it. Its first precept is that good is to be done and evil avoided.
4. **Human (positive) law:** rules made by rulers for the common good, derived from natural law, either as conclusions from it or as specific determinations of it.

**Validity.** A human law is just and binding in conscience only if it conforms to natural law. A law that contradicts it is not law but "a perversion of law" (*legis corruptio*), though it may sometimes be obeyed to avoid scandal or disorder.

**Significance.** It limited the power of rulers and was used by the Church against secular power. It influenced later natural-rights thinking.

**Criticism.** It rests on theology; its content is vague; it mixes law with morals (Austin).

## B-12 · Fuller's inner morality of law and the Hart–Fuller debate

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Revival of Natural Law"). Related: C-07, C-24, A-27, A-53.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** Fuller's eight desiderata 4 · the debate 4 · evaluation 2

### Answer

Lon L. Fuller (1902–78), in *The Morality of Law* (1964), argued that law has an *inner morality*, a set of procedural requirements that any system of rules must meet to be law. His eight "desiderata", illustrated by the story of the failed king Rex, are:

1. generality (rules, not ad hoc commands);
2. promulgation (publicity);
3. non-retroactivity;
4. clarity;
5. non-contradiction;
6. possibility of compliance;
7. constancy through time;
8. congruence between declared rules and official action.

A total failure in any one of these does not make a bad system of law, but no system of law at all.

**The debate (1958, 71 Harvard Law Review 593 and 630).** Hart defended the separation of law and morals. Fuller replied. In the *grudge informer* case, a woman who had denounced her husband under a Nazi statute was prosecuted after the war. Hart said the statute was law but too evil to be obeyed; Fuller said a system that had lost its inner morality lacked the quality of law.

**Evaluation.** Hart replied that Fuller's morality is only procedural and compatible with evil purposes. Fuller's contribution is to show that legality itself has values.

## B-13 · Manu and the Hindu concept of dharma and law

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q1; Dec 2023 Q1; Dec 2022 Q3 (all in Section A as "Manu"). This is the 10-mark form; the 4-mark form is A-17.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** who and what 2 · sources of dharma 3 · content and administration of law 3 · assessment 2

### Answer

*Manusmriti* (the *Manava Dharmashastra*), traditionally ascribed to Manu, is the best known Hindu law book. Scholars usually place its composition between about 200 BC and AD 200.

**Dharma and law.** In the Indian tradition law is part of *dharma*, the whole body of duties (religious, moral, social and legal). Law is not separated from morality, so the view is nearer to natural law than to positivism.

**Sources of dharma.** Manu names the Veda (*shruti*), the *smriti* (traditional texts), the conduct of the virtuous (*sadachara*) and what is agreeable to one's own conscience (*atmatushti*).

**Content.** The book deals with the duties of the four *varnas* and stages of life, family law, inheritance, contract, crime and punishment, and the duties of the king. It lists eighteen titles of law (*vyavahara-padas*), such as debt, deposits, partnership and theft. The king is the upholder of dharma, and punishment (*danda*) maintains order.

**Assessment.** Manu shows a developed system of custom-based law with the king subject to dharma. But it sanctions caste hierarchy and unequal treatment of women and *shudras*, which the Constitution rejects (Arts. 14–17). Modern Hindu law has been codified (1955–56).

## B-49 · Grotius, Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau: reason, the social contract and natural rights

**Asked as:** syllabus line ("Natural Law School: classical natural law"). Related: A-23, A-24, B-07, C-05, C-06.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** background 2 · Grotius 2 · Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau 4 · assessment 2

### Answer

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries natural law was separated from theology and rested on human reason. The social contract explained the origin of the State and of law.

**Grotius** (1583–1645), *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625): natural law is the dictate of right reason, drawn from man's social nature, and would hold even if there were no God. He founded modern international law.

**Hobbes** (1588–1679), *Leviathan* (1651): in the state of nature life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". Men give up their rights to an absolute sovereign for safety. Law is the sovereign's command, which anticipates Austin.

**Locke** (1632–1704), *Two Treatises of Government* (1689–90): the rights to life, liberty and property exist before government. Government rests on consent and is limited; a ruler who betrays the trust may be resisted. He influenced modern fundamental rights.

**Rousseau** (1712–78), *The Social Contract* (1762): sovereignty lies in the general will of the people.

**Assessment.** The contract is a fiction, not history. The theories reach opposite results (absolutism in Hobbes, limited government in Locke). Their lasting gift is the idea that law rests on reason and consent.

*Word count: 186*
