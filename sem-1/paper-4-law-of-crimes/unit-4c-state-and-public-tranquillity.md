## 2. History of sedition

1. **Origin.** Section 124A was **not** in Macaulay's draft Code or the Penal Code as enacted in 1860; it was inserted by the **Indian Penal Code (Amendment) Act XXVII of 1870**, on the initiative of Sir James Fitzjames Stephen, in response to the Wahabi movement †.
2. **Early cases.** *Queen-Empress v. Jogendra Chunder Bose* (1891) ILR 19 Cal 35 (the Age of Consent Bill case) †; ***Queen-Empress v. Bal Gangadhar Tilak*** (1897) ILR 22 Bom 112, where Strachey J. told the jury that "disaffection" means the absence of affection, and includes disloyalty and all feelings of enmity; Tilak was convicted for articles in *Kesari*; in 1908 he was again convicted of sedition and sentenced to six years' transportation †.
3. **Gandhi's trial (1922).** Mahatma Gandhi pleaded guilty to sedition for articles in *Young India* and called s. 124A "the prince among the political sections of the Indian Penal Code designed to suppress the liberty of the citizen" (paraphrase; the exact words were not checked) †.
4. **The test of disorder.** *Niharendu Dutt Majumdar v. King-Emperor* (1942) FCR 38 (Federal Court): sedition needs **incitement to disorder** or tendency to create public disorder; the **Privy Council** disagreed in ***King-Emperor v. Sadashiv Narayan Bhalerao*** (1947) 74 IA 89, holding that incitement to violence is not an essential element; the words need only excite disaffection †.
5. **Constitution.** Article 19(1)(a) guarantees free speech; Article 19(2), as amended by the First Amendment (1951), permits restrictions "in the interests of … **public order**". *Tara Singh Gopi Chand v. State* AIR 1951 Punj 27 held s. 124A void for violating Art. 19(1)(a); *Ram Nandan v. State of U.P.* AIR 1959 All 101 did the same †. The Supreme Court overruled *Ram Nandan* in *Kedar Nath* (1962).

## 3. Sedition: IPC s. 124A

### 3.1 The text (IPC s. 124A, as it stood)

"Whoever by **words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or otherwise**, **brings or attempts to bring into hatred or contempt, or excites or attempts to excite disaffection towards, the Government established by law in India**, shall be punished with **imprisonment for life**, to which fine may be added, or with **imprisonment which may extend to three years**, to which fine may be added, or with **fine**."

**Explanation 1.** The expression "disaffection" includes **disloyalty and all feelings of enmity**.
**Explanation 2.** Comments expressing **disapprobation of the measures of the Government** with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means, without exciting or attempting to excite hatred, contempt or disaffection, do not constitute an offence.
**Explanation 3.** Comments expressing disapprobation of the **administrative or other action of the Government** without exciting or attempting to excite hatred, contempt or disaffection, do not constitute an offence. (Text of IPC s. 124A from memory †; the BNS Explanation to s. 152 is the successor of Explanations 2 and 3.)

### 3.2 Essentials

1. **Words, signs or visible representation** (or "otherwise").
2. They **bring or attempt to bring into hatred or contempt**, or **excite or attempt to excite disaffection** towards the **Government established by law**.
3. "Government established by law" means the **visible symbol of the State**, not the persons for the time being in office (*Kedar Nath*).
4. After *Kedar Nath*, the words must have a **tendency or intention to create disorder, disturb law and order, or incite violence**.

### 3.3 Leading cases

- ***Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar*** AIR 1962 SC 955 (Constitution Bench). Kedar Nath, a Forward Communist Party member, made a speech in Bihar attacking the Government of the day in strong language and urging people to overthrow it (the exact words are not reproduced here; from memory). The question was whether s. 124A violated Art. 19(1)(a). **Held:** the section is **valid** if it is read narrowly. (i) "Government established by law" is the visible symbol of the State, and its stability is essential; (ii) strong words used to express disapprobation of the measures of the Government, with a view to their improvement or alteration by lawful means, are not sedition; (iii) the section applies only to words or acts which have the **intention or tendency to create disorder, or disturbance of law and order, or incitement to violence**; (iv) so read, it is a reasonable restriction in the interests of public order under Art. 19(2). The Court overruled *Ram Nandan*, and applied the narrowing to s. 124A and, in the same case, s. 505 †.
- ***Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1995) 3 SCC 214. Two persons shouted "Khalistan Zindabad" and "Raj Karega Khalsa" in the street on the day of Indira Gandhi's assassination, in a crowd. **Held:** casual sloganeering, without any call to violence and with no reaction, is not sedition, since it did not incite the people to violence †.
- ***Arup Bhuyan v. State of Assam*** (2011) 3 SCC 377. **Held:** mere **membership of a banned organisation** is not an offence unless the person resorts to violence or incites others to violence (reading down the TADA provision on the lines of the American "clear and present danger" rule) †.
- ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India*** (2015) 5 SCC 1. The Court struck down s. 66A of the Information Technology Act and drew the line between **discussion, advocacy and incitement**: only incitement, which has a proximate connection with public disorder, may be restricted †.
- ***Vinod Dua v. Union of India*** (2021) SCC OnLine SC 414. The Court quashed a sedition FIR against a journalist for criticising the Government in a YouTube show, applying *Kedar Nath* and holding that criticism of the Government's measures is not sedition †.
- **The Law Commission** (22nd, 279th Report, 2023) recommended that s. 124A be **retained** with procedural safeguards (a preliminary inquiry before an FIR) †.

### 3.4 The order of 11 May 2022

In ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court (CJI N.V. Ramana, Surya Kant and Hima Kohli JJ.) recorded the Union's statement that it would **re-examine and reconsider** s. 124A. The Court directed that the **provision be kept in abeyance** in the meantime: **no fresh FIRs** were to be registered and no investigation or coercive measure taken under s. 124A; **pending cases, appeals and proceedings** under it were to be kept in abeyance; a person newly charged could approach the court. The order did not strike down s. 124A ‡ (secondary report). On 12 September 2023 a three-judge Bench headed by CJI Chandrachud referred the challenge to a **larger Bench** (the report I read describes it as a seven-judge Bench, not yet constituted as of 22 May 2026) ‡. A Division Bench clarified on 21 May 2026 that sedition-related proceedings can continue if the accused does not object ‡ (secondary report).

### 3.5 Arguments for and against

| For retaining (the Government's and the Law Commission's case) | Against (the petitioners' case) |
|---|---|
| The State's security needs protection from secessionist and violent speech | The offence is vague ("disaffection", "contempt") and colonial in origin; it was used against Tilak and Gandhi |
| *Kedar Nath* narrowed the offence enough | Misused against journalists, students, critics; low conviction rates |
| Other democracies have similar provisions (treason, seditious conspiracy) | The UK repealed seditious libel in 2009; the US and others rely on incitement tests |
| UAPA and other laws cover violent acts | A free society must tolerate criticism of the Government |

## 4. BNS s. 152: "Acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India"

### 4.1 The text ‡

"Whoever, **purposely or knowingly**, by words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or by **electronic communication** or by **use of financial mean**, or otherwise, **excites or attempts to excite, secession or armed rebellion or subversive activities**, or **encourages feelings of separatist activities** or **endangers sovereignty or unity and integrity of India**; or indulges in or commits any such act shall be punished with **imprisonment for life** or with **imprisonment which may extend to seven years**, and shall also be liable to **fine**."

**Explanation.** "Comments expressing disapprobation of the measures, or administrative or other action of the Government with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means without exciting or attempting to excite the activities referred to in this section" do not constitute an offence under this section.

### 4.2 IPC s. 124A and BNS s. 152 compared

| Point | IPC s. 124A (sedition) | BNS s. 152 |
|---|---|---|
| Heading | Sedition | "Acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India" (the word *sedition* is gone) |
| Target | Hatred, contempt or **disaffection** towards the **Government established by law** | **Secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist feelings, endangering sovereignty or unity and integrity** |
| Mental element | Not stated | **"Purposely or knowingly"** |
| Means | Words, signs, visible representation, "otherwise" | Words, signs, visible representation, **electronic communication, use of financial means**, otherwise |
| Includes acts? | Words and attempts | Words and attempts, and "**indulges in or commits any such act**" |
| Punishment | Life ± fine; **or** up to 3 years ± fine; **or** fine only | Life, **or** up to 7 years, **and** fine; **no** fine-only or 3-year option |
| Explanations | Three | **One** (disapprobation of measures or administrative action by lawful means) |
| Cognizable | Cognizable, non-bailable, Sessions | Cognizable, non-bailable, Sessions (BNSS First Schedule †) |

**Government's case.** The Government's position on introducing the BNS was that sedition, a colonial relic, was removed and that s. 152 punishes acts that endanger the **country**, not criticism of the Government (reported; from memory †). **Critics' case.** The substance of s. 124A "remains intact" in wider words; "subversive activities" and "separatist feelings" are undefined; the minimum is higher; the electronic and financial means widen the net (the petition and reports in §4.3).

### 4.3 Challenge to s. 152 and current status

- **8 August 2025.** A three-judge Bench of the Supreme Court (CJI B.R. Gavai, K. Vinod Chandran and N.V. Anjaria JJ.) issued **notice** to the Union on a PIL by retired Major General S.G. Vombatkere challenging s. 152 as "repackaged sedition", and **tagged** it with the pending challenge to s. 124A; no interim stay of s. 152 was reported ‡ (secondary report).
- **February 2026.** A Bench of CJI Surya Kant and Bagchi J. observed that **judicial review** of the provision was possible; the challenge is tagged with the *Vombatkere* batch ‡ (secondary report).
- **As of 22 May 2026**, the validity of s. 124A was undecided, and I found no judgment on s. 152 ‡ (secondary report; **not rechecked after that date**). Check before the examination.

### 4.4 How to answer "Is s. 152 sedition by another name?"

State both cases fairly (§4.2): the text, the purposeful or knowing mental element, the narrower targets, the single Explanation, and the pending challenge; and note that *Kedar Nath* (incitement to violence or disorder) is likely to guide the reading of s. 152, because the Explanation protects criticism by lawful means.

## 6. Unlawful assembly: BNS s. 189

### 6.1 The definition ‡

An assembly of **five or more persons** is designated an **unlawful assembly**, if the **common object** of the persons composing that assembly is:

- **(a)** to overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force, the Central Government or any State Government or Parliament or the Legislature of any State, or any public servant in the exercise of the lawful power of such public servant; or
- **(b)** to resist the execution of any law, or of any legal process; or
- **(c)** to commit any mischief or criminal trespass, or other offence; or
- **(d)** by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to any person, to take or obtain possession of any property, or to deprive any person of the enjoyment of a right of way, or of the use of water or other incorporeal right of which he is in possession or enjoyment, or to enforce any right or supposed right; or
- **(e)** by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do what he is legally entitled to do.

**Explanation.** An assembly which was not unlawful when it assembled may subsequently become an unlawful assembly (the IPC's Explanation to s. 141, repeated †).

### 6.2 Essentials

1. **Five or more persons** assembled. (If five are charged but only some are proved to be members, s. 190 may fail: *Dalip Singh*, *Bharwad Mepa Dana*; Unit 1B.)
2. A **common object** of the assembly, which is one of the five listed. The object is that of the assembly, not of each person's private motive; it may be formed on the spot. Courts infer it from the conduct, the arms carried, the language used and the way the assembly acted (*Masalti v. State of U.P.* AIR 1965 SC 202 †).
3. **Unlawful object**: criminal force or show of it for (a), (d), (e); resistance to law for (b); any offence for (c).
4. An assembly **lawful at the start** can become unlawful.

### 6.3 Lawful and unlawful gatherings (Dec 2025 C-question)

| Point | Lawful assembly | Unlawful assembly |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Any number | Five or more |
| Object | Lawful (a meeting, a procession, a peaceful protest) | One of the five unlawful objects (s. 189(1)) |
| Means | Peaceful, without arms | Criminal force or show of criminal force |
| Constitutional position | Protected by **Art. 19(1)(b)** (assemble peaceably and without arms) | Not protected; Art. 19(3) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity, or public order |
| Police power | Regulation only; permission schemes | May be **dispersed** (BNSS ss. 148–150; Cr.P.C. ss. 129–131) †; prohibitory orders (BNSS s. 163; Cr.P.C. s. 144) † |
| Conversion | Becomes unlawful if the object changes or the order to disperse is defied (s. 189(3), (5)) | – |

**The right to assemble.** The right in Art. 19(1)(b) is subject to reasonable restrictions. The Supreme Court has held that there is no right to hold a meeting on a public street without regulation (*Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad* (1973) 1 SCC 227 †), that peaceful protest is part of the right and that police must use proportionate force (*Ramlila Maidan Incident, In re* (2012) 5 SCC 1 †), and that a protest cannot occupy a public way indefinitely (*Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police* (2020) 10 SCC 439, the Shaheen Bagh case †).

### 6.4 The punishments (s. 189(2)–(9)) ‡

| Sub-section | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Knowingly joining or continuing in an unlawful assembly as a member | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Joining or continuing after being **lawfully commanded to disperse** | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Being a member while **armed with a deadly weapon**, or anything likely to cause death used as a weapon of offence | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (5) | Knowingly joining or continuing in an assembly of five or more likely to disturb the public peace, **after a lawful order to disperse** | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (6) | **Hiring, engaging, employing, promoting or conniving at the hiring** of persons for an unlawful assembly | Punished as a member, and for any offence committed by the hired person |

| (8) | Being **hired**, or offering to be hired | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |

## 7. Common object: BNS s. 190 (IPC s. 149) – reference

The full discussion is in [Unit 1B §3](unit-1b-constructive-liability.md). In brief: if an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of its common object, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, **every person who at the time is a member** is guilty of that offence ‡ (text: "every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence"). *Masalti*; *Mizaji*; *Lalji* †.

## 8. Rioting: BNS s. 191 (IPC ss. 146–148)

1. **s. 191(1) (IPC 146).** "Whenever **force or violence** is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member thereof, **in prosecution of the common object** of such assembly, every member of such assembly is guilty of the offence of **rioting**." (Wording from the IPC †; the sub-section and punishments ‡.)
2. **s. 191(2).** Punishment: imprisonment up to **two years**, or fine, or both ‡.
3. **s. 191(3).** **Rioting armed with a deadly weapon**, or with anything which, used as a weapon of offence, is likely to cause death: up to **five years**, or fine, or both ‡.
4. **Essentials.** (i) An **unlawful assembly** (five or more with an unlawful common object); (ii) **force or violence** used by the assembly or any member; (iii) in **prosecution of the common object**.
5. **Riot and unlawful assembly.** Every riot is an unlawful assembly; not every unlawful assembly is a riot. The difference is the actual **use of force or violence**.
6. **Provocation to riot** (s. 192): wantonly giving provocation, intending or knowing it likely that it will cause riot ‡.
7. **Owner or occupier's liability** (s. 193): an owner or occupier of land on which an unlawful assembly or riot takes place, who knows of it and does not give notice to the police, is liable to a fine ‡.

## 9. Affray: BNS s. 194 (IPC ss. 159, 160)

"When **two or more persons**, by **fighting in a public place**, **disturb the public peace**, they are said to commit an **affray**." (Wording from the IPC s. 159 †.) **Punishment** (s. 194) ‡: imprisonment up to **one month**, or fine up to **₹1,000**, or both. **Explanation (IPC):** when the fight is in private, or does not disturb the public peace, it is not an affray.

## 10. Unlawful assembly, riot and affray compared (Dec 2023 C-question)

| Point | Unlawful assembly (s. 189) | Rioting (s. 191) | Affray (s. 194) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | **Five or more** | Five or more (it is an unlawful assembly that uses force) | **Two or more** |
| Common object | Required (one of five) | Required | **Not required**: a mere fight |
| Force or violence | Not necessary | **Necessary** | Fighting |
| Place | Anywhere | Anywhere | **Public place** |
| Disturbance of public peace | Not necessary | – | **Necessary** |
| Punishment | 6 months (member); 2 years (armed or after dispersal) | 2 years; 5 years (armed) | **1 month** or ₹1,000 |
| Relationship | Genus: the first stage | Aggravated: unlawful assembly + force | Different: minor, no assembly |
| Example | Five persons gather to take possession of a field by show of force | They beat the owner with lathis | Two men fight in a market |

**Sequence.** *Assembly* of five or more → *unlawful* if common object is one of the five → *riot* when force is used → members liable for the offences committed by s. 190.

## 11. Problem

**Eight villagers meet with lathis to stop a surveyor measuring land. When the surveyor refuses to leave, they beat him. One of them, X, stabs him with a knife and the surveyor dies. The others say they only wanted to frighten him.**

1. **Unlawful assembly:** eight persons, object to resist execution of law/legal process, or to commit an offence (s. 189(1)(b), (c)); armed with lathis, a deadly weapon (s. 189(4)).
2. **Rioting:** force was used in prosecution of the common object (s. 191(3), armed).
3. **Murder by X:** s. 103(1). **Liability of the others:** s. 190: murder committed by a member **in prosecution of the common object, or such as the members knew to be likely** (a stabbing during a beating with lathis may or may not have been known to be likely; the court looks at weapons, conduct and whether the knife was visible: *Mizaji*). If they are not liable for murder, they are liable for the beating (hurt, riot).
4. **Common intention (s. 3(5))** may also be pleaded if a prior plan to kill is shown.

## 12. Criticism

1. **Sedition/s. 152.** See §3.5 and §4.2.
2. **Unlawful assembly.** The "common object" is wide ("any offence"); mere presence in a crowd may expose an innocent person; *Masalti* asks courts to be careful but the practice differs.
3. **Prohibitory orders** (BNSS s. 163; Cr.P.C. s. 144) are used to prevent peaceful protests; the Supreme Court said in *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637 that such orders must be proportionate and may not be used to suppress legitimate expression †.
4. **Affray** is a petty offence; the real violence is dealt with as hurt.
5. **Hired crowds**: the BNS adds sub-sections on hiring; critics say enforcement is rare.

## 13. Current developments (to 29 October 2026)

1. IPC s. 124A is out of the BNS; s. 152 is in; both are under challenge before the Supreme Court (§3.4, §4.3); no final decision found as of the date of my last report (22 May 2026) ‡.
2. The BNS re-enacts ss. 141–160 as ss. 189–197 with higher or restated punishments (rioting: 2 years; armed: 5 years) ‡.
3. New punishments for hiring persons for unlawful assemblies, ss. 189(6)–(9) ‡.

## 15. Quick-revision box

- **IPC 124A:** words, signs, visible representation bringing or attempting to bring into hatred or contempt, or exciting disaffection towards the Government established by law; life ± fine, or 3 years ± fine, or fine. 1870, Stephen. *Kedar Nath* (1962): valid, only for incitement to disorder or violence. In abeyance since 11 May 2022.
- **BNS 152:** purposely or knowingly + secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist feelings, endangering sovereignty/unity/integrity; electronic and financial means; life or up to 7 years + fine; one Explanation. Challenged (notice 8 Aug 2025).
- **Unlawful assembly (s. 189):** five or more + one of five objects; member: 6 months; armed or after dispersal order: 2 years. **s. 190:** every member liable for the offence in prosecution of the common object.
- **Riot (s. 191):** unlawful assembly + force; 2 years; armed 5 years. **Affray (s. 194):** two or more, fight in public place, disturb peace; 1 month / ₹1,000.
