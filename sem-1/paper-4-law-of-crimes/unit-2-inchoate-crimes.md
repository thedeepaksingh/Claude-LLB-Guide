## 1. What is an inchoate crime?

1. **Meaning.** An **inchoate** (or "preliminary", "incomplete") crime is an offence committed by taking a step **towards** another crime, whether or not that other crime is finally committed. The law punishes the step itself because it is dangerous and shows a guilty mind.
2. **The three inchoate crimes of the Code.**

| Inchoate crime | Where | One-line idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Abetment** | BNS ss. 45–60 (IPC 107–120) ‡ | Helping, urging or joining another so that he commits an offence |
| **Criminal conspiracy** | BNS s. 61 (IPC 120A–120B) ‡ | Agreeing with another to commit an offence (or a legal act by illegal means) |
| **Attempt** | BNS s. 62 (IPC 511) and special sections ‡ | An act towards the commission that falls short of completion |

3. **How they differ from the completed crime.** The target offence need not be committed. Abetment can be complete when the instigation is made. Conspiracy is complete when the agreement is made (with an overt act where the object is not itself an offence). Attempt is complete when the act goes beyond mere preparation.

## 2. Abetment: meaning and kinds (BNS s. 45; IPC s. 107)

### 2.1 The text

BNS s. 45 (word for word as IPC s. 107 †): *"A person abets the doing of a thing, who—*
*First.—Instigates any person to do that thing; or*
*Secondly.—Engages with one or more other person or persons in any conspiracy for the doing of that thing, if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy, and in order to the doing of that thing; or*
*Thirdly.—Intentionally aids, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing."*

**Explanation 1.** A person who, by wilful misrepresentation, or by wilful concealment of a material fact which he is bound to disclose, voluntarily causes or procures, or attempts to cause or procure, a thing to be done, is said to instigate the doing of it.

**Explanation 2.** Whoever, either prior to or at the time of the commission of an act, does anything in order to facilitate the commission of that act, and thereby facilitates the commission thereof, is said to **aid** the doing of that act. (Wording closely paraphrased †.)

### 2.2 The three modes of abetment

1. **Instigation.** To *instigate* is to goad, urge forward, provoke, incite or encourage. The Supreme Court has said that there must be a **positive act** of instigation; it may be by words, conduct or a series of acts, and it need not be express (*Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2001) 9 SCC 618 †). A wish, a reprimand or an angry outburst such as "go and die" is not by itself instigation, because the speaker must **intend** the act (*Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v. State of M.P.* (2002) 5 SCC 371 †).
2. **Conspiracy (as a form of abetment).** The abettor must (a) engage with one or more other persons in a conspiracy to do the thing, and (b) an **act or illegal omission must take place in pursuance** of that conspiracy. Without the act, there is no abetment by conspiracy (though there may be the separate offence of criminal conspiracy: see §6).
3. **Intentional aid.** Aid may be by an act or an **illegal omission**, before or at the time of the offence, which actually **facilitates** it. The aid must be **intentional**. Giving a lift to a person who then commits a crime, without knowing his purpose, is not abetment.

### 2.3 Abettor (BNS s. 46; IPC s. 108)

An **abettor** is a person who abets (i) the commission of an offence, or (ii) the commission of an act which would be an offence if done by a person capable by law of committing an offence with the same intention or knowledge as the abettor. The Explanations make these points:

| Explanation | Point | Example (mine unless stated) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Abetting the **illegal omission** of an act is an offence even if the abettor is not himself bound to do the act | A persuades a witness to stay away from court |
| 2 | It is **not necessary that the act abetted be committed**, or the effect be caused | A instigates B to murder C; B refuses. A is guilty of abetting murder |
| 3 | It is **not necessary that the person abetted be capable of committing an offence**, or have the same guilty intention | A, with a guilty intention, abets a child or a lunatic to do an act which would be an offence if done by an adult: A is guilty |
| 4 | **Abetment of an abetment** is an offence | A instigates B to instigate C to murder Z. If C does it, A is liable |
| 5 | It is **not necessary that the abettor concert the offence with the person abetted**; it is enough that he engages in the conspiracy in pursuance of which the offence is committed | The IPC's own illustration: A and B plan to poison Z, A to administer it. B tells C of the plan without naming A; C procures and delivers the poison; A administers it and Z dies. C has not conspired with A but is an abettor |

(Content of the Explanations †, from the IPC text, which the BNS repeats.)

### 2.4 Abetment within and outside India

- **IPC s. 108A / BNS s. 47 †:** a person who, **in India**, abets an act **outside India** which would be an offence if committed in India is an abettor.
- **BNS s. 48 †** (new in the BNS): a person who, **outside India**, abets the commission in India of an act which would be an offence is an abettor.

### 2.5 Essentials of abetment

1. A **specific offence (or act that would be an offence)** is abetted.
2. The abettor has done **one of the three acts**: instigation, engaging in a conspiracy followed by an act, or intentional aid.
3. The abettor has the **guilty mind**: he intends the offence to be committed, or knows its circumstances.
4. The act abetted **need not be committed** (Explanation 2). If it is committed, the abettor is punished under s. 49 (below); if it is not, under s. 55 or 56 (below).

## 3. Punishment for abetment (BNS ss. 49–60 †; IPC ss. 109–120)

| BNS section † | IPC | Subject | Punishment / rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| s. 49 | 109 | Abetment, **if the act abetted is committed in consequence** and no express provision | Same as for the offence |
| s. 50 | 110 | Abettor's **intention differs** from the doer's | Punished as if the act had been done with the abettor's intention or knowledge |
| s. 51 | 111 | **Different act done** from the act abetted | If the act done was a **probable consequence** of the abetment, and was committed under the instigation, with the aid, or pursuant to the conspiracy, the abettor is liable as if he had abetted it directly |
| s. 52 | 112 | **Cumulative** punishment | Where the abetted act and the different act are separate offences, the abettor is liable for both |
| s. 53 | 113 | **Different effect** caused | Liable for the effect actually caused if he knew it was likely |
| s. 54 | 114 | Abettor **present** when offence is committed | Deemed to have committed it |
| s. 55 | 115 | Abetment of an offence punishable with **death or life imprisonment**, if the offence is **not committed** | Up to **7 years** and fine; if harm results, up to **14 years** and fine |
| s. 56 | 116 | Abetment of an offence punishable with **imprisonment**, if not committed | Up to **one-fourth** of the longest term, or fine, or both; larger if the abettor or the person abetted is a public servant |
| s. 57 | 117 | Abetting commission of an offence **by the public or by more than ten persons** | Up to **3 years**, or fine, or both |
| s. 58 | 118 | **Concealing a design** to commit an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment | Up to **7 years** and fine (3 years if the offence is not committed) |
| s. 59 | 119 | **Public servant concealing** a design to commit an offence which it is his duty to prevent | Up to **half** the longest term; up to 10 years where the offence is punishable with death or life imprisonment |
| s. 60 | 120 | Concealing a design to commit an offence punishable with **imprisonment** | Up to **one-fourth** (or one-eighth, if the offence is not committed) of the longest term |

(Table of ranges from the IPC; the BNS section numbers are †.)

**Special sections.** Abetment of suicide: BNS s. 108 (IPC s. 306), imprisonment up to **10 years** and fine ‡; abetment of suicide of a **child or person of unsound mind**: BNS s. 107 (IPC s. 305), death, life imprisonment, or up to 10 years, and fine ‡. Abetment of dowry-related and other offences is dealt with under the sections concerned.

## 4. Key cases on abetment

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2001) 9 SCC 618 † | Instigation means to goad, urge or incite; there must be a **positive act**; not made out by an angry remark such as "go ahead and die" without intention |
| *Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v. State of M.P.* (2002) 5 SCC 371 † | Words used in a quarrel ("go and die") do not by themselves amount to instigation; there must be **mens rea** and a link with the suicide |
| *Gangula Mohan Reddy v. State of A.P.* (2010) 1 SCC 750 † | Abetment involves a **mental process** of instigating or intentionally aiding; the instigation must be to a degree that left the victim with no other option |
| *Amalendu Pal @ Jhantu v. State of W.B.* (2010) 1 SCC 707 † | Some **direct or indirect act of incitement** is necessary; mere harassment, without a proximate act, is not abetment of suicide; no conviction on a loose reading of the facts |

**Suicide and abetment.** An attempt to commit suicide is no longer an offence in the BNS except where made to compel or restrain a public servant (s. 226) †. Abetment of suicide (s. 108) remains. The Court looks for (i) a proximity of the accused's act to the suicide, and (ii) an intention to push the victim to it. Ordinary quarrels or marital discord are not enough.

## 5. Abetment: illustrations

1. A, with the intention that B should kill C, hands him a gun on the day of the murder and tells him where C will be. B kills C. A has abetted by **aid** and instigation; he is liable under s. 49 for murder as an abettor.
2. A instigates B to kill C. B refuses. A is guilty of abetment of murder (Explanation 2 to s. 46); punishable under s. 55 (up to 7 years, since murder carries death or life).
3. A instigates B to beat C. B kills C with a knife. If death was a **probable consequence** of the beating that A instigated (a weapon was present, etc.), A is liable under s. 51; if the killing was independent and unforeseeable, A is liable only for the beating.
4. A is present when B beats C to death and has instigated the beating. A is **deemed to have committed** the offence (s. 54).

## 6. Criminal conspiracy (BNS s. 61; IPC ss. 120A, 120B)

### 6.1 History

Criminal conspiracy as an offence in itself was added to the IPC by the **Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1913** (Chapter V-A, ss. 120A–120B) †. Before that, conspiracy was punishable only as abetment by conspiracy (which needs an act in pursuance). The BNS has merged ss. 120A and 120B into one section, **s. 61** ‡.

### 6.2 The text (paraphrased from the Act ‡ except where marked †)

**s. 61(1).** When two or more persons agree to do, or cause to be done, (a) an **illegal act**, or (b) an act which is **not illegal by illegal means**, such an agreement is designated a **criminal conspiracy**.

**Proviso.** No agreement except an agreement to commit an **offence** shall amount to a criminal conspiracy unless some **act besides the agreement** is done by one or more parties to such agreement in pursuance thereof †.

**Explanation.** It is immaterial whether the illegal act is the ultimate object of the agreement or is merely incidental to that object †.

**s. 61(2).** Whoever is a party to a criminal conspiracy (a) to commit an offence punishable with **death, imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for two years or upwards**, shall, where no express provision is made for its punishment in this Sanhita, be punished in the same manner as if he had **abetted** such offence; (b) other than such a conspiracy, shall be punished with imprisonment for a term not exceeding **six months**, or with fine, or with both †.

### 6.3 Essentials

1. An **agreement** between **two or more persons** (a person cannot conspire with himself; but the other need not be identified or convicted).
2. The agreement is to do (or cause to be done) an **illegal act**, or a **legal act by illegal means**.
3. Where the object is an **offence**, **the agreement alone suffices**. Where the object is illegal but not an offence (for example a civil wrong), or the means are illegal but the act is legal, an **overt act** in pursuance of the agreement is needed (the proviso).
4. A **guilty mind**: knowledge of the agreement and intention to further its object.

### 6.4 How conspiracy is proved

1. Conspiracy is hatched in **secrecy**, so direct evidence is rare. The agreement is usually **inferred from circumstances**: the conduct of the parties before, during and after the offence. The inference must be drawn from facts that are **unequivocal** and point to an agreement (*Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)* (1988) 3 SCC 609 †).
2. **Things said or done by one conspirator in reference to the common design** are relevant against all (Evidence Act s. 10; BSA 2023 s. 8 †).
3. **One conspiracy or several?** The Court looks at whether there is one overall agreement ("wheel" with a hub and spokes, or "chain" with links) or a number of separate agreements (*Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab* (1977) 4 SCC 540 †).
4. **Conspirators are liable for what is done in pursuance** even if they did not take part in every act, and a person who joins later or leaves earlier is liable only for the period in which he was a member.
5. **Acquittal of co-conspirators.** Where all but one are acquitted for the same conspiracy, the one cannot be convicted of a conspiracy with the acquitted persons (a conspiracy needs two), but he can be convicted if the evidence shows he conspired with unidentified persons †.

### 6.5 Leading cases

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| ***Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)*** (1988) 3 SCC 609 † | Assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi: conspiracy is proved by inference; the agreement is the gist; the Court upheld the conviction of Kehar Singh as a conspirator (and Satwant Singh as an assassin) and acquitted Balbir Singh (from memory) |
| ***State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu*** (2005) 11 SCC 600 † | Parliament attack case: conspiracy proved by circumstantial evidence; the Court examined the agreement, the overt acts and the role of each |
| ***Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab*** (1977) 4 SCC 540 † | One conspiracy or several; conspiracy continues till the object is achieved |
| *State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa* (1996) 4 SCC 659 † | Mens rea of conspiracy: knowledge of the plan and intention to aid it |

## 7. Conspiracy compared with abetment, common intention and attempt

| Point | Conspiracy (s. 61) | Abetment by conspiracy (s. 45, second) | Common intention (s. 3(5)) | Attempt (s. 62) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | A substantive offence | A mode of abetment | A rule of joint liability | A substantive offence (or part of it) |
| Agreement | Essential | Essential | Prior meeting of minds | Not essential |
| Act beyond agreement | **Not needed** if the object is an offence | An **act or illegal omission in pursuance** is necessary | The criminal act is done | An act towards the offence |
| Persons | Two or more | One abettor and the doer | Two or more | One or more |
| Punishment | Abettor's punishment (offence of 2 years or more), else 6 months | As abetment (s. 49, 55, 56) | As for the offence | Half of the offence's term (s. 62), or special provision |
| Offence completed? | Not necessary | Necessary for s. 49; not for s. 55–56 | Yes | No |

**One line.** *Conspiracy punishes the agreement; abetment by conspiracy punishes the agreement plus an act in pursuance of it; attempt punishes an act towards the offence.*

## 8. Attempt: summary (BNS s. 62; IPC s. 511)

1. **Stages.** Intention → preparation → attempt → commission. Attempt is the third stage. See [Unit 1A §4](unit-1a-concept-elements-stages.md) for the full discussion and the tests (proximity, equivocality, last step, social danger).
2. **Section 62.** Attempt to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment (including life), where no express provision is made, is punished with up to **half** of the imprisonment for life, or of the longest term, or with fine as provided for the offence, or both ‡.
3. **Special sections** prevail: attempt to murder (s. 109; IPC 307), attempt to commit culpable homicide (s. 110; IPC 308), attempt to commit robbery (s. 309(5) ‡), and others.
4. **Leading cases.** *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* AIR 1961 SC 1698 (a candidate who applied for admission with forged documents was guilty of attempt to cheat once he sent the application); *Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab* (1991) 4 SCC 341 (carrying paddy to the border is preparation, not attempt); *Om Prakash v. State of Punjab* AIR 1961 SC 1782 (attempt to murder: hurt need not be caused) †.
5. **Impossible attempt.** An attempt to steal from an empty pocket or a box without jewels is still an attempt (IPC s. 511, Illustration (a); *R v. Shivpuri*) †.

## 10. Criticism

1. **Abetment.** The boundary between "instigation" and ordinary speech is thin, and in abetment-of-suicide cases (s. 108) the courts have to guard against criminalising quarrels and marital discord. Many FIRs add s. 108 as a matter of routine, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly quashed them where there was no positive act.
2. **Conspiracy.** Conspiracy has been called "the darling of the modern prosecutor's nursery" (Judge Learned Hand †): it is easy to allege, hard to rebut, allows evidence of one conspirator's acts against all, and may punish an agreement with no act. The 1913 amendment widened the Code's reach.
3. **Overlap.** Abetment by conspiracy, criminal conspiracy, common intention and attempt overlap, and the sentence for conspiracy to commit a serious offence is that of an abettor, which may be lower than the principal's.
4. **BNS.** The BNS keeps the structure unchanged: it merges ss. 120A–120B into s. 61 and adds s. 48 (abetment from outside India) †. It does not define "preparation" or give guidance on the line between preparation and attempt.

## 11. Current developments (to 29 October 2026)

1. IPC chapters V and V-A are now BNS ss. 45–60 and s. 61; the words are substantially unchanged ‡. IPC case law continues to apply.
2. New: s. 48 (abetment outside India for an offence in India) †; organised crime (s. 111) and terrorist act (s. 113) are conspiracy-like offences with their own punishments ‡.
3. I did not find a Supreme Court decision since 1 July 2024 that rewrites the law of abetment, conspiracy or attempt under the BNS. **Not checked** exhaustively; verify with a current law reporter.

## 12. Quick-revision box

- **Abetment (s. 45):** instigation · conspiracy + act/illegal omission · intentional aid. The act abetted need **not** be committed (s. 46, Expl. 2).
- **Abettor present (s. 54), different act (s. 51), different intention (s. 50).**
- **Punishment:** act committed → as the offence (s. 49); not committed → death/life offence: 7 years (14 if harm); imprisonment offence: ¼ of longest term (ss. 55–56).
- **Suicide:** s. 108 (10 years) and s. 107 (child/unsound mind). *Ramesh Kumar; Sanju; Gangula; Amalendu Pal*.
- **Conspiracy (s. 61):** agreement between two or more to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means · agreement alone suffices if the object is an offence; overt act needed otherwise · punishment as abettor (offence of 2 years or more) else 6 months. *Kehar Singh; Navjot Sandhu; Yash Pal Mittal*.
- **Attempt (s. 62):** up to half the longest term; special sections prevail. *Abhayanand Mishra; Malkiat Singh*.
