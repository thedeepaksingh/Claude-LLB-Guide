## 1. The idea and the map

1. **Meaning.** A **general exception** is a circumstance in which an act that would otherwise be an offence is **not** an offence, because the law either **excuses** the actor (no culpable mind: infancy, insanity, involuntary intoxication, mistake of fact, accident) or **justifies** the act (private defence, necessity, acts done in good faith for another's benefit, judicial acts).
2. **Where they sit.** BNS Chapter III, **General Exceptions**, ss. 14–44 ‡ (IPC Chapter IV, ss. 76–106). They apply to **every** offence in the Sanhita and other penal laws unless excluded, subject to s. 1 and to the special laws' own rules.
3. **Burden of proof.** The prosecution must prove the offence. When the accused pleads a general exception, the burden of proving that the case falls within it is on him: Evidence Act s. 105 (BSA 2023, corresponding section †). The court **presumes absence of the circumstances** until the accused proves them, but he need only show a **preponderance of probabilities**, not prove beyond reasonable doubt (*Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat* AIR 1964 SC 1563 for insanity †; *Partap v. State of U.P.* (1976) 1 SCC 203 †). In the case of private defence, the accused need not even plead it if the evidence shows it (*Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1968 SC 702 †).
4. **Excuse and justification.** Excuses (infancy, insanity) lead to **acquittal** because there is no guilty mind. Justifications (private defence) make the act **lawful**, and a person may resist a justified act only if the law gives another right of defence.

## 2. Mistake of fact (BNS ss. 14 and 17; IPC ss. 76 and 79)

1. **The rule.** *Ignorantia facti excusat; ignorantia juris non excusat*: ignorance of a **fact** may excuse; ignorance of the **law** does not. The sections protect a person who, by reason of a **mistake of fact and not of law**, in **good faith** believes himself to be **bound by law** to do an act (s. 14) or to be **justified by law** in doing it (s. 17).
2. **Text (IPC s. 76, repeated by BNS s. 14 †).** *"Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who is, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law in good faith believes himself to be, bound by law to do it."* Section 79 (BNS s. 17) is the same, with "justified by law" in place of "bound by law".
3. **Good faith** means with **due care and attention** (BNS s. 2(11) ‡): a careless belief is not in good faith.
4. **IPC illustrations (paraphrased).** (a) *Section 76:* A, a soldier, fires on a mob by the order of his superior officer, in conformity with the commands of the law: A has committed no offence. B, a soldier, fires on the mob by the order of his superior, but the order is not in conformity with the law: B is guilty. (b) *Section 79:* A sees Z commit what appears to A to be a murder. A, in good faith, seizes Z to bring him before the authorities. A has committed no offence, though Z was in fact acting in self-defence.
5. **Reasonable belief.** The belief must be honest and reasonable in the circumstances. Mistake of **law** is no defence (a person who believes that the law allows him to do an act which is forbidden is guilty).
6. **Comparison.** *R v. Tolson* (1889) 23 QBD 168: an honest and reasonable belief in a fact which, if true, would make the act innocent, is a defence (bigamy: the husband's honest belief that his wife was dead) †. *R v. Prince* (1875) LR 2 CCR 154: belief that the girl was over sixteen was no defence to taking an unmarried girl under sixteen out of her father's possession, since the statute protected the girl †. Under the BNS, bigamy (s. 82; IPC 494) allows a defence where the spouse has been absent and unheard of for seven years ‡.
7. **Mistake of fact and mens rea.** Mistake of fact negatives the guilty mind. Mistake of the law of another country or of a private right can sometimes be a mistake of fact (for example a claim of right in property: no dishonest intention).
8. **Difference between s. 14 and s. 17.** Section 14 applies where the actor believes he is **bound** by law (a duty); s. 17 where he believes he is **justified** (a right). The difference is in what he believes.

## 3. Judicial acts (BNS ss. 15, 16; IPC ss. 77, 78)

1. **Section 15 (IPC 77).** Nothing is an offence which is done by a **Judge** when acting **judicially** in the exercise of any power which is, or which in good faith he believes to be, given to him by law †.
2. **Section 16 (IPC 78).** Nothing which is done in pursuance of, or which is warranted by, the **judgment or order of a Court of Justice**, if done whilst such judgment or order remains in force, is an offence, notwithstanding the Court may have had no jurisdiction to pass it, provided the person doing the act in good faith believes that the Court had jurisdiction †.
3. **Purpose.** To protect the independence of the judiciary and the officers who carry out orders (a police officer arresting under a warrant; a jailor carrying out a sentence). The civil counterpart is the Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850, and the Judges (Protection) Act, 1985 †.
4. **Limits.** The protection is for **judicial** acts, not administrative acts of a judge; and for acts done in **good faith**. A magistrate who knowingly passes an order without jurisdiction is not protected.

## 4. Accident (BNS s. 18; IPC s. 80)

1. **Text (IPC s. 80 †).** *"Nothing is an offence which is done by accident or misfortune, and without any criminal intention or knowledge in the doing of a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means and with proper care and caution."*
2. **Essentials.** (i) The act is **lawful**; (ii) done in a **lawful manner by lawful means**; (iii) with **proper care and caution**; (iv) **without** criminal intention or knowledge; (v) the harm is by **accident or misfortune**.
3. **Illustration (IPC).** A is at work with a hatchet; the head flies off and kills a man who is standing nearby. If there was no want of proper caution on A's part, his act is excusable and not an offence.
4. **Accident and negligence.** If the harm results from want of care, there is no accident, and the actor may be liable for rash or negligent death (BNS s. 106; IPC 304A) ‡.
5. **Case law.** The defence fails when the act was unlawful to begin with (a fatal fight, a prohibited act) or when proper care was not taken; I have not listed a reported case because I could not confirm one.
6. **Accident and private defence.** A person who fires in private defence at an assailant and accidentally kills a bystander may be protected by s. 44 (IPC 106) where there was a deadly assault and no other way to defend.

## 5. Necessity (BNS s. 19; IPC s. 81)

1. **Text (IPC s. 81, paraphrased †).** Nothing is an offence merely by reason of its being done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it be done **without any criminal intention to cause harm**, and in **good faith** for the purpose of **preventing or avoiding other harm** to person or property. **Explanation:** whether the harm to be prevented or avoided was of such a nature and so imminent as to justify the risk of doing the act with the knowledge that it was likely to cause harm is a question of fact.
2. **Illustration (IPC).** A, the captain of a steam vessel, finds that, before he can stop his vessel, he must inevitably run down a boat B with twenty or thirty passengers unless he changes the course; by changing course he must run down a boat C with only two passengers. If he changes his course without any intention of running down C, and in good faith to avoid the danger to B's passengers, he is not guilty, even though he knew C might be run down.
3. **Essentials.** (i) Harm is **likely**, but there is **no criminal intention** to cause it; (ii) the act is done in **good faith**; (iii) the purpose is to avoid **greater harm**; (iv) the harm avoided is **imminent**.
4. **Necessity is not a defence to murder.** *R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884) 14 QBD 273: shipwrecked sailors killed and ate the cabin boy to survive; the court held that necessity is no defence to murder and sentenced them to death (commuted to six months) †. By contrast, in *Re A (Children)* [2001] Fam 147 the Court of Appeal allowed the separation of conjoined twins, knowing it would cause the death of the weaker twin, relying on necessity; the decision is much debated †.
5. **Indian position.** Section 19 speaks of "harm" and its terms are wide, but because s. 32 (compulsion) excludes murder, the courts will not accept necessity for intentional killing of an innocent person. Necessity may justify a lesser harm to avoid a greater: pulling down a house to stop a fire from spreading; breaking a lock to rescue a child †.

## 6. Infancy (BNS ss. 20, 21; IPC ss. 82, 83)

1. **Section 20 (IPC 82).** Nothing is an offence which is done by a **child under seven years** of age. The presumption is **conclusive** (*doli incapax*): the child cannot be guilty.
2. **Section 21 (IPC 83).** Nothing is an offence which is done by a child **above seven and under twelve** who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge of the nature and consequences of his conduct on that occasion. The presumption of incapacity is **rebuttable**: the prosecution must show the child's understanding.
3. **Common law.** Under seven: no liability; seven to fourteen: rebuttable presumption of incapacity; the Children and Young Persons Act 1963 raised the English age to ten, and the presumption was abolished by the Crime and Disorder Act 1998, s. 34 †.
4. **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.** A **child in conflict with law** is under **18**. The Act overrides ordinary criminal trial: the child is dealt with by the Juvenile Justice Board. For a **heinous offence** committed by a child **between 16 and 18**, the Board may conduct a preliminary assessment (s. 15) and the Children's Court may try the child as an adult (s. 18(3)); no death penalty or life without release for any child (s. 21) †. The Act was passed after the **Nirbhaya** case (2012) and the debate on lowering the age. *Salil Bali v. Union of India* (2013) 7 SCC 705 upheld the age of 18 †. *Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi* (2020) 2 SCC 787 held that an offence with a maximum of more than seven years and no minimum is a **serious**, not "heinous", offence †.
5. **Proof of age.** Age is determined by the school certificate, birth certificate, or medical (ossification) test, in that order (JJ Act, s. 94 †); in doubt the benefit goes to the child. The plea can be raised **at any stage**, even in the Supreme Court (*Gopinath Ghosh v. State of W.B.* 1984 Supp SCC 228 †; *Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan* (2009) 13 SCC 211 †).
6. **Note.** Section 21 is not a defence for a child above twelve. The BNS defines **child** as a person under 18 (s. 2(3) ‡) for sexual offences and other offences against children, but not for the general-exception age limits (7 and 12) †.

## 7. Insanity (unsoundness of mind) (BNS s. 22; IPC s. 84)

### 7.1 The text

BNS s. 22 (as IPC s. 84): *"Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law."* (The August 2023 Bill had used "mental illness"; the Act as passed uses "unsoundness of mind" †.)

### 7.2 Essentials

1. **Unsoundness of mind**: not defined in the Code. Courts take it as a mental condition, not necessarily a medical disease, which prevents the cognitive faculties from working.
2. **At the time of the act**: the accused must have been insane at the **material time**, not before or after.
3. **Incapable of knowing** (i) the **nature of the act**, or (ii) that it is **wrong or contrary to law**. It is a **cognitive** test, not a test of will.
4. **Burden on the accused**, on a preponderance of probabilities (Evidence Act s. 105; BSA †).

### 7.3 Legal insanity and medical insanity

| Point | Legal insanity | Medical insanity |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Law (s. 22) | Psychiatry |
| Test | Cognitive incapacity **at the time of the act** | Any mental disorder |
| Effect | Defence | None by itself |
| Examples | Cannot know what he does or that it is wrong | Schizophrenia, epilepsy, depression, personality disorders |
| Result | Acquittal (then safe custody) | A mentally ill person may still be liable |

The Supreme Court has said that "unsoundness of mind" in s. 84 means **legal** insanity, not mere medical insanity (*Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.* (2008) 16 SCC 109 †). Not every mental disorder is a defence; only a cognitive incapacity at the time of the act.

### 7.4 M'Naghten rules and the Indian law

- ***M'Naghten's Case* (1843) 10 Cl & F 200.** Daniel M'Naghten shot Edward Drummond, secretary to Sir Robert Peel, in the delusion that he was being persecuted. He was acquitted. The House of Lords' judges laid down the rules: every man is presumed sane; to establish the defence it must be clearly proved that, at the time of the act, the party was labouring under such a **defect of reason, from disease of the mind**, as not to know the **nature and quality** of the act, or if he did know it, that he did not know that it was **wrong** †. Section 84 follows these rules, with "contrary to law" added.
- **Differences.** The IPC speaks of "unsoundness of mind", not "disease of the mind"; it adds "contrary to law"; and Indian courts have not accepted "irresistible impulse" or "insane delusion" as separate tests.
- ***Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat*** AIR 1964 SC 1563: the accused killed his wife by multiple stab wounds, and pleaded insanity. The Court held that (i) the onus is on the accused, but (ii) he need only prove it by a preponderance of probability, like a party in a civil case; (iii) the court must look at the whole conduct, including the motive, the manner (planning, concealment), and conduct before and after the act; and (iv) mere abnormality of mind, a fit of anger or a family history of insanity is not enough †.
- ***Sheralli Wali Mohammed v. State of Maharashtra*** (1972) 4 SCC 79: the accused killed his young daughter (facts from memory); the Court rejected the plea, since the lack of motive and some abnormal behaviour, without more, did not prove legal insanity †.
- **Epilepsy, sleep-walking, hypnosis, delirium** may qualify if they produce the cognitive incapacity; **mere** lack of motive or violent behaviour does not.

### 7.5 Procedure and consequence

- If the accused is of unsound mind at the **trial**, the court inquires and may postpone the trial until he recovers (BNSS ss. 367–368; Cr.P.C. ss. 328–329) †. If he was of unsound mind at the **time of the act**, the verdict is "**acquitted on the ground of unsoundness of mind**"; the court may order detention in safe custody (BNSS s. 369 †; Cr.P.C. s. 330) in a mental health establishment, subject to the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 †.
- The Supreme Court has recognised mental illness as a ground for commuting a death sentence (*Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014) 3 SCC 1 †).

## 8. Intoxication (BNS ss. 23, 24; IPC ss. 85, 86)

| Point | Involuntary intoxication (s. 23; IPC 85) | Voluntary intoxication (s. 24; IPC 86) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | The intoxicant was **administered without his knowledge or against his will** | The accused drank or took it **voluntarily** |
| Effect | If, by reason of it, he is **incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it is wrong or contrary to law**, he is not guilty | **Not** a defence; he is presumed to have the **same knowledge** as if he had not been intoxicated |
| Intention | – | If an **intention** is needed, intoxication is considered in deciding whether he had it; if the thing was administered without his knowledge or against his will, the intention is not presumed |

1. **Section 24 in plain words.** Where an act is not an offence unless done with a particular **knowledge or intention**, a person doing it in a state of voluntary intoxication is treated as having the **knowledge** he would have had if sober, but **not** necessarily the **intention**: the court examines the facts to see whether he in fact formed it †.
2. ***Basdev v. State of PEPSU*** AIR 1956 SC 488: (facts from memory) a landowner shot a boy at a gathering after drinking. The Court held that drunkenness is not an excuse, but that where the offence needs a **specific intent**, the evidence of intoxication must be considered to see whether he was so drunk as to be **incapable of forming that intent**; mere drunkenness does not show it. The Court looked at the conduct (he was able to walk, talk and give directions) and confirmed the conviction †.
3. ***DPP v. Beard*** [1920] AC 479: drunkenness not a defence to murder (rape in the course of which the girl was suffocated) unless it renders the accused incapable of forming the **specific intent**; the House of Lords stated rules †. ***DPP v. Majewski*** [1977] AC 443: voluntary intoxication is no defence to offences of **basic intent**, because getting intoxicated is itself reckless; it may be relevant for **specific intent** crimes †.
4. **Comparison.** English law distinguishes basic and specific intent; the IPC speaks of knowledge (presumed) and intention (a question of fact).
5. **Delirium tremens.** Insanity caused by long drinking may be a defence under s. 22 (it is a disease of the mind), not s. 24 †.

## 9. Consent (BNS ss. 25–31; IPC ss. 87–93)

| BNS † | IPC | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| s. 25 | 87 | An act **not intended**, and **not known** to be likely, to cause **death or grievous hurt**, done by **consent** of a person above **eighteen**, is not an offence by reason of any harm it may cause to that person (a fencing match with foils) |
| s. 26 | 88 | An act **not intended to cause death**, done by consent in **good faith for the person's benefit** (a surgeon performs an operation with consent; the patient dies) |
| s. 27 | 89 | An act done in **good faith for the benefit of a child under twelve or a person of unsound mind**, by or with the consent of the **guardian**; excludes intentionally causing death, or doing anything known likely to cause death, other than for preventing death or grievous hurt or curing grievous disease |
| s. 28 | 90 | **Consent is not valid** if given under **fear of injury** or **misconception of fact**, by a person of **unsound mind** or **intoxicated**, or by a **child under twelve** |
| s. 29 | 91 | Acts that are **offences independently of the harm** to the person consenting (for example causing a miscarriage) are not protected by ss. 25–27 |
| s. 30 | 92 | **No consent**: an act done in **good faith for the benefit** of a person without consent is not an offence if the circumstances make it **impossible** to get consent in time (a surgeon operates on an unconscious accident victim) |
| s. 31 | 93 | A **communication** made in good faith to a person for his benefit, even if it causes him harm (a doctor tells a patient that he is about to die, and the shock kills him) |

1. **Limits.** Consent cannot justify **death** (s. 25 excludes it; s. 26 requires good faith and benefit) or **grievous hurt** (unless for his benefit). A person cannot consent to his own murder: **consent** reduces murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS s. 101, Exception 5; IPC s. 300, Exception 5) † (see Unit 4A1).
2. **Sport and games.** Consent covers injuries within the rules of the game; not those from deliberate violence outside the rules.
3. ***Poonai Fattemah v. Emperor*** (1869) 12 WR (Cr) 7: a snake-charmer who invited a person to be bitten by a venomous snake and the person died: consent was no defence, since death was likely †.
4. ***R v. Brown*** [1994] 1 AC 212: consent is no defence to sadomasochistic acts causing actual bodily harm †.
5. **Medical treatment.** *Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda* (2008) 2 SCC 1: consent for diagnosis does not authorise a different surgery, unless an emergency justifies it under s. 92 †. **Euthanasia:** *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1 held that passive euthanasia (withdrawing life support) and advance directives (living wills) are lawful, with safeguards; active euthanasia is murder or culpable homicide †.
6. **Consent obtained by fear or misconception** (s. 28) is the base for the offences of rape (consent obtained by fear, deception, etc.), cheating and kidnapping.

## 10. Compulsion by threats (BNS s. 32; IPC s. 94)

1. **Text (IPC s. 94 †).** *"Except murder, and offences against the State punishable with death, nothing is an offence which is done by a person who is compelled to do it by threats, which, at the time of doing it, reasonably cause the apprehension that instant death to that person will otherwise be the consequence: provided the person doing the act did not of his own accord, or from a reasonable apprehension of harm to himself short of instant death, place himself in the situation by which he became subject to such constraint."*
2. **Essentials.** (i) A **threat of instant death** (not future harm, not hurt); (ii) the apprehension is **reasonable**; (iii) the actor did not voluntarily **join** the group (a person who joins a gang of dacoits cannot plead that he was later forced to commit crimes); (iv) the offence is not **murder** or an offence against the State punishable with **death** (waging war, s. 147; IPC 121).
3. **Illustration (IPC).** A is compelled by gang members at gunpoint to assist them in breaking into a house: he can plead compulsion unless he joined them voluntarily.
4. **Comparison.** *DPP for Northern Ireland v. Lynch* [1975] AC 653 allowed duress as a defence to a secondary party to murder; *R v. Howe* [1987] AC 417 overruled it and held that duress is no defence to murder, either for the principal or for a secondary party †.
5. **Duress and necessity.** Duress is a threat from **another person**; necessity is a pressure from **circumstances**.

## 11. Trifles (de minimis) (BNS s. 33; IPC s. 95)

1. **Text (IPC s. 95 †).** *"Nothing is an offence by reason that it causes, or that it is intended to cause, or that it is known to be likely to cause, any harm, if that harm is so slight that no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain of such harm."*
2. **Maxim.** *De minimis non curat lex*: the law does not concern itself with trifles.
3. **Test.** Whether the harm is trifling depends on the **nature of the injury**, the **relation of the parties**, the **knowledge or intention** with which the harm was caused, and the **circumstances** (for example, whether the harm was caused as a joke). It is an **objective** test: no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain. In *Veeda Menezes v. Yusuf Khan* AIR 1966 SC 1773 the Supreme Court explained that whether harm is "so slight" depends on the nature of the injury, the relationship of the parties, the knowledge and intention of the doer and the other circumstances (principle from memory †; the facts of the case were not checked).
4. **Examples.** A pat on the back, a mere push in a crowd, taking a pinch of snuff, drawing water from a stream in a field.
5. **Applied sparingly.** It is a defence only for harm that is truly slight; courts are slow to apply it where the law treats the kind of wrong seriously whatever the size of the harm (for example offences against women or children) †.

## 12. The right of private defence (BNS ss. 34–44; IPC ss. 96–106)

### 12.1 Basis

1. **Meaning.** Every person has a right to protect his own body and property, and the body and property of any other person, against unlawful aggression, using necessary force, even to the point of causing death in the cases allowed. An act done in the exercise of this right is **not an offence** (s. 34; IPC 96).
2. **Reasons.** The State cannot protect every person at every moment; the right is a recognition of the natural instinct of self-preservation; and the law does not expect a person to run away from a wrongdoer. It is **preventive, not punitive**: it exists to ward off an attack, not to avenge it.
3. **Supreme Court statements.** *Jai Dev v. State of Punjab* AIR 1963 SC 612: the right is a **defensive** right, not available when there is time to have recourse to the public authorities; the means used must be reasonable, but a person under attack cannot be expected to weigh his reaction **in golden scales** †. *Yogendra Morarji v. State of Gujarat* (1980) 2 SCC 218: the accused need not **retreat** if he is attacked; the test is whether there was a reasonable apprehension and whether he used only the force needed †.

### 12.2 The scheme

| BNS † | IPC | Content |
|---|---|---|
| s. 34 | 96 | Things done in private defence are not offences |
| s. 35 | 97 | The right of private defence of the **body** (own or another's, against any offence affecting the human body) and of **property** (movable or immovable, against theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass or their attempts), subject to s. 37 |
| s. 36 | 98 | The right exists against an act which would be an offence but for the **youth, unsoundness of mind, intoxication or misconception** of the doer |
| s. 37 | 99 | **Restrictions**: no right (1) against a **public servant** acting in good faith under colour of his office, though the act may not be strictly justifiable by law; (2) against a person acting under the **direction of a public servant**, in good faith under colour of office; (3) where there is **time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities**; (4) the right never extends to the **infliction of more harm than is necessary** |
| s. 38 | 100 | When the right of private defence of the body **extends to causing death**: **seven** cases ‡ |
| s. 39 | 101 | In other cases, the right extends to causing **any harm other than death** |
| s. 40 | 102 | **Commencement and continuance** of the right in respect of the body |
| s. 41 | 103 | When the right of private defence of **property** extends to causing death |
| s. 42 | 104 | In other cases, any harm other than death |
| s. 43 | 105 | Commencement and continuance of the right in respect of property |
| s. 44 | 106 | **Deadly assault**: the right to run the risk of harm to an innocent person |

### 12.3 Section 37 (IPC 99): the four restrictions

1. **Against public servants.** No right of private defence against an act done, or attempted to be done, by a public servant acting in good faith under colour of his office, though that act may not be strictly justifiable by law. *Explanation 1*: a person is not deprived of the right unless he knows or has reason to believe that the doer is a public servant. *Explanation 2*: the same if the act is done under the direction of a public servant, unless he knows or has reason to believe it and has been told the authority.
2. **Time to approach authorities.** The right is not available where there is **time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities**.
3. **Proportion.** The right in no case extends to the inflicting of **more harm than it is necessary** to inflict for the purpose of defence.

(Section 37's text is paraphrased †.)

### 12.4 Private defence of the body (ss. 35, 38–40)

**Section 38 (IPC 100)**: the right of private defence of the body extends, under the restrictions of s. 37, to the voluntary causing of **death or of any other harm** to the assailant, if the offence which occasions the exercise of the right is of any of the following **seven descriptions** ‡:

1. **First.** An assault which causes a **reasonable apprehension that death will otherwise be the consequence**.
2. **Secondly.** An assault which causes a reasonable apprehension that **grievous hurt** will otherwise be the consequence.
3. **Thirdly.** An assault with the intention of committing **rape**.
4. **Fourthly.** An assault with the intention of gratifying **unnatural lust** (the BNS has dropped s. 377; check the current wording of this clause †).
5. **Fifthly.** An assault with the intention of **kidnapping or abducting**.
6. **Sixthly.** An assault with the intention of **wrongfully confining** a person, under circumstances which may reasonably cause him to apprehend that he will be unable to have recourse to the public authorities for his release.
7. **Seventhly.** An act of **throwing or administering acid**, or an attempt to do so, which may reasonably cause the apprehension that grievous hurt will otherwise be the consequence.

(The seven descriptions are from the IPC as amended in 2013, which the BNS keeps; the exact BNS wording of the fourth clause is not checked †.)

**Section 39 (IPC 101)**: if the offence is not of these descriptions, the right extends to causing **any harm other than death**.

**Section 40 (IPC 102)**: the right **commences** as soon as a **reasonable apprehension of danger** to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, though the offence may not have been committed; and it **continues** as long as the apprehension of danger to the body continues.

### 12.5 Private defence of property (ss. 35, 41–43)

**Section 41 (IPC 103)**: the right of private defence of property extends to causing **death** to the wrongdoer if the offence, the committing of which or the attempting to commit which occasions the exercise of the right, is of any of these descriptions †:

1. **First.** **Robbery**.
2. **Secondly.** **House-breaking by night**.
3. **Thirdly.** **Mischief by fire** committed on any building, tent or vessel which is used as a human dwelling or as a place for the custody of property.
4. **Fourthly.** **Theft, mischief or house-trespass** under such circumstances as may reasonably cause apprehension that **death or grievous hurt** will be the consequence if the right is not exercised.

**Section 42 (IPC 104)**: in other cases of theft, mischief or criminal trespass, the right extends to any harm **other than death**.

**Section 43 (IPC 105)**: the right **commences** when a reasonable apprehension of danger to the property commences; it **continues**:

- against **theft**: till the offender has effected his retreat with the property, or the assistance of the public authorities is obtained, or the property has been recovered;
- against **robbery**: as long as the offender causes or attempts to cause to any person death or hurt or wrongful restraint, or as long as the fear of instant death or instant hurt or instant personal restraint continues;
- against **criminal trespass or mischief**: as long as the offender continues in the commission of it;
- against **house-breaking by night**: as long as the house-trespass which has begun by such house-breaking continues. †

**Only property offences listed.** The right of private defence of property exists only against **theft, robbery, mischief and criminal trespass** (and attempts). It does not exist against cheating, criminal breach of trust or misappropriation, because in those cases the owner has delivered the property or has a civil remedy.

### 12.6 Section 44 (IPC 106): deadly assault and risk to an innocent person

If, in the exercise of the right of private defence against an assault which reasonably causes the apprehension of death, the defender is so situated that he cannot effectually exercise that right without risk of harm to an **innocent person**, his right extends to the running of that risk. *Illustration (IPC)*: A is attacked by a mob who attempt to murder him. He cannot effectually exercise his right of private defence without firing on the mob, and he cannot fire without risk of harming young children mingled with the mob. A commits no offence if he fires and harms any of the children †.

### 12.7 Excess of the right: from murder to culpable homicide

If a person, in the exercise in good faith of the right of private defence, **exceeds the power** given to him by law and causes the death of the person against whom he is exercising it, **without premeditation and without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary**, he is guilty not of murder but of **culpable homicide not amounting to murder** (BNS s. 101, Exception 2; IPC s. 300, Exception 2) †. See Unit 4A1.

### 12.8 Principles laid down by the Supreme Court

1. ***Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab*** (2010) 2 SCC 333 †: the Supreme Court summarised the law (summary from memory): (i) the right is available to a person who is attacked or fears an attack, and is an **inherent** right; (ii) it can be exercised for the body and property of **others**; (iii) the accused need not plead it expressly, but the court must examine it on the evidence; (iv) the burden is on the accused, but only to a **preponderance of probabilities**; (v) the **injuries** on the accused and the prosecution's failure to explain them are relevant; (vi) the right does not extend to inflicting **more harm than necessary**; (vii) it is a **defensive** right and is available only when the **need** is immediate and **there is no time** to seek help; (viii) the court does not weigh the exercise in **golden scales**; (ix) a person attacked need not run away.
2. ***Mohinder Pal Jolly v. State of Punjab*** (1979) 3 SCC 30 †: the accused fired on a crowd that was causing a disturbance at his premises (facts from memory). The principle drawn from it is that the **right of private defence of property** extends to causing death only in the cases listed in s. 103 (IPC); firing on a crowd that is trespassing or causing mischief, without a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt, goes beyond the right (facts and final sentence not checked †).
3. ***Vidhya Singh v. State of M.P.*** (1971) 2 SCC 1 †: the Court held that when an accused person exercising the right of private defence uses force in excess, he is guilty of the offence committed in excess; the burden is on the accused; the right is a **right of defence, not retribution**.
4. ***Jai Dev v. State of Punjab*** AIR 1963 SC 612 †: the right is **defensive**, not retaliatory; it is not available when there is time to have recourse to the authorities; and the Court does not weigh the defender's reaction in golden scales. A land-possession dispute was the setting (details from memory).
5. ***Yogendra Morarji v. State of Gujarat*** (1980) 2 SCC 218 †: the accused need not run away; the test is whether he had a reasonable apprehension and used the necessary force.
6. ***Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration*** AIR 1968 SC 702 †: the accused need not plead the right of private defence if the facts on the record show it; in a dispute over land, a person in possession may resist forcible dispossession, but only with the force the need requires (principle from memory).
7. ***Puran Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1975) 4 SCC 518 †: the accused need not plead the right; it is enough if the facts show it; the Court looks at the injuries on the accused.
8. ***Amjad Khan v. State*** AIR 1952 SC 165 †: a case of a plea of private defence in the setting of communal violence; the Court looked at the apprehension at the time (details from memory).

### 12.9 Illustrations

1. A is attacked by B with a knife and cannot escape. A strikes B with a lathi and kills him. If A reasonably apprehended death, his act falls within s. 38 First and he is not guilty.
2. A fires at a thief who is running away with his television, and kills him. The thief had no weapon and A's life was not in danger. The right of private defence of property does not extend to causing death (theft is not a s. 41 offence unless it causes a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt). A is guilty (at least culpable homicide).
3. A, a police officer in uniform, arrests B under a lawful warrant. B hits him. B has no right of private defence (s. 37).

## 13. Criticism and current developments

1. **Subjectivity.** The standard of "reasonable apprehension" is hard to apply to a person faced with a sudden attack, and the court decides at leisure what the accused had seconds to decide.
2. **Private defence of property.** s. 41 allows killing to protect property in a few cases; critics call it excessive, as property is preserved at the cost of life; reformers want it limited to cases of danger to life.
3. **Insanity.** The M'Naghten test is narrow (cognitive only), and does not account for modern psychiatric understanding or volitional incapacity. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 and the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 have changed the treatment side, but the test in s. 22 is the old one †. I did not check what reform bodies have recommended.
4. **Voluntary intoxication.** Reformers say the law punishes a person for getting drunk, and the rule that knowledge is presumed but intention is not is hard to apply.
5. **Necessity.** The section is broad but little used; I did not find an Indian decision that accepts necessity as a defence to killing an innocent person.
6. **BNS.** The BNS has kept Chapter III with the same substance and the renumbering ss. 14–44 ‡. The wording of s. 22 follows the IPC ("unsoundness of mind") †. I did not find a case since 1 July 2024 that changes the position. **Not checked** exhaustively.

## 15. Quick-revision box

- **General exceptions:** BNS ss. 14–44 (IPC 76–106). Burden on the accused, but only by a preponderance of probability.
- **Mistake:** of fact, not of law; in good faith with due care (s. 14, 17). **Accident:** lawful act, lawful manner, lawful means, proper care (s. 18). **Necessity:** harm to avoid greater harm, in good faith (s. 19); no defence to murder (*Dudley & Stephens*).
- **Infancy:** under 7: absolute; 7–12: if immature (ss. 20–21); JJ Act 2015: under 18; 16–18 for heinous offences may be tried as adult.
- **Insanity (s. 22):** at the time of the act; cannot know the nature of the act or that it is wrong or contrary to law; legal, not medical; M'Naghten; *Dahyabhai*.
- **Intoxication:** involuntary (s. 23) = defence if incapable; voluntary (s. 24) = knowledge presumed, intention a question of fact; *Basdev*; *Beard*; *Majewski*.
- **Consent (ss. 25–31):** not for death or grievous hurt; 18 years; invalid if by fear, misconception, unsound mind, intoxication, child under 12.
- **Compulsion (s. 32):** threat of instant death; not murder or State offences punishable with death.
- **Trifles (s. 33):** harm so slight no person of ordinary sense would complain.
- **Private defence (ss. 34–44):** body: death in **seven** cases (s. 38); property: death in **four** cases (s. 41: robbery, house-breaking by night, mischief by fire to dwelling, theft/mischief/trespass with apprehension of death/GH); restrictions (s. 37): public servants, time to approach authorities, no more force than necessary; begins with reasonable apprehension, ends with it; excess: culpable homicide not amounting to murder (s. 101, Exception 2). *Darshan Singh; Mohinder Pal Jolly; Jai Dev; Vidhya Singh*.
