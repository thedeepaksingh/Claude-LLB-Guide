## C-08 · Discuss the right of private defence of the body. When does it extend to causing death?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q10 (C, "private defence of person up to death"), Dec 2024 Q13(c) (C, "private defence"). Related: A-62 to A-67, B-31 to B-33.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** basis and scheme (ss. 34–35) 3 · restrictions (s. 37) 3 · when it extends to death (s. 38, seven cases) 5 · other harm (s. 39) and commencement and continuance (s. 40) 2 · deadly assault (s. 44) and excess 2 · cases 3 · burden of proof 1 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Every person has a right to protect his own body, and the body of another, against unlawful aggression, even to the point of causing death in the cases the law allows. The State cannot protect every person at every moment; the right recognises the instinct of self-preservation, and the law does not require a person to run away from a wrongdoer. An act done in the lawful exercise of the right is **not an offence** (BNS s. 34; IPC s. 96). The right is **preventive, not punitive**: it is a right of defence, not of retaliation.

**1. The scheme (BNS ss. 34–44; IPC ss. 96–106)** †

| BNS | IPC | Content |
|---|---|---|
| s. 34 | 96 | Things done in private defence are not offences |
| s. 35 | 97 | Right of private defence of the **body** (own or another's, against any offence affecting the human body) and of **property**, subject to s. 37 |
| s. 36 | 98 | Right exists against an act that would be an offence but for the doer's youth, unsoundness of mind, intoxication or misconception |
| s. 37 | 99 | **Restrictions** on the right |
| s. 38 | 100 | When the right of private defence of the body **extends to causing death** |
| s. 39 | 101 | In other cases, harm **other than death** |
| s. 40 | 102 | **Commencement and continuance** (body) |
| s. 44 | 106 | **Deadly assault**: risk to an innocent person |

**2. Restrictions (BNS s. 37; IPC s. 99)** †. There is **no right** of private defence:
1. against an act done, or attempted, by a **public servant** acting in good faith under colour of his office, though the act may not be strictly justifiable by law (the person must know or have reason to believe that the doer is a public servant);
2. against an act done by the **direction of a public servant**, in good faith under colour of office;
3. where there is **time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities**;
4. and the right **never extends to the inflicting of more harm than is necessary** for the purpose of defence.

**3. When the right of private defence of the body extends to causing death (BNS s. 38; IPC s. 100).** Subject to the restrictions in s. 37, the right extends to the voluntary causing of **death or any other harm** to the assailant if the offence which occasions the exercise of the right is of any of these **seven descriptions** ‡:
1. an **assault** which causes a reasonable apprehension that **death** will otherwise be the consequence;
2. an assault which causes a reasonable apprehension that **grievous hurt** will otherwise be the consequence;
3. an assault with the intention of committing **rape**;
4. an assault with the intention of gratifying **unnatural lust** (the BNS has dropped IPC s. 377; the exact BNS wording of this clause is not checked †);
5. an assault with the intention of **kidnapping or abducting**;
6. an assault with the intention of **wrongfully confining** a person, under circumstances which may reasonably cause him to apprehend that he will be unable to have recourse to the public authorities for his release;
7. an **act of throwing or administering acid**, or an attempt to do so, which may reasonably cause the apprehension that grievous hurt will otherwise be the consequence (added to the IPC in 2013).

In **all other cases** of offences against the body, the right extends to causing **any harm other than death** (s. 39; IPC s. 101).

**4. Commencement and continuance (BNS s. 40; IPC s. 102).** The right **commences** as soon as a **reasonable apprehension of danger** to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, even though the offence has not been committed; it **continues** as long as the apprehension of danger to the body continues. After the danger has ended, force used is **retaliation**, not defence.

**5. Deadly assault and risk to an innocent person (BNS s. 44; IPC s. 106).** If, in defending against an assault that reasonably causes apprehension of death, the defender cannot effectively exercise the right without risk of harm to an innocent person, his right extends to running that risk. *Illustration:* A is attacked by a mob who attempt to murder him; he cannot defend himself without firing on the mob, and he cannot fire without risk of harm to children mingled with the mob. A commits no offence if he fires and harms any of the children †.

**6. Excess of private defence.** If a person, in the good-faith exercise of the right, **exceeds the power** given to him by law and causes the death of the person against whom he is defending, **without premeditation and without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary**, he is guilty not of murder but of **culpable homicide not amounting to murder** (BNS s. 101, Exception 2; IPC s. 300, Exception 2) †.

**7. Principles laid down by the courts** (all from memory †)
1. ***Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab*** (2010) 2 SCC 333. The Supreme Court summarised the law: the right is an **inherent** right, available to a person attacked or reasonably apprehending attack; it can be exercised for the body and property of **others**; the accused need not plead it expressly if the facts on record show it; the burden is on the accused but only to the level of **preponderance of probabilities**; **injuries on the accused**, unexplained by the prosecution, are relevant; the right does not extend to **more harm than necessary**; it is **defensive**, not retaliatory; the defender is not expected to weigh the force used in **golden scales**; and a person attacked need not run away.
2. ***Jai Dev v. State of Punjab*** (AIR 1963 SC 612). The right is a defensive right, not available where there is time to have recourse to public authorities; it cannot be measured with nicety.
3. ***Yogendra Morarji v. State of Gujarat*** (1980) 2 SCC 218. There is **no duty to retreat**; the test is whether the accused had a reasonable apprehension of danger and used only the force needed.
4. ***Vidhya Singh v. State of M.P.*** (1971) 2 SCC 1. The right is one of **defence, not retribution**; the burden is on the accused; force in excess is an offence.
5. ***Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration*** (AIR 1968 SC 702). The accused need not plead private defence if the facts on record show it; a person in possession may resist forcible dispossession, but only with the force the need requires.
6. ***Puran Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1975) 4 SCC 518. It is enough if the facts show the right; the court looks at the injuries on the accused.

**8. Burden of proof.** The burden of proving that the case falls within a General Exception is on the accused (Evidence Act s. 105; Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 108 †). He discharges it by a **preponderance of probabilities**, not beyond reasonable doubt, and may do so from the prosecution's own evidence. The court presumes the absence of the circumstances unless the accused proves them.

**9. Illustrations**
1. A is attacked by B with a knife and cannot escape. A strikes B with a lathi and kills him. If A reasonably apprehended death, his act falls within s. 38 (first) and he is not guilty.
2. A, attacked by B with a lathi, disarms him. B runs away. A pursues and kills him. The danger had ended; the right had ceased (s. 40). A is guilty, at least of culpable homicide.
3. A, a police officer in uniform, arrests B under a lawful warrant. B hits him. B has no right of private defence (s. 37).
4. A, with reasonable apprehension of death, fires at B, an assailant armed with a spear; the bullet also kills a bystander. A is protected by s. 44 if he could not defend himself without that risk.

**10. Criticism.** "Reasonable apprehension" is judged by the court at leisure although the accused had seconds to decide. The listing of seven cases is rigid. Courts therefore apply a **practical** test, not a mathematical one, and scrutinise the injuries on both sides to see who was the aggressor. In a free fight, where both sides are aggressors, the right is not available to either.

**Conclusion.** The right of private defence of the body extends to causing death only in the seven cases of s. 38, within the restrictions of s. 37, from the moment a reasonable apprehension arises until it ceases. Beyond that, the defender may cause harm other than death. A defender who exceeds the right in good faith, without premeditation, is guilty of culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

## C-09 · Discuss the right of private defence of property. When does it extend to causing death?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q9 (C, "private defence of property, up to death"). Related: A-62 to A-67, B-31 to B-33.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** basis and extent (s. 35) 3 · restrictions 2 · when it extends to death (s. 41, four cases) 5 · other harm (s. 42) 1 · commencement and continuance (s. 43) 4 · cases and limits 3 · excess and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** The right of private defence extends to **property** as well as body (BNS s. 35; IPC s. 97). It may be exercised for property of the defender or of **any other person**, movable or immovable, but only against four kinds of offence: **theft, robbery, mischief and criminal trespass**, or an attempt to commit any of them ‡. It extends to causing **death** only in the cases listed in BNS s. 41 (IPC s. 103). It is more limited than the right of private defence of the body, because property is not worth a human life unless the circumstances bring the offence close to a danger to life.

**1. Offences against which the right exists (BNS s. 35; IPC s. 97).** The right is available to defend property "against any act which is an offence falling under the definition of **theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass**, or which is an attempt to commit theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass", subject to the restrictions in s. 37 †. The right does **not** exist against cheating, criminal breach of trust or misappropriation, because in those cases the owner has delivered the property and has a remedy in law.

**2. Restrictions (BNS s. 37; IPC s. 99)** †. As for the body: no right against a public servant acting in good faith under colour of his office, or a person acting under his direction; no right where there is time to have recourse to the public authorities; and no more harm than is necessary may be inflicted.

**3. When the right of private defence of property extends to causing death (BNS s. 41; IPC s. 103).** The right extends, subject to s. 37, to the voluntary causing of **death or any other harm** to the wrongdoer if the offence, the committing of which or the attempting to commit which occasions the exercise of the right, is of any of these descriptions †:
1. **Robbery.**
2. **House-breaking by night.**
3. **Mischief by fire** committed on any building, tent or vessel which is used as a human dwelling or as a place for the custody of property.
4. **Theft, mischief or house-trespass** under such circumstances as may reasonably cause apprehension that **death or grievous hurt** will be the consequence if the right of private defence is not exercised.

**4. Other cases (BNS s. 42; IPC s. 104).** If the offence is theft, mischief or criminal trespass not of the above descriptions, the right extends to causing **any harm other than death** to the wrongdoer.

**5. Commencement and continuance (BNS s. 43; IPC s. 105).** The right **commences** when a reasonable apprehension of danger to the property commences. It **continues** †:
- against **theft**: till the offender has effected his retreat with the property, or the assistance of the public authorities is obtained, or the property has been recovered;
- against **robbery**: as long as the offender causes or attempts to cause to any person death or hurt or wrongful restraint, or as long as the fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant personal restraint continues;
- against **criminal trespass or mischief**: as long as the offender continues in the commission of it;
- against **house-breaking by night**: as long as the house-trespass which has begun by such house-breaking continues.

**6. Deadly assault (BNS s. 44; IPC s. 106).** Where the defender faces a reasonable apprehension of death and cannot defend without risk to an innocent person, his right extends to running that risk †.

**7. Comparison with private defence of the body**

| Point | Body (ss. 38–40) | Property (ss. 41–43) |
|---|---|---|
| Offences | Any offence affecting the human body | Only theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass (and attempts) |
| Death caused when | Seven cases ‡ (apprehension of death or grievous hurt, rape, etc.) | Four cases † (robbery; house-breaking by night; mischief by fire to a dwelling; theft, mischief or house-trespass with apprehension of death or grievous hurt) |
| Commencement | Reasonable apprehension of danger to the body | Reasonable apprehension of danger to the property |
| Continuance | While the apprehension of danger continues | Depends on the offence (theft: till retreat or recovery, etc.) |

**8. Cases** (from memory †)
- ***Mohinder Pal Jolly v. State of Punjab*** (1979) 3 SCC 30. The accused fired on a crowd that was causing a disturbance at his premises, with fatal result. The Supreme Court held that the right of private defence of property extends to causing death only in the cases listed in the Code; firing on a crowd that was trespassing or causing mischief, without a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt, went beyond the right (facts and final sentence not checked).
- ***Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration*** (AIR 1968 SC 702). A person in possession may resist forcible dispossession from land, but only with the force the need requires; the accused need not plead the right if the facts show it.
- ***Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab*** (2010) 2 SCC 333. The general principles: the right is **defensive, not retaliatory**; there is no duty to retreat; the defender is not judged in golden scales; the burden is on the accused to a preponderance of probabilities.
- ***Jai Dev v. State of Punjab*** (AIR 1963 SC 612). No right where there is time to have recourse to the authorities.

**9. Excess of the right.** If the right is exceeded in good faith, without premeditation and without intention of doing more harm than necessary, and death results, the offence is culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS s. 101, Exception 2; IPC s. 300, Exception 2) †.

**10. Illustrations**
1. A fires at a thief who is running away with his television and kills him. The thief had no weapon and A's life was not in danger. The right does not extend to causing death (theft is not a s. 41 offence unless it causes a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt). A is guilty, at least of culpable homicide.
2. Robbers armed with swords enter A's house at night to rob him. A shoots one dead. Robbery and house-breaking by night are s. 41 offences; A is protected.
3. B sets fire to A's dwelling house at night. A fires at B and kills him. Mischief by fire on a dwelling is a s. 41 offence; A is protected.
4. A sees B cutting a tree on A's land. A shoots B dead. This is mischief or criminal trespass without apprehension of death or grievous hurt; the right extends to harm other than death only (s. 42), so A is guilty.

**11. Criticism.** Section 41 allows a life to be taken to protect property in a few cases. Critics say it is excessive and that it should be limited to cases of danger to life. The Supreme Court has therefore read the section narrowly, insisting on a **reasonable apprehension** of danger to life where the section requires it and on proof of the actual circumstances.

**Conclusion.** The right of private defence of property exists only against theft, robbery, mischief and criminal trespass. It extends to causing death only in the four cases listed in s. 41, within the restrictions of s. 37; otherwise it allows harm short of death (s. 42). It begins with a reasonable apprehension of danger to the property and ends as s. 43 provides.

## C-10 · Discuss insanity and intoxication as defences to criminal liability

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2024 Q1 (A-55, "legal v. medical insanity"). Related: A-53 to A-57, B-28, B-29.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** insanity: text and essentials 4 · legal v. medical 2 · M'Naghten and cases 4 · procedure and burden 1 · intoxication: involuntary 2 · voluntary 3 · cases 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Code recognises that a person who cannot understand what he does, or does not know that it is wrong, lacks the guilty mind that criminal liability requires. Insanity is dealt with in BNS **s. 22** (IPC s. 84), and intoxication in BNS **ss. 23 and 24** (IPC ss. 85 and 86) †.

**A. INSANITY (unsoundness of mind): BNS s. 22; IPC s. 84**

**1. The text.** "Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of **unsoundness of mind**, is **incapable of knowing the nature of the act**, or that he is doing what is either **wrong or contrary to law**." †

**2. Essentials**
1. **Unsoundness of mind**: not defined in the Code. It is a mental condition that prevents the cognitive faculties from working.
2. **At the time of the act**: the accused must have been insane at the material time, not before or after it.
3. **Incapable of knowing** (i) the **nature of the act**, or (ii) that it is **wrong or contrary to law**. The test is **cognitive**, not a test of will.
4. The **burden** is on the accused, by a preponderance of probabilities (Evidence Act s. 105; BSA s. 108 †).

**3. Legal insanity and medical insanity**

| Point | Legal insanity | Medical insanity |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Law (s. 22) | Psychiatry |
| Test | Cognitive incapacity **at the time of the act** | Any mental disorder |
| Effect | A defence | None by itself |
| Examples | Cannot know what he does or that it is wrong | Schizophrenia, epilepsy, depression, personality disorders |

The Supreme Court holds that "unsoundness of mind" means **legal** insanity, not mere medical insanity (*Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.*, (2008) 16 SCC 109 †). Not every mental disorder is a defence: only cognitive incapacity at the time of the act.

**4. The M'Naghten rules (1843).** Daniel M'Naghten shot Edward Drummond, secretary to Sir Robert Peel, under the delusion that he was being persecuted, and was acquitted. The judges laid down: every man is presumed sane; to establish the defence it must be proved that, at the time of the act, the accused was labouring under such a **defect of reason, from disease of the mind**, as not to know the **nature and quality** of the act, or, if he did know it, that he did not know that it was **wrong** †. Section 22 follows these rules, with "contrary to law" added. Indian courts have not accepted "irresistible impulse" as a separate test.

**5. Indian cases**
- ***Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat*** (AIR 1964 SC 1563): the accused killed his wife with multiple stab wounds and pleaded insanity. The Court held that the onus is on the accused, but only to a **preponderance of probability**; that the court looks at the whole conduct, including motive, planning, concealment and conduct before and after the act; and that mere abnormality, a fit of anger or a family history of insanity is not enough †.
- ***Sheralli Wali Mohammed v. State of Maharashtra*** (1972) 4 SCC 79: lack of motive and some abnormal behaviour, without more, did not prove legal insanity †.
- ***Hari Singh Gond*** (2008): legal, not medical, insanity †.
- **Epilepsy, sleep-walking, delirium** may qualify if they produce the cognitive incapacity; mere violent behaviour does not.

**6. Procedure and consequence.** If the accused is of unsound mind at the **trial**, the court inquires and may postpone the trial until he recovers (BNSS ss. 367–368; CrPC ss. 328–329 †). If he was of unsound mind at the **time of the act**, the verdict is "acquitted on the ground of unsoundness of mind", and the court may order safe custody in a mental health establishment (BNSS s. 369; CrPC s. 330 †), subject to the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017. The Supreme Court treats mental illness as a ground for commuting a death sentence (*Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India*, (2014) 3 SCC 1 †).

**B. INTOXICATION: BNS ss. 23, 24; IPC ss. 85, 86**

**7. Involuntary intoxication (s. 23; IPC s. 85).** Nothing is an offence done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of intoxication, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it is wrong or contrary to law, **provided the thing which intoxicated him was administered to him without his knowledge or against his will** †.

**8. Voluntary intoxication (s. 24; IPC s. 86).** Where an act is not an offence unless done with a particular **knowledge or intention**, a person who does it in a state of **voluntary** intoxication is presumed to have the **same knowledge** as he would have had if he had not been intoxicated, **unless the thing which intoxicated him was administered without his knowledge or against his will**. As to **intention**, there is no presumption: the court examines the evidence of intoxication to decide whether he in fact formed the intention †.

| Point | Involuntary (s. 23) | Voluntary (s. 24) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Administered without knowledge or against will | Taken voluntarily |
| Effect | Defence if incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it is wrong | **Not a defence**; knowledge presumed as if sober |
| Intention | – | A question of fact |

**9. Cases**
- ***Basdev v. State of PEPSU*** (AIR 1956 SC 488): a landowner, drunk at a gathering, shot a boy. The Court held that drunkenness is no excuse, but where the offence needs a **specific intent**, the evidence of intoxication must be considered to see whether he was so drunk as to be **incapable of forming that intent**; mere drunkenness is not enough. The accused's conduct (walking, talking, giving orders) showed he was capable, and the conviction stood †.
- ***DPP v. Beard*** [1920] AC 479: drunkenness is no defence unless it renders the accused incapable of forming the **specific intent**; insanity from drink (delirium tremens) is a defence †.
- ***DPP v. Majewski*** [1977] AC 443: voluntary intoxication is no defence to offences of **basic intent**, because getting drunk is itself reckless; it may be relevant to **specific intent** †.
- **Delirium tremens** from long drinking is a disease of the mind and may qualify as insanity under s. 22, not s. 24 †.

**10. Comparison.** Insanity requires a *disease or defect of mind* and acquits; involuntary intoxication requires an *intoxicant given without the accused's knowledge or against his will*; voluntary intoxication is generally *no defence* but is relevant to intention.

**11. Criticism.** The M'Naghten test is cognitive only, and ignores volitional incapacity and modern psychiatry; reformers favour a test based on mental illness, as in the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (I did not check what reform bodies have recommended). The law on voluntary intoxication punishes a person for getting drunk, and the rule (knowledge presumed, intention a question of fact) is hard to apply. The BNS has kept both rules unchanged ‡.

**Conclusion.** A person is exempt for unsoundness of mind only if he could not know the nature of his act or that it was wrong or contrary to law at the time; medical illness alone does not suffice. Intoxication excuses only where it was administered without knowledge or against will; voluntary intoxication gives no defence, though it may show that a specific intent was lacking.

## C-11 · Explain the general exceptions of mistake of fact, accident, necessity, infancy, consent and compulsion

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2022 Q3 (A-46, "mistake of fact"). Related: A-46 to A-52, A-58 to A-61, B-25 to B-27, B-30.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction 1 · mistake 4 · accident 3 · necessity 3 · infancy 3 · consent 3 · compulsion 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Chapter III of the BNS (**General Exceptions, ss. 14–44**; IPC Chapter IV, ss. 76–106) lists circumstances in which an act that would otherwise be an offence is not one. They apply to every offence in the Sanhita and other penal laws unless excluded ‡. The burden of proving them lies on the accused, to a preponderance of probabilities (Evidence Act s. 105; BSA s. 108 †).

**1. Mistake of fact (BNS ss. 14 and 17; IPC ss. 76 and 79).** *Ignorantia facti excusat; ignorantia juris non excusat.* Nothing is an offence done by a person who is, or who by reason of a **mistake of fact and not of law**, in **good faith** believes himself to be, **bound by law** to do it (s. 14), or **justified by law** in doing it (s. 17) †.
- **Good faith** means with due care and attention (BNS s. 2(11)) ‡: a careless belief is not in good faith.
- *Illustrations (IPC):* a soldier fires on a mob on the order of his superior, in conformity with the law: no offence; A, seeing Z commit what appears to be murder, in good faith seizes him: no offence, even though Z was acting in self-defence.
- *Section 14 v. s. 17:* s. 14 applies where the actor believes he is **bound** (a duty); s. 17 where he believes he is **justified** (a right).
- The mistake must be of **fact**; a mistake of **law** is no defence.
- *R v. Tolson* (1889): honest and reasonable belief that the husband was dead was a defence to bigamy; *R v. Prince* (1875): honest belief that the girl was over sixteen was no defence to taking an unmarried girl under sixteen out of her father's possession †.

**2. Accident (BNS s. 18; IPC s. 80).** Nothing is an offence which is done by **accident or misfortune**, without any criminal intention or knowledge, in the doing of a **lawful act** in a **lawful manner** by **lawful means** and with **proper care and caution** †. All five conditions must be present. *Illustration (IPC):* A is at work with a hatchet; the head flies off and kills a man standing nearby; if A took proper care, no offence. If the harm results from want of care, there is no accident, and the person may be liable for causing death by negligence (BNS s. 106) ‡.

**3. Necessity (BNS s. 19; IPC s. 81).** Nothing is an offence merely because it is done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause harm, if it is done **without any criminal intention** to cause harm and in **good faith** for the purpose of **preventing or avoiding other harm** to person or property. The question whether the harm to be avoided was of such a nature and so imminent as to justify the risk is a question of fact †.
- *Illustration (IPC):* the captain of a steam vessel, who cannot stop it, changes course so as to run down a boat with two passengers rather than a boat with twenty or thirty. He is not guilty if he acted in good faith and without intention to run down the smaller boat.
- *R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884): shipwrecked sailors killed and ate the cabin boy; necessity is **no defence to murder** †.
- Necessity may justify a lesser harm to avoid a greater one (pulling down a house to stop fire spreading).

**4. Infancy (BNS ss. 20, 21; IPC ss. 82, 83).** A child **under seven** cannot commit an offence (conclusive presumption, *doli incapax*). A child **above seven and under twelve** who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct on the occasion cannot commit an offence; this presumption is **rebuttable** †. The **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015** deals with a "child in conflict with law" (under 18) through the Juvenile Justice Board; a child aged **16 to 18** who commits a **heinous offence** may, after a preliminary assessment, be tried as an adult, but cannot be sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without release †. *Salil Bali v. Union of India* (2013) upheld the age of 18 †. Age can be raised at any stage, even in the Supreme Court †.

**5. Consent (BNS ss. 25–31; IPC ss. 87–93)** †
- **s. 25 (87):** an act not intended, and not known to be likely, to cause **death or grievous hurt**, done by consent of a person above **eighteen**, is not an offence by reason of harm to that person (a fencing match with foils).
- **s. 26 (88):** an act not intended to cause death, done by consent in **good faith for the person's benefit** (a surgeon's operation).
- **s. 27 (89):** an act done in good faith for the benefit of a **child under twelve or a person of unsound mind**, with the consent of the guardian.
- **s. 28 (90):** consent is **not valid** if given under **fear of injury** or **misconception of fact**, by a person of unsound mind or intoxicated, or by a child under twelve.
- **s. 29 (91):** acts that are offences independently of the harm caused (causing miscarriage) are not protected.
- **s. 30 (92):** an act done in good faith for a person's benefit, **without** consent, when it is impossible to get consent in time (a surgeon operates on an unconscious accident victim).
- **s. 31 (93):** a good-faith communication made for a person's benefit is not an offence even if it causes harm.
- **Limit:** consent cannot justify death or grievous hurt (*Poonai Fattemah v. Emperor*, 1869: a snake-charmer who invited a person to be bitten and the person died, consent was no defence †). Consent to being killed reduces murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS s. 101, Exception 5; IPC s. 300, Exception 5).
- *Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda* (2008) 2 SCC 1: consent for diagnosis does not authorise a different surgery unless an emergency justifies it †. *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1: passive euthanasia and advance directives are lawful, with safeguards †.

**6. Compulsion (BNS s. 32; IPC s. 94).** Except **murder** and **offences against the State punishable with death**, nothing is an offence done by a person who is compelled to do it by **threats which reasonably cause the apprehension that instant death** to him will otherwise be the consequence, provided he did not of his own accord, or from a reasonable apprehension of harm short of instant death, place himself in the situation by which he became subject to the constraint †. A person who voluntarily joins a gang of dacoits cannot plead that he was later forced to commit crimes. Compare *R v. Howe* (1987): duress is no defence to murder, even to a secondary party †.

**7. Trifles (BNS s. 33; IPC s. 95).** Nothing is an offence by reason that it causes, or is intended or known to be likely to cause, harm so slight that no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain of it (*de minimis non curat lex*) †. *Veeda Menezes v. Yusuf Khan* (1966) †.

**8. Judicial acts (BNS ss. 15, 16; IPC ss. 77, 78).** No offence is committed by a Judge acting judicially in the exercise of a power given by law, or by a person acting in pursuance of a court's judgment or order in good faith †.

**9. Comparison table**

| Exception | Key condition | Does not apply to |
|---|---|---|
| Mistake of fact (ss. 14, 17) | Good faith belief; fact, not law | Mistake of law |
| Accident (s. 18) | Lawful act, manner, means; proper care | Negligent acts |
| Necessity (s. 19) | No criminal intention; good faith; greater harm avoided | Murder (*Dudley and Stephens*) |
| Infancy (ss. 20–21) | Under 7: absolute; 7–12: immaturity | – |
| Consent (ss. 25–31) | Valid consent; adult; no death or GH | Death, grievous hurt |
| Compulsion (s. 32) | Threat of instant death | Murder; State offences punishable with death |

**Conclusion.** The general exceptions rest on the principle that criminal liability needs a guilty mind and a wrongful act. Mistake of fact, accident, infancy and compulsion remove or negate the mental element; necessity and consent justify or excuse particular acts. Each is limited by its text, which the answer should quote.
