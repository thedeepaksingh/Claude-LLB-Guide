## C-19 · Define theft and explain its ingredients. Distinguish theft from extortion, and say when theft amounts to robbery

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q9 (C, "theft v. extortion"), Dec 2022 Q12 (C, "theft; when robbery"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2023 Q6 (B-48, "theft ingredients"), Dec 2023 Q7 (B-49, the box problem). Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q5 (A-104, "theft v. misappropriation"), Dec 2023 Q1 (A-14, "movable property"). Related: A-102 to A-104, A-118, A-119, B-48, B-49, B-51.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** definition and Explanations 4 · five ingredients 5 · cases 3 · punishment and aggravated forms 2 · theft v. extortion 3 · when theft is robbery 3

### Answer

**Introduction.** Theft is the basic offence against property in Chapter XVII of the BNS (**s. 303**; IPC ss. 378–379). Snatching (s. 304), extortion (s. 308), robbery (s. 309) and dacoity (s. 310) are built on it ‡.

**1. Definition (BNS s. 303(1); IPC s. 378).** "Whoever, **intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, moves that property in order to such taking**, is said to commit theft." ‡

**Explanations** ‡: (1) A thing attached to the earth, not being movable property, is not the subject of theft, but becomes so as soon as it is **severed**. (2) A moving effected by the same act which affects the severance may be theft. (3) A person causes a thing to move by removing an obstacle which prevented it from moving, or by separating it from any other thing, as well as by actually moving it. (4) A person who causes an animal to move is said to move that animal and everything which, in consequence of the motion so caused, is moved by that animal. (5) The **consent** may be **express or implied**, given by the person in possession or by anyone having authority, express or implied, for that purpose.

The Section carries sixteen illustrations ‡. The main ones (from the IPC): A cuts down a tree on Z's ground to take it dishonestly: theft as soon as he severs it. A meets a bullock carrying a box of treasure and drives it to take the treasure: theft as soon as the bullock moves. A, Z's servant, runs away with plate entrusted to his care: theft (the servant's custody is the master's possession). A finds a ring lying on the high road, not in anyone's possession: no theft, but possibly misappropriation. A hides a ring on Z's table to take it later: theft at the time of first moving it.

**2. Ingredients**

| Ingredient | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1. Dishonest intention** | Intention to cause **wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another** (BNS s. 2(7), 2(36), 2(37)); it must exist **at the time of moving** |
| **2. Movable property** | Corporeal movable property (s. 2(21)); land and things attached to the earth are not, until severed. Lost or abandoned things and wild animals at large are in no one's possession and cannot be stolen. Electricity is movable property for theft (Electricity Act, 2003, s. 135 †) |
| **3. Out of the possession of another** | The property must be in someone's **possession**, actual or constructive, not merely owned by him; a servant's custody is the master's possession |
| **4. Without consent** | The owner's consent, express or implied (Explanation 5), is absent |
| **5. Moving** | Some moving of the property to effect the taking; it is not necessary to carry it away |

If the property is a claim of right made in good faith (A, believing property of Z to be his own, takes it out of B's possession), there is no dishonest intention and no theft ‡.

**3. Cases** (from memory †)
- ***K.N. Mehra v. State of Rajasthan*** (AIR 1957 SC 369). Two trainee airmen took a training aircraft from an airfield and flew off; they were charged with theft. The Supreme Court held that the **dishonest intention** existed when the aircraft started to move; the later abandonment did not matter, and **theft was complete** when the aircraft moved with that intention.
- ***Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan*** (AIR 1963 SC 1094). A public servant induced a clerk to take a file from the office so that it could be used for a private purpose, and it was later returned. The Court held that a **temporary removal** for a dishonest purpose is theft: it is not necessary that the taker intends to keep the property permanently; the intention to cause wrongful gain or loss, even temporarily, is enough.

**4. Punishment and aggravated forms.** Theft: imprisonment up to **three years**, or fine, or both; second or later conviction: rigorous imprisonment of **one to five years** and fine; the BNS makes **community service** the punishment for a **first conviction** where the value of the property is **under ₹5,000** and the property is returned or its value restored (s. 303(2) and proviso) ‡. Aggravated theft (from the IPC order †): in a dwelling house, means of transport or place of worship (s. 305); by a clerk or servant (s. 306); after preparation to cause death, hurt or restraint (s. 307). **Snatching** (s. 304, new): theft in which the offender suddenly, quickly or forcibly seizes, secures, grabs or takes away movable property from any person; up to three years and fine ‡.

**5. Theft distinguished from extortion**

| Point | Theft (s. 303) | Extortion (s. 308) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Dishonestly **moving** movable property out of another's possession without consent | Intentionally putting a person in **fear of injury** and thereby dishonestly **inducing him to deliver** property, a valuable security, or anything signed or sealed |
| Consent | **No consent** | Consent is obtained, but by **putting in fear** |
| Property | **Movable** property only | **Any** property, valuable security, or anything signed or sealed |
| How obtained | Offender **takes** | Victim **delivers** |
| Fear | None | **Fear of injury** is essential |
| Punishment | Up to 3 years (s. 303(2)) | Up to 7 years (s. 308(2)) ‡ |

*Illustration of extortion* ‡: A threatens to publish a defamatory libel concerning Z unless Z gives him money.

**6. When theft amounts to robbery (BNS s. 309(2); IPC s. 390).** In all robbery there is either theft or extortion (s. 309(1)). **Theft is robbery** if, in order to the committing of the theft, or in committing the theft, or in carrying away or attempting to carry away property obtained by the theft, the offender, for that end, **voluntarily causes or attempts to cause to any person death or hurt or wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint** ‡. Punishment: rigorous imprisonment up to **ten years** and fine (**fourteen years** if on the highway between sunset and sunrise); attempt: up to seven years; hurt caused: life, or up to ten years, and fine, for the offender and every person jointly concerned (s. 309(4)–(6)) ‡.

| Point | Theft | Robbery (by theft) |
|---|---|---|
| Violence | None needed | Causing or attempting death, hurt or wrongful restraint, or fear of **instant** such harm |
| Victim | Need not be present | Must be present |
| Punishment | Up to 3 years | RI up to 10 years (14 on the highway at night) |

**7. Related offences.** *Misappropriation* (s. 314; IPC s. 403): property comes into the hands innocently and is later converted dishonestly. *Criminal breach of trust* (s. 316): property is entrusted. The line is the point at which the dishonest intention arises (C-21).

**8. Problem (Dec 2023 Q7).** A enters B's house at night, moves a heavy box to the courtyard, opens it, finds nothing worth taking and leaves. A has moved movable property in B's possession without consent. If A intended to take the box itself, the offence of theft is complete on moving it (*K.N. Mehra*; the bullock illustration). If A intended only to take what was inside and the box was empty, he is guilty of **attempt to commit theft** (BNS s. 62; the IPC illustration of a box without jewels). A has also committed house-trespass, and lurking house-trespass or house-breaking by night with intent to commit theft (s. 331(4): up to five years and fine, up to fourteen years if the intended offence is theft) ‡. The two thefts are alternatives.

**Conclusion.** Theft needs a dishonest intention, movable property, the possession of another, no consent, and a moving. It differs from extortion in that the property is taken without consent and without fear of injury, and it becomes robbery when violence or fear of instant violence accompanies it.

## C-20 · Explain extortion, robbery and dacoity, and show how extortion may amount to robbery and robbery to dacoity

**Asked as:** Set at 10 marks: Dec 2025 Q7 (B-50, "extortion; when it amounts to dacoity"). Not yet set at 20 marks. Related: A-105 to A-108, B-50, B-51.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** extortion (definition, essentials, punishment) 6 · robbery 5 · dacoity 5 · links among the three and comparison 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Extortion, robbery and dacoity form a chain of aggravation: extortion by fear becomes **robbery** when the victim is in the presence of the offender and is put in fear of **instant** harm, and robbery becomes **dacoity** when committed by **five or more** persons. They are in BNS **ss. 308, 309 and 310** (IPC ss. 383–389, 390–394, 391–402) ‡.

**1. Extortion (BNS s. 308(1); IPC s. 383).** "Whoever **intentionally puts any person in fear of any injury** to that person, or to any other, and **thereby dishonestly induces the person so put in fear to deliver** to any person any **property or valuable security, or anything signed or sealed which may be converted into a valuable security**, commits extortion." ‡

*Illustrations* ‡: A threatens to publish a defamatory libel concerning Z unless Z gives him money: extortion. A threatens to confine Z's child wrongfully unless Z signs a promissory note: extortion. A, by putting Z in fear of grievous hurt, induces Z to sign a blank paper and deliver it: extortion. (The BNS adds an illustration of a threat of a child's death by electronic message to obtain money.)

*Essentials.* (1) Intentionally putting a person in **fear of injury** (BNS s. 2(14): harm illegally caused to body, mind, reputation or property); (2) the fear may relate to the person or **any other**; (3) **dishonest** inducement; (4) **delivery** of property, valuable security, or a signed or sealed thing by the person put in fear. Without delivery there is only an attempt, separately punished.

*Punishment (s. 308)* ‡:

| Sub-section | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Extortion | Up to **7 years**, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Putting a person in fear of injury (or attempting) to commit extortion | Up to **2 years**, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Putting a person in fear of **death or grievous hurt** to commit extortion | Up to **7 years** and fine |
| (5) | Extortion by putting a person in fear of death or grievous hurt | Up to **10 years** and fine |
| (6)–(7) | Putting a person in fear of an **accusation** of an offence punishable with death, life or up to 10 years | Up to **10 years** and fine |

(The matching of each sub-section's wording is from the page I read; confirm the exact wording †.)

**2. Robbery (BNS s. 309; IPC ss. 390–394)** ‡
1. In all robbery there is either **theft** or **extortion** (s. 309(1)).
2. **Theft is robbery** if, in order to the committing of the theft, or in committing it, or in carrying away or attempting to carry away property obtained by theft, the offender, for that end, **voluntarily causes or attempts to cause death or hurt or wrongful restraint**, or fear of **instant** death, hurt or restraint (s. 309(2)).
3. **Extortion is robbery** if the offender, at the time of committing the extortion, is **in the presence** of the person put in fear, and commits the extortion by putting that person or another in fear of **instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint**, and by so putting in fear **induces the person then and there to deliver up the thing extorted** (s. 309(3)). The offender is "present" if sufficiently near to put the other in such fear.
4. **Punishment:** rigorous imprisonment up to **ten years** and fine; on the **highway between sunset and sunrise**, up to **fourteen years** (s. 309(4)); attempt: up to seven years and fine (s. 309(5)); voluntarily causing hurt in committing or attempting robbery: the offender and any other person **jointly concerned**: life, or up to ten years, and fine (s. 309(6)).

*Illustration (IPC s. 390):* A holds Z down and fraudulently takes Z's money and jewels from Z's clothes without Z's consent: A has committed theft and, in order to commit that theft, has wilfully put Z in fear of instant restraint; A has committed robbery.

**3. Dacoity (BNS s. 310; IPC ss. 391, 395, 396, 399, 400, 402)** ‡
1. **s. 310(1).** When **five or more persons conjointly commit or attempt to commit a robbery**, or where the whole number of persons conjointly committing or attempting, and persons present and aiding, amount to five or more, every person so committing, attempting or aiding is said to commit **dacoity**.
2. **s. 310(2).** Punishment: **imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years**, and fine.
3. **s. 310(3).** If any one of five or more persons conjointly committing dacoity **commits murder**, **every one** is punishable with **death, imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years**, and fine (constructive liability: Unit 1B).
4. **s. 310(4).** Making **preparation** to commit dacoity: rigorous imprisonment up to ten years and fine.
5. **s. 310(5).** Being one of five or more persons **assembled** for the purpose of committing dacoity: up to seven years and fine.
6. **s. 310(6).** Belonging to a **gang** associated for habitually committing dacoity: life, or up to ten years, and fine.
Related sections † (IPC order): robbery or dacoity with attempt to cause death or grievous hurt (s. 311; IPC 397), attempt to commit robbery or dacoity when armed (s. 312; IPC 398), belonging to a gang of robbers (s. 313; IPC 401).

**4. From extortion to dacoity: the chain**
1. **Extortion** (s. 308): fear of injury; the victim delivers; the offender need not be present.
2. **Robbery** (s. 309(3)): the offender is **present**, the fear is of **instant** harm, and delivery is **then and there**.
3. **Dacoity** (s. 310): the robbery is committed by **five or more** persons.
So extortion that amounts to robbery becomes dacoity when committed by five or more persons. *Example:* five armed men enter a shop at night, threaten the owner with instant death unless he hands over the cash, and he does: extortion → robbery → dacoity.

**5. Compared**

| Point | Extortion | Robbery | Dacoity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Property obtained by putting in fear of injury | Theft or extortion plus violence or fear of **instant** violence | Robbery by **five or more** |
| Offender's presence | Not necessary | **Necessary** (for extortion robbery) | Necessary |
| Fear | Of any injury, present or future | **Instant** death, hurt or restraint | Instant |
| Number | One or more | One or more | **Five or more**, including those present and aiding |
| Punishment | Up to 7 years (s. 308(2)) | RI up to 10 years (14 on highway at night) | Life, or RI up to 10 years |
| Murder in the course | – | s. 309(6): hurt: life or up to 10 years | s. 310(3): **every** member: death, life, or at least 10 years |

**6. Problems**
1. A threatens by letter to burn Z's house unless Z pays ₹1 lakh within a week; Z pays. Extortion (s. 308), not robbery: the offender is absent and the fear is not of instant harm.
2. A snatches a necklace from a woman and runs: snatching (s. 304: up to three years). If he pushed her and caused hurt in doing so, robbery with hurt (s. 309(6)).
3. Five persons break into a house at night, threaten the owner with death, and take cash; one kills the owner: dacoity with murder; all are liable under s. 310(3).

**7. Criticism.** The distinctions (presence, instant fear, immediate delivery) are fine, and courts apply them case by case. The BNS adds illustrations, the new offence of snatching, and a longer list of sub-section punishments, but keeps the structure.

**Conclusion.** Extortion is robbery when the offender is present and induces delivery by fear of instant harm; robbery is dacoity when five or more persons commit or attempt it. Each step carries a heavier penalty, and in dacoity with murder every member is liable to death.

## C-21 · Distinguish criminal misappropriation from criminal breach of trust. Explain cheating and cheating by personation

**Asked as:** Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q5 (A-104, "theft v. misappropriation"), Dec 2024 Q3 (A-112, "cheating"), Dec 2025 Q4 (A-113, "cheating by personation"). Not yet set at 20 marks. Related: A-109 to A-113, B-52, B-53.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** misappropriation 3 · criminal breach of trust 5 · distinction table 3 · cheating (definition, essentials, cases) 6 · personation and punishment 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** These offences share a dishonest intention, but differ in how the property comes to the accused: by consent obtained through **deception** (cheating), by **entrustment** (criminal breach of trust), or **innocently** (misappropriation). They are in BNS ss. 314–319 (IPC ss. 403–420) ‡.

**1. Dishonest misappropriation (BNS s. 314; IPC s. 403).** "Whoever **dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use any movable property**" is punished with imprisonment of either description for a term not less than six months, which may extend to two years, and fine (the minimum is the BNS's addition †). *Explanations (IPC):* a dishonest misappropriation for a time only is misappropriation; a finder of property not in the possession of another who takes it for his own use commits misappropriation if he knows the owner or has the means of finding him and does not make reasonable efforts. *Illustration:* A finds a ring lying on the high road and sells it at once without trying to find the owner: misappropriation. The offence also covers a person who gets property by delivery by mistake, or property of a deceased person (s. 315; IPC s. 404) †.

**2. Criminal breach of trust (BNS s. 316; IPC ss. 405–409).** "Whoever, being in any manner **entrusted with property, or with any dominion over property**, **dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use that property**, or **dishonestly uses or disposes of that property in violation of any direction of law** prescribing the mode in which such trust is to be discharged, or of any **legal contract**, express or implied, which he has made touching the discharge of such trust, or **wilfully suffers any other person so to do**, commits criminal breach of trust." ‡
- **Explanations** ‡: an employer who deducts the employee's contribution to a provident fund or family pension fund from his wages is deemed to be entrusted with it; the same for the Employees' State Insurance Fund.
- **Essentials.** (1) **Entrustment**: the accused is entrusted with property or with dominion over it; ownership is not required, but there must be a trust, that is, a fiduciary handing-over (*State of Gujarat v. Jaswantlal Nathalal*, AIR 1968 SC 700; *R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration*, AIR 1962 SC 1821: property includes funds, and a person with dominion over a company's funds is entrusted †). (2) **Dishonest** misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal in violation of law or contract, or wilfully suffering another to do so.
- **Punishment** ‡: s. 316(2): up to **5 years**, or fine, or both; (3) carrier, wharfinger or warehouse-keeper: up to **7 years** and fine; (4) clerk or servant: up to **7 years** and fine; (5) public servant, banker, merchant, factor, broker, attorney or agent in the way of business: **life, or up to ten years**, and fine.
- *Illustrations* ‡: a warehouse-keeper who sells furniture entrusted to him commits CBT; an agent who invests Z's money in his own business instead of company paper commits CBT; A who in good faith buys bank shares thinking it benefits Z commits no CBT (no dishonesty).

**3. Theft, misappropriation and criminal breach of trust distinguished**

| Point | Theft (s. 303) | Misappropriation (s. 314) | CBT (s. 316) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Possession at the start | In **another's** possession; taken without consent | Acquired **innocently** or lawfully (finding, delivery by mistake) | **Entrusted** with possession or dominion |
| Dishonest intention | At the time of moving | Arises **after** possession is obtained | Arises **after** entrustment |
| Moving | Needed | Not needed | Not needed |
| Entrustment | No | No | **Yes** |
| Illustration | A ring lying on a table in Z's house | A ring lying on the high road | A plate left with a warehouse-keeper who sells it |
| Punishment | Up to 3 years | 6 months to 2 years † | Up to 5 years; up to life / 10 years for public servant, banker, etc. |

*Theft takes against consent; misappropriation keeps what came innocently; breach of trust betrays what was entrusted.*

**4. Cheating (BNS s. 318; IPC s. 415)**
*Definition (s. 318(1))* ‡. "Whoever, by **deceiving any person**, **fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived** to **deliver any property to any person**, or to **consent that any person shall retain any property**, or **intentionally induces the person so deceived to do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived**, and which act or omission **causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation or property**, is said to cheat." *Explanation:* a **dishonest concealment of facts** is a deception.

*Essentials.* (1) **Deception** of a person (by words, conduct or dishonest concealment); (2) **fraudulent or dishonest inducement**; (3) the person deceived **delivers property**, consents to its retention, or does or omits to do something he would not otherwise do; (4) the act or omission causes or is likely to cause **damage or harm** in body, mind, reputation or property; (5) the dishonest intention must exist **at the time of the inducement**.

*Punishment (s. 318)* ‡: (2) cheating: up to **3 years**, or fine, or both; (3) cheating a person whose interest the offender is bound by law or contract to protect: up to **5 years**, or fine, or both; (4) cheating and **dishonestly inducing the person to deliver property** or to make, alter or destroy a valuable security: up to **7 years** and fine.

*Illustrations* ‡: A pretends to be in the Civil Service and obtains goods on credit with no intention to pay: cheating. A obtains a loan by assuring repayment with no intention to repay: cheating. A promises to deliver indigo plant with no intention to deliver, to obtain an advance: cheating; if A intended to deliver at the time and later broke the contract, it is only a civil breach.

*Cases* (from memory †). In ***Ram Jas v. State of U.P.*** (1970) 2 SCC 740 and ***Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar*** (2000) 4 SCC 168 the Court held that, to convict of cheating, the prosecution must show a fraudulent or dishonest intention **at the time the promise was made**; failure to keep a promise does not by itself prove that intention. ***S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar*** (2002) 1 SCC 241 distinguished a mere breach of contract from cheating and criminal breach of trust. ***Dr. Vimla v. Delhi Administration*** (AIR 1963 SC 1572): "defraud" involves deceit and injury, actual or likely, to a person or to the public. ***Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar*** (1961): the candidate who applied for the M.A. examination on false documents was guilty of an attempt to cheat.

**5. Cheating by personation (BNS s. 319; IPC ss. 416, 419)** ‡. A person is said to cheat by personation if he cheats by **pretending to be some other person**, or by knowingly **substituting one person for another**, or representing that he or any other person is a person other than he or such other person really is. *Explanation:* the offence is committed whether the person personated is a **real or imaginary** person. *Illustrations:* A cheats by pretending to be a certain rich banker of the same name; A cheats by pretending to be B, a person who is dead. **Punishment:** up to **five years**, or fine, or both ‡.

**6. Cheating compared**

| Point | Cheating | Theft | CBT | Extortion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Owner's consent | Obtained by **deception** | None | Given in entrustment | Obtained by **fear** |
| Intention at the start | At the inducement | At the moving | After entrustment | At the inducement |
| Deception | **Yes** | No | No | No (fear) |

**7. Breach of contract and cheating.** A mere breach of contract is not cheating; the courts regularly quash FIRs that are civil disputes dressed as cheating (*Hridaya Ranjan*; *Palanitkar*), and the BNS has added no safeguard.

**Conclusion.** The three offences are distinguished by the way the property reaches the accused and the time when the dishonest intention arises. In cheating, the intention exists at the inducement; in criminal breach of trust, after a trust; in misappropriation, after an innocent receipt.

## C-22 · Define mischief. Explain criminal trespass, house-trespass, lurking house-trespass and house-breaking and their punishments

**Asked as:** Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q3 (A-114, "mischief"). Not yet set at 20 marks; trespass arises in the Dec 2023 box problem (Q7). Related: A-114 to A-117, B-54.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** mischief (definition, essentials, punishment) 8 · criminal trespass and house-trespass 4 · lurking house-trespass and house-breaking 4 · punishments (s. 331) 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Mischief protects the value and usefulness of property; criminal trespass protects possession and the sanctity of the home. They are in BNS **ss. 324–334** (IPC ss. 425–462) ‡.

**1. Mischief (BNS s. 324(1); IPC s. 425).** "Whoever, with **intent to cause, or knowing that he is likely to cause, wrongful loss or damage to the public or to any person**, **causes the destruction of any property, or any such change in any property or in the situation thereof as destroys or diminishes its value or utility, or affects it injuriously**, commits mischief." ‡
- **Explanation 1:** it is not essential that the offender intend to cause loss or damage to the owner; it is enough that he intends or knows that he is likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to **any person** by injuring any property, whether it belongs to that person or not. **Explanation 2:** mischief may be committed by an act affecting property belonging to the person who commits the act, or to him and others jointly.
- **Illustrations** ‡: burning a valuable security; melting ice in an ice-house; letting cattle into a field to damage the crop.
- **Essentials.** (1) Intent to cause, or knowledge of the likelihood of, **wrongful loss or damage** to the public or any person; (2) **destruction** of property, or a **change** in property or its situation that **destroys or diminishes** its value or utility or affects it injuriously. The property need not be the victim's.
- **Punishment (s. 324)** ‡:

| Sub-section | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Mischief | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Mischief causing loss or damage to any property, including Government or local-authority property | Up to 1 year, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Loss or damage of **₹20,000 or more** | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (5) | Loss or damage of **₹1 lakh or more** | Up to 5 years, or fine, or both |
| (6) | Mischief after **preparation to cause death, hurt or wrongful restraint**, or fear of these | Up to 5 years and fine |

- **Aggravated forms** (ss. 325–328 †; IPC ss. 428–438): killing or maiming animals; injury by fire or explosives to buildings; mischief to railway, aircraft or vessel; running a vessel aground.
- **Mischief compared:** in mischief the offender **destroys or damages**; in theft he **takes**; in trespass he **enters**.

**2. Criminal trespass (BNS s. 329(1); IPC s. 441)** †. Whoever **enters into or upon property in the possession of another** with intent to commit an offence or to **intimidate, insult or annoy** any person in possession, or, having lawfully entered, **unlawfully remains** there with such intent, commits criminal trespass.

**3. House-trespass (s. 329(2); IPC s. 442)** †. Criminal trespass by entering into or remaining in any building, tent or vessel used as a **human dwelling**, or any building used as a place of **worship** or for the **custody of property**.

**4. Lurking house-trespass (s. 330; IPC s. 443)** †. House-trespass **after taking precautions to conceal** it from a person who has a right to exclude or eject the trespasser from the building.

**5. House-breaking (s. 330; IPC s. 445)** †. House-trespass by **one of six modes**: (1) through a passage made by the trespasser or an abettor to enter; (2) through a passage not intended for human entrance, or reached by scaling a wall or building; (3) through a passage opened by the trespasser or an abettor in a way not permitted by the occupier; (4) by opening any lock to enter or leave; (5) by criminal force, assault or threat of assault; (6) through a passage which the trespasser knew had been fastened against entry and which he unfastened. **House-breaking by night** is house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise (IPC s. 446) †.

**6. Punishments (BNS s. 331)** ‡

| Sub-section | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking | Up to **2 years** and fine |
| (2) | Same, after sunset and before sunrise | Up to **3 years** and fine |
| (3) | Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking **to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment** | Up to **3 years** and fine; **10 years** if the offence intended is **theft** |
| (4) | Same, **at night** | Up to **5 years** and fine; **14 years** if theft |
| (5) | **After preparation** for hurt, assault, wrongful restraint, or fear of these | Up to **10 years** and fine |
| (6) | Same, at night | Up to **14 years** and fine |
| (7) | While committing it, **causing grievous hurt** or attempting to cause death or grievous hurt | **Life**, or up to 10 years, and fine |
| (8) | Same, at night; **every person jointly concerned** is liable | **Life**, or up to 10 years, and fine |

Other sections †: house-trespass to commit an offence and after preparation to cause hurt (ss. 332, 333; IPC ss. 449–452); breaking open a receptacle (s. 334; IPC ss. 461–462).

**7. Compared**

| Offence | Entry into | Aggravation |
|---|---|---|
| Criminal trespass | Property in the possession of another | Intent to commit an offence or intimidate, insult or annoy |
| House-trespass | A dwelling, place of worship or place for custody of property | Entry into a building |
| Lurking house-trespass | As above | Precautions to conceal |
| House-breaking | As above | One of six modes of entry; at night is more serious |

**8. Illustrations**
1. A, in a quarrel, throws Z's phone to the ground, breaking it and causing ₹25,000 loss: mischief under s. 324(4) (up to two years).
2. A enters Z's dwelling at night by picking the lock, intending to steal: house-breaking by night to commit theft: s. 331(4) (up to fourteen years).
3. A hides in Z's shop after closing time to steal later: lurking house-trespass (the shop is a place for the custody of property).

**Conclusion.** Mischief punishes damage to property; trespass and house-breaking protect possession and the home. The BNS has raised the monetary thresholds in mischief and kept the graded scheme for house-breaking, with the highest penalties when the entry is at night and the intention is theft or hurt.
