## C-01 · Define crime and discuss its essential elements

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q9 (C, "Define crime and discuss its elements"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2024 Q7 (B-02, "concept and elements of crime"), Dec 2025 Q6 (B-01, "define crime"). Related: A-01 to A-03, B-03.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and definitions 5 · characteristics 3 · human being 1 · *mens rea* 4 · *actus reus* 3 · injury 2 · strict liability and exceptions 1 · crime v. tort 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** *Crime* comes from the Latin *crimen*, a charge or accusation. A crime is a wrong that the State treats as an offence against the community and punishes. Neither the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC) nor the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) defines "crime". They define **"offence"**: a thing made punishable by the Sanhita (BNS s. 2(24); IPC s. 40) ‡. In law, therefore, a wrong becomes a crime when the State **makes it punishable**.

**1. Definitions**

| Jurist | Definition | Stress |
|---|---|---|
| **Blackstone** | An act committed or omitted in violation of a public law forbidding or commanding it | The law is **public** |
| **Stephen** | An act forbidden by law and revolting to the moral sentiments of society | Law **and** morality |
| **Kenny** | A wrong whose sanction is punitive, and is in no way remissible by any private person, but only by the Crown, if at all | The **sanction** and who controls it |
| **Austin** | A wrong pursued by the sovereign or his subordinates | **Who prosecutes** |
| **Glanville Williams** | Whatever the law treats as a crime: a legal wrong that can be followed by criminal proceedings which may end in punishment | **Procedure** and **outcome** |

No single definition is complete: Blackstone omits the mental element and the injury, Stephen makes morality the test although many offences are morally neutral (a traffic offence), and Williams is accurate but circular. The practical test is **the law makes it punishable and the State prosecutes**.

**2. Characteristics of a crime**
1. It is a **harm to the community** as well as to the victim.
2. The harm is **forbidden by law**; there is no crime without a law (Art. 20(1) of the Constitution: no conviction except for an offence under a law in force at the time).
3. A **guilty mind** is normally needed.
4. The **State prosecutes** (the Magistrate takes cognisance on a police report or complaint under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)).
5. The aim of the proceeding is **punishment**, not compensation.
6. The State, not the victim, controls the end of the case, subject to compounding and withdrawal.

**3. Essential elements.** The classical statement is that a crime has four elements: **(1) a human being, (2) a guilty mind (*mens rea*), (3) a guilty act or omission (*actus reus*), and (4) injury to another.**

**(1) A human being.** Only a human being of the age and soundness of mind the law requires can commit a crime. "Person" includes a company or association or body of persons, incorporated or not (BNS s. 2(26); IPC s. 11) ‡. A company can be prosecuted, and for an offence carrying a mandatory prison term the court can impose the fine (*Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement*, 2005) †. Children under seven, children of seven to twelve with immature understanding, and persons of unsound mind are protected by the General Exceptions (BNS ss. 20–22; IPC ss. 82–84).

**(2) *Mens rea*.** The maxim is ***actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea***: an act does not make a person guilty unless his mind is also guilty. In *Fowler v. Padget* (1798) Lord Kenyon said that the intent and the act must both concur to constitute the crime. *Mens rea* is not "evil motive". It is the **mental element in the definition of the offence**: intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraud, rashness or negligence. The Code does not state a general rule. Each definition carries its own words ("intending", "knowing", "dishonestly", "fraudulently", "voluntarily") and the General Exceptions remove criminality where the mind could not be guilty (infancy, insanity, accident, mistake of fact). The Supreme Court holds that *mens rea* is an essential ingredient unless a statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (*Nathulal v. State of M.P.*, 1966) †. Motive differs from intention: motive is the reason, intention the purpose. Motive is not an element of guilt but helps proof and sentence.

**(3) *Actus reus*.** This is the external element: the conduct, its circumstances and its result. It is an **act** or an **illegal omission** (BNS s. 3(4); IPC s. 32 – an illegal omission is treated as an act) ‡. A positive act must be **voluntary**. An omission is punishable only where there is a **legal duty** to act, arising from statute (BNS s. 106(2): the driver must report the accident), contract, relationship (a parent who starves a child: *R v. Gibbins and Proctor*, 1918 †) or creation of danger (*R v. Miller*, 1983 †). The act must be the **cause** of the result; the Code says death is caused even if the victim already had a disease which the injury hastened, or if proper treatment might have saved him (Explanations 1 and 2 to BNS s. 100; IPC s. 299) ‡. The act and the guilty mind must **coincide**: in *Thabo Meli v. R* (1954) the accused attacked a man intending to kill, believed him dead and threw him over a cliff; he died of exposure; the series of acts was one transaction and the accused were guilty of murder †.

**(4) Injury.** "Injury" means any harm whatever **illegally caused** to any person in **body, mind, reputation or property** (BNS s. 2(14); IPC s. 44) ‡. The harm must be illegal: a surgeon's operation in good faith causes bodily harm but no injury. The harm may be to the **community** (waging war, rioting, public nuisance). Attempt, abetment and conspiracy are punished before the injury occurs because they threaten it.

**4. Exceptions to the requirement of *mens rea*.** (i) **Strict-liability** offences created by statute for public welfare (food, drugs, customs); but there is a presumption that *mens rea* is needed (*Sherras v. De Rutzen*, 1895), displaced only by clear words or by the subject matter (*Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v. A-G of Hong Kong*, 1985); (ii) **public nuisance**; (iii) **constructive liability** (BNS ss. 3(5) and 190; see C-05). *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* (1965) (undeclared gold under an exchange-control notification: conviction without proof of knowledge of the law) and *Nathulal* (1966) (stocking grain: *mens rea* was required) show the two sides †.

**5. Crime distinguished from tort**

| Point | Crime | Tort |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong to | The community | A private person |
| Who proceeds | The State | The injured person |
| Aim | Punish and deter | Compensate |
| Mental element | Normally *mens rea* | Not always |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt | Balance of probabilities |
| Compromise | Only compoundable offences (BNSS) | Freely |

One act may be both: assault, defamation (BNS s. 356), death by negligence (BNS s. 106).

**6. Crime is not fixed for all time.** What is punished changes with society. Sedition (IPC s. 124A) and adultery (IPC s. 497) were crimes; the BNS has no sedition offence in that form and no adultery offence ‡. The Supreme Court struck down s. 497 in *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) and read down s. 377 for consenting adults in *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018). New offences have been added: organised crime (s. 111), mob lynching (s. 103(2)), snatching (s. 304) ‡.

**Conclusion.** A crime is a legally prohibited act or omission, done with the mental element the offence requires, that causes or threatens injury, and for which the State prosecutes and punishes. The four elements are present in every ordinary offence; the exceptions (strict liability, constructive liability) are where the law departs from the rule that guilt requires a guilty mind.

## C-02 · Stages of crime: intention, preparation and attempt; liability and punishment at each stage

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q12 (C, "stages of crime and liability/punishment"), Dec 2023 Q12 (C, "intention, preparation, attempt"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2025 Q6 (B-01, "define crime and its stages"). Related: A-16 to A-19, B-06, B-07.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** four stages stated 3 · intention 2 · preparation (rule and exceptions) 4 · attempt (statute, tests, cases) 8 · accomplishment and punishment table 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** A crime that is carried through passes four stages: **intention, preparation, attempt and accomplishment**. The law punishes only the last two (and some special cases of preparation). The difficulty is the line between **preparation** and **attempt**, which the Code does not draw.

**1. Intention.** The first stage is the mental decision to commit the offence. It is **not punishable**, however wicked, because bare thought cannot be proved and has caused no harm (*cogitationis poenam nemo patitur*). Once intention is **expressed**, it may become a different offence: a threat (criminal intimidation), an agreement (conspiracy, BNS s. 61; IPC s. 120A–120B), or an instigation (abetment, BNS s. 45; IPC s. 107).

**2. Preparation.** Preparation is arranging or collecting what is needed to commit the offence: buying a pistol, collecting poison, hiring a getaway vehicle.
- **General rule: not punishable.** The act is usually equivocal; the same act may be done for a lawful purpose, and the offender may change his mind.
- **Exceptions: where the Code expressly punishes it** because the danger is serious: preparation to commit **dacoity** (BNS s. 310(4); IPC s. 399) ‡; collecting arms to wage war against the Government (BNS s. 149 †; IPC s. 122); possessing counterfeit-coin or forgery equipment (IPC ss. 235, 242, 257, 266, BNS equivalents †); making preparation to cause death or hurt in robbery, theft or house-breaking (BNS ss. 307, 331(5)–(6) ‡).
- **Illustration.** Buying a pistol to shoot X is preparation. Loading it and pointing it at X is attempt.

**3. Attempt**

*(a) The provisions.* The Code does not define "attempt". **BNS s. 62 (IPC s. 511)** punishes an attempt to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment (including life) when "in such attempt he does any act towards the commission of the offence" and no express provision is made for the punishment of the attempt. The punishment is imprisonment of any description provided for the offence, up to **one-half of the longest term** (or of life imprisonment) or fine, or both ‡. Some offences have **their own attempt provisions**, which prevail: attempt to murder (BNS s. 109; IPC s. 307), attempt to commit culpable homicide (s. 110; IPC s. 308), attempt to rob (s. 309(5); IPC s. 393) ‡.

*(b) Elements.* (i) **Intention** to commit a specific offence; (ii) an **act towards** it, beyond preparation; (iii) the offence is **not completed** (if it is, the accused is convicted of the offence itself).

*(c) Tests for the line between preparation and attempt.*

| Test | Idea | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Last act** | The offender has done everything he needs to do | Early English rule, associated with cases such as *R v. Eagleton* (1855); too narrow, because it would excuse a person stopped just before the last act † |
| **Proximity** | The act is proximate, not remote | *R v. Eagleton* †: acts remotely leading to the offence are preparation; the last proximate act is an attempt |
| **Equivocality** | The act, on its face, shows the intention | Stephen; discussed in *R v. Robinson* (1915) (facts and result not re-checked) † |
| **"More than merely preparatory"** | Statutory test in England | Criminal Attempts Act 1981, s. 1(1) † |
| **Indian approach** | Attempt begins when the offender does an act **directly connected** with the offence which, but for interruption, would result in it | *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* (1961) |

*(d) Indian cases.*
- **Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar** (AIR 1961 SC 1698). A candidate applied for admission to the M.A. examination with false documents. He was held guilty of **attempting to cheat**: the attempt began when he submitted the application, since that was the act directly connected with the intended deception. Buying the forms and preparing the papers were preparation.
- **Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab** (1991) 4 SCC 341. A person carrying paddy by truck from Punjab towards Delhi was stopped while still inside Punjab, before the point from which it would have left the State in breach of the export-control order. Held: **preparation, not attempt**, because he had not reached the point where the offence would be committed †.
- **State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub** (1980) 3 SCC 57. Silver was loaded in a truck near the coast, intercepted before it could be exported. Held: an **attempt** to export, because the acts were proximate to the offence and, but for the interception, would have resulted in it †.

*(e) Impossible attempt.* An attempt is complete even if the offence could not have been committed because of a fact unknown to the accused. The illustration to s. 511 is that of a person who breaks open a box to steal jewels and finds none: he has attempted theft (the BNS keeps the Illustrations †). English law moved from refusing (*Haughton v. Smith*, 1975) to accepting liability (*R v. Shivpuri*, 1987) †.

*(f) Attempt and the complete offence.* If the offence is completed, the accused is convicted of it, not of attempt. If the evidence proves only an attempt, the court may convict for the attempt (BNSS provision on conviction of an offence not charged) †.

**4. Accomplishment.** The offence is completed. The accused is punished under the section that defines the offence, in full.

**5. Liability and punishment at each stage**

| Stage | Liable? | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | No | None |
| Preparation | Generally no; yes where expressly made an offence (dacoity, waging war, etc.) | As provided in that section |
| Attempt | Yes | s. 62: up to **one-half** of the longest term (life treated as 20 years, BNS s. 6 †) or fine or both; special sections: s. 109 (attempt to murder: up to 10 years + fine; if hurt is caused, life or the same term †), s. 309(5) (attempt to rob: RI up to 7 years ‡) |
| Accomplishment | Yes | The full punishment for the offence |

**6. Attempt, abetment and conspiracy.** These are called **inchoate (incomplete) offences** and are in Unit 2. A person may be guilty of conspiracy or abetment even though no attempt is made.

**7. Criticism.** The Code gives no test for "an act towards the commission". Courts have applied "proximity", "directly connected" and "unequivocal" tests case by case, which makes outcomes hard to predict (compare *Malkiat Singh* with *Mohd. Yakub*). A general attempt provision with a clear test, like the English "more than merely preparatory" test, would help.

**Conclusion.** Of the four stages, the law punishes the last two. Preparation is punished only where the Code says so. Attempt begins where the accused does an act directly connected with the offence which would result in it but for interruption, and it is punished with up to half the maximum term (s. 62) or under a special section.

## C-03 · *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*: discuss *mens rea*, *actus reus* and strict liability in Indian criminal law

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 10 marks: Dec 2022 Q7 (B-04, "mens rea"). Set at 4 marks: Dec 2024 Q2 (A-05, *actus non facit reum*), Dec 2023 Q3 (A-20, strict liability). Related: B-05, B-08, B-09.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** maxim and meaning 3 · *mens rea* in the Code 5 · *actus reus*, omission, causation 4 · concurrence 1 · strict liability (reasons, cases, India) 6 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** ***Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*** — "an act does not make a person guilty unless his mind is also guilty". The maxim comes from the 12th-century *Leges Henrici Primi*, was stated by Coke, and was applied by Lord Kenyon in *Fowler v. Padget* (1798): "the intent and the act must both concur to constitute the crime". A crime therefore has a **physical element** (*actus reus*) and a **mental element** (*mens rea*). Strict-liability offences are the exception.

**1. *Mens rea* in the Code**
1. **Meaning.** *Mens rea* is the **mental element required by the definition of the offence**; it is not "evil motive" or ill will.
2. **No general clause.** The Code's framers did not state it as a general rule. The Code puts the mental element in each **definition**: "intending" (murder, BNS s. 101(a)), "knowing" (culpable homicide, s. 100), "dishonestly" (theft, s. 303), "fraudulently" (forgery), "voluntarily" (hurt, s. 115), "rashly or negligently" (death by negligence, s. 106). The General Exceptions (Chapter III: ss. 14–44) remove liability where the mind cannot be guilty: infancy (ss. 20–21), insanity (s. 22), mistake of fact in good faith (ss. 14 and 17), accident (s. 18), necessity (s. 19) †. They show that the Code's definitions presuppose a guilty mind.

| State of mind | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Intention** | Decision to bring about a result | Murder (s. 101(a)) |
| **Knowledge** | Awareness that a result is likely or certain | Culpable homicide (s. 100) |
| **Dishonesty** | Intention to cause wrongful gain or loss (s. 2(7); IPC s. 24) | Theft |
| **Fraud** | Intent to defraud (s. 2(9); IPC s. 25) | Forgery |
| **Rashness/negligence** | Conscious disregard of a risk / failure to take care | Death by negligence (s. 106) |

3. **Motive and intention.** Motive is the reason for the act; intention is the purpose. Motive is irrelevant to guilt (a mercy killing is not a defence) but relevant to proof and sentence.
4. **Statutory offences.** The courts begin with a **presumption** that *mens rea* is required: *Sherras v. De Rutzen* (1895), *Sweet v. Parsley* (1970), *B v. DPP* (2000) †. In India the Supreme Court has held that *mens rea* is an essential ingredient of a criminal offence unless a statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (*Nathulal v. State of M.P.*, AIR 1966 SC 43) †.

**2. *Actus reus***
1. **Act or omission.** *Actus reus* is the external element: the conduct, its circumstances and its result. The Code treats an **illegal omission** as an act (BNS s. 3(4); IPC s. 32) ‡.
2. **Voluntary act.** A positive act must be voluntary; a reflex or an act in sleep is not enough (*Bratty v. A-G for Northern Ireland*, 1963 †).
3. **Omission.** Liability for an omission needs a **legal duty** to act: statute (BNS s. 106(2): duty to report), contract (*R v. Pittwood*, 1902 †), relationship (*R v. Gibbins and Proctor*, 1918; *R v. Instan*, 1893 †) or creation of danger (*R v. Miller*, 1983 †). The Code's illustration of a jailer who intentionally omits to feed a prisoner and causes death shows that omission can amount to murder.
4. **Causation.** The act must be the cause of the result ("but for" and "operating and substantial cause"). The offender takes the victim as he finds him (*R v. Blaue*, 1975 †). The Code says: death is caused although it would not have occurred but for a pre-existing disease or although timely treatment might have averted it (Explanations 1 and 2 to s. 100) ‡.
5. **Concurrence.** *Actus reus* and *mens rea* must coincide in time. In *Thabo Meli v. R* (1954), the accused attacked the victim intending to kill and, believing him dead, threw the body over a cliff. He died of exposure. The Privy Council treated the series of acts as one transaction †.

**3. Strict liability**
1. **Meaning.** An offence is of strict liability when *mens rea* is not required as to one or more elements of the *actus reus*. The prosecution need not prove intention or knowledge as to that element, and honest mistake is no defence.
2. **Why used.** For statutes protecting the public from serious social danger (adulterated food, import-export control, traffic and licensing laws, pollution): (i) the penalty is small and the stigma slight; (ii) proof of intention would make enforcement impossible; (iii) strict liability encourages the highest standard of care.
3. **English cases**

| Case | Rule |
|---|---|
| *R v. Prince* (1875) | Taking an unmarried girl under 16 out of her father's possession: honest belief that she was over 16 was no defence |
| *R v. Tolson* (1889) | Bigamy: honest and reasonable belief that the husband was dead was a defence |
| *Hobbs v. Winchester Corporation* (1910) | Selling unsound meat: liability without knowledge |
| *Sherras v. De Rutzen* (1895) | Presumption of *mens rea*; rebutted only by clear words or subject matter |
| *Harding v. Price* (1948) | Driver unaware that he had hit another vehicle: knowledge needed for failing to report |
| *Gammon v. A-G of Hong Kong* (1985) | Presumption displaced where the statute deals with an issue of social concern and strict liability promotes its object |
| *Sweet v. Parsley* (1970) | A householder who let rooms where cannabis was smoked: *mens rea* required |

4. **Indian position.** The presumption is that *mens rea* is required but can be displaced by the words or object of the statute. In *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* (AIR 1965 SC 722) the accused flew from Zurich to Manila with gold and did not declare it on landing at Bombay; he was convicted of contravening an exchange-control notification and *mens rea* (knowledge of the notification) was not necessary †. In *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* (AIR 1966 SC 43) a grain dealer was prosecuted for stocking grain in contravention of a licensing order made under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955; the Court held that *mens rea* is an ingredient of that offence and acquitted him because he had acted in good faith †. In *Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab* (1994) the Court read a mental element into the TADA offences †. The Indian approach is therefore: **a presumption of *mens rea*; displaced only by the words of the statute, its subject matter and the social danger**.
5. **Criticism.** Strict liability can punish a morally innocent person, and the justification (deterrence) is weak where the accused could not have prevented the offence. The better view is that it should be limited to regulatory offences with light penalties and to statutes that clearly intend it.
6. **Absolute liability** in the criminal sense (no defence of reasonable care or mistake at all) is rare; do not confuse it with the civil rule in *M.C. Mehta v. Union of India* (1987).

**4. Where the Code itself departs from the rule.** Constructive liability (BNS ss. 3(5), 190: C-05), including the "knew to be likely" limb of s. 190; and voluntary intoxication, where the person is presumed to have the same knowledge as if he were sober (BNS s. 24; IPC s. 86) †.

**Conclusion.** *Mens rea* and *actus reus* must exist together unless the statute excludes the mental element. The Indian Code puts the mental element into each definition and the General Exceptions; the courts presume that statutory offences also need it. Strict liability is the exception, justified by public welfare and confined to what the statute requires.

## C-04 · Punishment: kinds of punishment under the BNS, theories of punishment, the death penalty and life imprisonment

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q1 (A-22, solitary confinement). Related: A-21, A-23 to A-26, B-11, B-12, B-13.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** kinds of punishment (s. 4) 4 · solitary confinement and fine 3 · theories 5 · death penalty 5 · life imprisonment and remission 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Punishment is the pain or loss the State imposes on a person for an offence. Chapter II of the BNS (ss. 4–13) lists the punishments, and the BNSS (the earlier CrPC) deals with sentencing procedure. Theories of punishment explain why the State punishes.

**1. Kinds of punishment (BNS s. 4; IPC s. 53)** ‡
- (a) **Death**;
- (b) **imprisonment for life**;
- (c) **imprisonment**, of two descriptions: **rigorous** (with hard labour) and **simple**;
- (d) **forfeiture of property**;
- (e) **fine**;
- (f) **community service** — *new*; unpaid work of benefit to the community. The BNS provides it for some petty offences: for example, theft of property of value below ₹5,000 by a first offender who returns the property (s. 303(2) proviso) ‡; an attempt to commit suicide to compel a public servant (s. 226); a public servant unlawfully engaging in trade (s. 202) †; defamation (s. 356(2)) †.

**Related sections** (headings ‡): s. 5, commutation of sentence; s. 6, fractions of terms of punishment; s. 7, sentence may be wholly or partly rigorous or simple; s. 8, amount of fine and imprisonment in default; s. 9, limit of punishment for an offence made up of several offences; s. 10, where it is doubtful of which of several offences the accused is guilty; s. 11, solitary confinement; s. 12, limit of solitary confinement; s. 13, enhanced punishment after a previous conviction.

**2. Solitary confinement (s. 11; IPC s. 73) and its limit (s. 12; IPC s. 74)**
- It may be ordered only where the offence carries **rigorous imprisonment**, as part of the sentence, **not exceeding three months in all**: up to **one month** if the sentence is six months or less; up to **two months** if it is more than six months and not more than one year; up to **three months** if it is more than a year ‡.
- It must not exceed **14 days at a time**, with intervals of at least equal length; where the sentence exceeds three months, it must not exceed **seven days in any month**, with similar intervals ‡.
- The Supreme Court has held that solitary confinement can be imposed only by the court within these limits; the jail authorities cannot subject a prisoner to it as a matter of course (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration*, 1978) †.

**3. Theories of punishment**

| Theory | Idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **Retributive** | The wrongdoer deserves punishment; "an eye for an eye" | Vengeful; ignores the offender's future and the harm punishment does |
| **Deterrent** | Punish to deter the offender (special) and others (general) | Little proof it works; the offender may be used as an example |
| **Preventive** | Remove the offender from society (imprisonment, death) | Does not reform him; costly |
| **Reformative** | Reform him through probation, parole, juvenile justice, education | May be too lenient for grave crimes |
| **Expiatory** | The offender atones for the wrong | Moral rather than legal |

The Indian system is a mix. Retribution and deterrence appear in the death penalty and enhanced punishments; reform appears in probation (Probation of Offenders Act, 1958; BNSS), the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, parole and remission, and in community service. The Supreme Court has said that reformation is a legitimate aim even in capital cases (*Bachan Singh*, below).

**4. Death penalty**
- **Offences carrying death under the BNS** include: waging war against the Government (s. 147) ‡; abetment of mutiny actually committed (s. 160 †); giving false evidence to procure a capital conviction, where an innocent person is executed (s. 230 †); murder (s. 103(1): death or life + fine); mob lynching (s. 103(2): death or life or at least seven years + fine) ‡; murder by a life-convict (s. 104: death or imprisonment for life, meaning the remainder of his natural life) ‡; abetment of suicide of a child or insane person (s. 107: death, life or up to ten years) ‡; dacoity with murder (s. 310(3)) ‡; rape causing death or leaving the victim in a persistent vegetative state (s. 66 †); gang rape of a woman under 18 (s. 70(2) †); and repeat offences under the rape provisions (s. 71 †).
- **Constitutionality.** *Jagmohan Singh v. State of U.P.* (1973) upheld the death penalty. ***Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1980) 2 SCC 684, a Constitution Bench by 4:1, upheld its validity but said it should be imposed only in the **"rarest of rare" cases**, where the alternative of life imprisonment is "unquestionably foreclosed", after balancing **aggravating and mitigating** circumstances. The court must give "special reasons" for the death sentence (BNSS s. 393(3), replacing CrPC s. 354(3) †).
- ***Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1983) 3 SCC 470 listed five categories (manner of the murder, motive, anti-social or socially abhorrent nature, magnitude, personality of the victim) as guidelines †.
- ***Mithu v. State of Punjab*** (1983) 2 SCC 277 struck down IPC s. 303 (mandatory death for a murder by a life convict) as violating Arts. 14 and 21. The BNS s. 104 now allows **death or life imprisonment**, so there is no mandatory death sentence ‡.
- **Other safeguards:** the High Court must confirm a death sentence (BNSS s. 407, replacing CrPC s. 366 †); mercy petitions to the Governor and the President (Arts. 72, 161). Delay in disposing of a mercy petition and mental illness may justify commutation (*Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India*, 2014 †).
- **Debate.** For: justice for the worst crimes; deterrence. Against: irreversible error; no proof of greater deterrence; arbitrary application (Bhagwati J., dissenting in *Bachan Singh*; the Law Commission's 262nd Report (2015) recommended abolition for all offences except terrorism-related offences †).

**5. Life imprisonment.** It means imprisonment for the **remainder of the convict's natural life** unless the appropriate Government remits or commutes the sentence (*Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra*, AIR 1961 SC 600 †). Remission does not follow automatically after 14 years (BNSS provisions replacing CrPC ss. 432–433A †). The Supreme Court has held that a court may fix a **fixed term** of imprisonment beyond 14 years, without remission, in place of death (*Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka*, 2008; *Union of India v. V. Sriharan*, Constitution Bench 2016) †. For fractions of a term, life imprisonment is reckoned as 20 years (BNS s. 6 †).

**6. Fine and forfeiture.** The amount is unlimited where no limit is set, but must not be excessive (BNS s. 8 †). The court can order imprisonment in default. A fine alone is the punishment for many petty offences. Forfeiture of property is now rare in the Code and is found mainly in special Acts (for example, the NDPS Act, 1985) †.

**7. Evaluation.** The BNS keeps the IPC's structure, adds community service and abolishes the mandatory death penalty for a life-convict who murders. Critics say that sentencing remains discretionary, that Indian law has no sentencing guidelines or sentencing commission, and that prison overcrowding (a large majority are under-trial) undermines both reform and deterrence.

**Conclusion.** Section 4 lists six punishments. The courts select among them guided by the theories of punishment, with the death penalty confined to the rarest of rare cases and life imprisonment meaning the remainder of life subject to remission.

## C-05 · Constructive criminal liability: common intention (s. 3(5) / s. 34) and common object (s. 190 / s. 149); differences between them

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 (C, "constructive criminal liability; s. 34 and s. 149"), Dec 2023 Q9 (C, "constructive liability – ss. 34 and 149"), Dec 2025 Q13 (C, "constructive criminality"), Dec 2024 Q13(a) (C, "constructive liability"). Related: B-15 to B-18, A-28 to A-34.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning of constructive liability 2 · s. 3(5) / s. 34 (text, essentials, cases) 6 · s. 190 / s. 149 (text, unlawful assembly, essentials, cases) 6 · differences 4 · overlap and other forms 1 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** **Constructive (joint) liability** means that a person is liable for an offence which he did not himself wholly commit, because he joined with others in the enterprise. The act of one is treated as the act of all. In group crime it is often impossible to say who struck the fatal blow; if each member could say "I did not strike that blow", the group would escape. The Code has two main provisions: **common intention** (BNS s. 3(5); IPC s. 34) and **common object** (BNS s. 190; IPC s. 149).

**1. Common intention: BNS s. 3(5) (IPC s. 34)**

*Text.* "When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone." ‡ The words "in furtherance of the common intention of all" were added to the original s. 34 by Act XXVII of 1870, after *Queen v. Gorachand Gope* (1866) †.

*Nature.* Section 34 does **not create a substantive offence**; it lays down a **rule of joint liability** (the offence is the one under the section that defines it, for example murder under s. 103) †.

*Essentials.*
1. A **criminal act** is done (including a series of acts and an illegal omission).
2. It is done by **several persons** (at least two).
3. It is done **in furtherance of the common intention of all**.
4. The accused **participated** in the act. Mere presence at the scene is not enough, but physical presence at the spot is not always required.

*Common intention* means a **prior meeting of minds** and a **pre-arranged plan**. In ***Mahbub Shah v. King-Emperor*** (1945, PC) the Privy Council held that the intention must be shared by all; **similar intention** is not enough. The plan may be formed **on the spot**, shortly before the act. It is usually inferred from conduct before, during and after the act, the arms carried, and the nature of the injuries. If two persons independently shoot at the same victim without any meeting of minds, they have similar, not common, intention, and each is liable only for his own act.

*Participation.* In ***Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor*** (1925, PC) a group went to a post office to rob it and the postmaster was shot dead. The accused had stood outside as a guard and had not fired (from memory †). The Privy Council held him liable for murder under s. 34 and said, in Lord Sumner's words, "they also serve who only stand and wait". In *Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad* (1955) the Court said that common intention must be shown to be shared by the accused, and the accused who played no part can be acquitted even though others are convicted †. In *Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1963) the Court stressed participation in the commission of the offence †.

*Limit.* Each person is liable for the criminal act done **in furtherance of** the intention. If one participant does something the others did not intend and had no reason to expect, the others are not liable for that act. If the plan was only to cause hurt and one kills, the others are liable for hurt unless they shared the intention to kill †.

**2. Common object: BNS s. 190 (IPC s. 149)**

*Text.* "If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence." ‡

*Unlawful assembly (BNS s. 189(1); IPC s. 141).* An assembly of **five or more** persons is unlawful if the common object of the persons composing it is: (1) to overawe by criminal force or show of criminal force the Central or a State Government, Parliament, a State Legislature or a public servant; (2) to resist the execution of any law or legal process; (3) to commit any mischief, criminal trespass or other offence; (4) to take or obtain possession of property, or deprive a person of a right of way, water or other incorporeal right, or to enforce a right or supposed right, by criminal force or show of criminal force; (5) by criminal force or show of criminal force, to compel a person to do what he is not legally bound to do or omit what he is legally entitled to do ‡. An assembly lawful at the start can become unlawful.

*Essentials of s. 190.* (i) an unlawful assembly of five or more; (ii) an offence by a **member**; (iii) in **prosecution of the common object**, or one the members **knew to be likely**; (iv) the accused was a **member at the time**.

*Key principles.*
- **Membership is enough.** No overt act is needed. In ***Masalti v. State of U.P.*** (AIR 1965 SC 202) the Court held that **mere presence in a crowd does not make a person a member**, but a person present who shares the common object is liable even without an overt act. As a rule of prudence the court looks for support from several reliable witnesses, because innocent bystanders may be falsely implicated †.
- **The "knew to be likely" limb.** An offence beyond the common object makes a member liable only if the members **knew** it was likely: *Mizaji v. State of U.P.* (1959) †.
- **Five or more.** The assembly must have five members, but conviction of fewer than five does not defeat s. 190 if others, though not identified or charged, were present: *Dalip Singh v. State of Punjab* (1953); *Bharwad Mepa Dana v. State of Bombay* (1960) †.
- **All members liable as principals** for murder done by one in prosecution of the object (*Lalji v. State of U.P.*, 1989 †).
- A member who joined later or left earlier is liable only for what happened while he was a member.

**3. Difference between common intention and common object**

| Point | Common intention (s. 3(5); IPC s. 34) | Common object (s. 190; IPC s. 149) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | A rule of joint liability; creates no substantive offence | A distinct liability attached to **membership** of an unlawful assembly; conviction is for the offence committed, read with s. 190 (some judgments call it a specific liability or offence) † |
| Number | **Two or more** | **Five or more** |
| What must be shared | A **common intention**: prior meeting of minds | A **common object** of the kind listed in s. 189(1) |
| Participation | **Active participation** needed | **Membership** enough; no overt act needed |
| Offence covered | The act done in furtherance of the intention | Offence committed in prosecution of the object, **or one the members knew to be likely** |
| Unlawful assembly | Not required | Required |
| When formed | Before the act (can be on the spot) | At any stage (can be on the spot) |

**Overlap.** Where the evidence shows both, the court may convict under either. If s. 190 fails because fewer than five are shown to be members, s. 3(5) may be applied if the facts prove a common intention and the accused is not prejudiced (*Willie (William) Slaney v. State of M.P.*, 1956; *Nanak Chand v. State of Punjab*, 1955; *Krishna Govind Patil v. State of Maharashtra*, 1963) †.

**4. Other forms of constructive liability.** Abettor present when the offence is committed (BNS s. 54; IPC s. 114); abettor liable for a probable different act (s. 51; IPC s. 111); dacoity with murder (s. 310(3); IPC s. 396); robbery with hurt (s. 309(6); IPC s. 394); and the new **group offences** of murder and grievous hurt by five or more persons on identity grounds (ss. 103(2), 117(4)) ‡.

**5. Illustration.** A, B and C decide to rob D. A and B enter; C stands outside as a lookout. A kills D. If the plan included violence, all three are liable for murder under s. 3(5). If B and C had agreed only to rob, and did not know that A carried a weapon, B and C are liable only for robbery. Again, if twelve villagers armed with lathis gather to take possession of a field by force and one kills a rival, every member is liable for murder under s. 190; a bystander who does not share the object is not a member.

**6. Criticism.** The rule can convict persons who did not themselves do the killing; the Supreme Court has warned against using s. 190 against bystanders in large crowds. The line between common and similar intention is a matter of inference. The BNS keeps both rules unchanged.

**Conclusion.** Constructive liability is a rule of necessity for group crime. Section 3(5) punishes those who *plan and act together* (two or more); section 190 punishes *members of an unlawful assembly* for what is done in pursuit of their object (five or more). Both are rules of joint liability; the accused is convicted of the offence actually committed, read with the section.
