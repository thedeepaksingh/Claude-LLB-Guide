## B-01 · Define crime. Explain the stages of crime

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q6 ("Define crime and explain its stages"). 20-mark forms: C-01, C-02.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 3 · four stages 5 · conclusion 2

### Answer

A **crime** is a wrong against the community, forbidden by law and punished by the State. The Code defines "offence", not "crime": a thing made punishable by the Sanhita (BNS s. 2(24); IPC s. 40) ‡. Kenny: a wrong whose sanction is punitive and remissible only by the Crown.

**Stages of crime**
1. **Intention**: the mental decision. Not punishable (*cogitationis poenam nemo patitur*).
2. **Preparation**: arranging the means (buying poison). Generally not punishable; punishable where the Code says so (preparation to commit dacoity, BNS s. 310(4); IPC s. 399) ‡.
3. **Attempt**: an act directly towards the offence that goes beyond preparation. Punishable under BNS s. 62 (IPC s. 511), up to one-half of the longest term, or under special sections (attempt to murder, s. 109; IPC s. 307) ‡. *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* (1961): a candidate who applied for an examination on forged documents attempted to cheat.
4. **Accomplishment**: the offence is completed and punished under its own section.

Preparation becomes attempt when the act is proximate to the offence and, but for interruption, would result in it.

**Conclusion.** The law punishes the last two stages and, exceptionally, preparation.

## B-02 · Explain the concept and elements of crime

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q7 ("Concept and elements of crime"). 20-mark form: C-01.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** concept and definition 3 · four elements 6 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Concept.** A crime is a public wrong, made punishable by law and prosecuted by the State. Blackstone: an act committed or omitted in violation of a public law forbidding or commanding it. The Code defines "offence" (BNS s. 2(24); IPC s. 40) ‡.

**Elements**
1. **A human being** capable of being punished: of the age and sanity the law requires. "Person" includes a company (BNS s. 2(26)) ‡; children under seven, and persons of unsound mind, are protected by the General Exceptions (BNS ss. 20–22).
2. **A guilty mind (*mens rea*)**: the mental element in the definition of the offence: intention, knowledge, dishonesty, rashness. *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea* (*Fowler v. Padget*, 1798). Strict-liability offences are the exception.
3. **A guilty act or omission (*actus reus*)**: a voluntary act, or an illegal omission where there is a legal duty (BNS s. 3(4) ‡).
4. **Injury** to another: any harm illegally caused to any person in body, mind, reputation or property (BNS s. 2(14); IPC s. 44) ‡.

The act and the guilty mind must coincide (*Thabo Meli*, 1954 †).

**Conclusion.** All four elements are present in an ordinary offence; attempt, abetment and conspiracy punish conduct before the injury occurs.

## B-03 · Distinguish between crime and tort

**Asked as:** not yet set in Paper IV. 20-mark form: C-01. (Paper III asked tort v. crime at 4 marks.)
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · six points 6 · overlap and conclusion 2

### Answer

A **crime** is a wrong to the community punished by the State; a **tort** is a civil wrong to a private person remedied by damages.

| Point | Crime | Tort |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong to | The community | A private person |
| Who proceeds | The State | The injured person |
| Aim | Punish and deter | Compensate |
| Mental element | *Mens rea* normally needed | Not always |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt | Balance of probabilities |
| Compromise | Only compoundable offences (BNSS) | Freely allowed |
| Law | Codified (BNS) | Mostly uncodified |

**Overlap.** One act may be both: assault, defamation (BNS s. 356), death by negligence (BNS s. 106). The two proceedings are separate; a conviction does not bar a civil claim.

**Conclusion.** Crime looks to the harm to society and punishment; tort looks to the victim's loss and compensation.

## B-04 · Explain *mens rea*. What is its importance and what are the exceptions?

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q7 ("Mens rea"). 20-mark form: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** maxim and meaning 3 · how the Code treats it 3 · exceptions 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

***Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea***: an act does not make a person guilty unless his mind is also guilty. Lord Kenyon in *Fowler v. Padget* (1798): the intent and the act must both concur.

**Meaning.** *Mens rea* is not evil motive. It is the **mental element required by the definition of the offence**: intention, knowledge, dishonesty (BNS s. 2(7)), fraud (s. 2(9)), rashness or negligence ‡.

**In the Code.** There is no general clause. Each definition carries its own words ("intending", "knowing", "voluntarily"), and the General Exceptions (BNS ss. 14–44) remove liability where the mind cannot be guilty: infancy, insanity, mistake of fact, accident. The Supreme Court holds that *mens rea* is an essential ingredient unless a statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication (*Nathulal v. State of M.P.*, 1966 †).

**Exceptions.** (i) **Strict-liability** statutory offences for public welfare (*State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George*, 1965 †); (ii) **public nuisance**; (iii) **constructive liability** (BNS ss. 3(5), 190).

**Conclusion.** Guilt needs a guilty mind unless the statute clearly says otherwise.

## B-05 · Explain *actus reus*. How does the law treat omissions and causation?

**Asked as:** not yet set. 20-mark form: C-03. Related: A-06, A-07.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · act and omission 4 · causation 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

***Actus reus*** is the external element of a crime: the conduct, its circumstances and its result which the law prohibits.

**Act.** A **voluntary** act: a reflex or an act in sleep is not enough (*Bratty*, 1963 †).

**Omission.** The Code treats an **illegal omission** as an act (BNS s. 3(4); IPC s. 32) ‡. Liability for failing to act needs a **legal duty**: from **statute** (Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, s. 134: the driver must help the injured and report the accident †; BNS s. 106(2) adds a reporting duty, not yet in force as far as I found); **contract** (*R v. Pittwood*, 1902); **relationship** (*R v. Gibbins and Proctor*, 1918); or **creating a danger** (*R v. Miller*, 1983) †. A jailer who intentionally starves a prisoner to death commits murder.

**Causation.** The act must be a cause of the result. The accused takes the victim as he finds him (*R v. Blaue*, 1975 †). The Code provides: death is caused though the victim had a disease which the injury hastened, or though proper treatment might have prevented it (Explanations 1 and 2 to BNS s. 100; IPC s. 299) ‡.

**Conclusion.** The act and the guilty mind must coincide (*Thabo Meli*, 1954 †).

## B-06 · Explain intention, preparation and attempt

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 20 marks: Dec 2023 Q12 (C-02).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** intention 2 · preparation 3 · attempt 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Intention.** The decision to commit the offence. **Not punishable**: thought alone has caused no harm and cannot be proved. Once expressed, it may become a threat, conspiracy (BNS s. 61) or abetment (s. 45) ‡.

**Preparation.** Arranging or collecting the means: buying a pistol or poison. **Generally not punishable**, because the act is equivocal. **Punishable where the Code says so**: preparation to commit dacoity (BNS s. 310(4); IPC s. 399) ‡; collecting arms to wage war (BNS s. 149 †; IPC s. 122).

**Attempt.** An act directly towards the commission that goes beyond preparation. The Code does not define it. **BNS s. 62 (IPC s. 511)** punishes it with up to **one-half** of the longest term (or of life imprisonment) or fine, or both ‡; special sections prevail (attempt to murder, s. 109; attempt to rob, s. 309(5)) ‡. Attempt is complete even if the offence was impossible: the empty box (illustration to s. 511 †).

*Illustration.* Buying a pistol to shoot X is preparation; loading it and pointing it at X is attempt.

**Conclusion.** Intention is free; preparation is generally free; attempt and accomplishment are punished.

## B-07 · How is preparation distinguished from attempt? Refer to decided cases

**Asked as:** not yet set. 20-mark form: C-02. Related: A-17, A-18, A-19.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** the problem 1 · English tests 3 · Indian approach and cases 5 · conclusion 1

### Answer

The Code does not draw the line between preparation and attempt; the courts do.

**Tests**
- **Last act**: the offender has done all he can; too narrow, since it excuses a person stopped just before the last act †.
- **Proximity**: the act must be proximate, not remote (*R v. Eagleton*, 1855) †.
- **Equivocality**: the act must on its face show the intention †.
- **English statute**: an act "more than merely preparatory" (Criminal Attempts Act 1981, s. 1(1)) †.

**Indian approach.** Attempt begins when the offender does an act **directly connected** with the offence which, but for interruption, would result in it.
- *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* (AIR 1961 SC 1698): the candidate who applied for the M.A. examination on forged papers attempted to cheat.
- *State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub* (1980): silver loaded in a truck near the coast, intercepted before export: attempt to export †.
- *Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab* (1991): paddy carried by truck, stopped before leaving the State: preparation, not attempt †.

**Conclusion.** The test is proximity of the act to the offence.

## B-08 · Explain strict liability in criminal law

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2023 Q3 (A-20). 20-mark form: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning 2 · reasons 2 · cases 4 · Indian position and criticism 2

### Answer

An offence of **strict liability** is one in which *mens rea* is not required as to one or more elements of the *actus reus*: honest mistake is no defence.

**Why used.** Statutes protecting public welfare (food, drugs, customs, licensing): the penalty is light, proof of intention would make enforcement impossible, and strictness encourages care.

**Cases** (English)
- *R v. Prince* (1875): taking a girl under 16 from her father: belief that she was older was no defence.
- *Hobbs v. Winchester Corporation* (1910): selling unsound meat.
- *Sherras v. De Rutzen* (1895): presumption that *mens rea* is required, rebutted by clear words or subject matter.
- *Gammon v. A-G of Hong Kong* (1985): displaced where the statute deals with a social concern †.

**India.** *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* (1965): *mens rea* not needed for contravening an exchange-control notification; *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* (1966): *mens rea* required for stocking grain †.

**Criticism.** It can punish the morally innocent; it should be confined to regulatory offences with light penalties.

## B-09 · Distinguish between motive and intention

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 4 marks: A-08. Related: C-03.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meanings 3 · differences 5 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Intention** is the purpose with which an act is done: what the actor means to bring about. **Motive** is the reason that moves the actor to act: why.

| Point | Intention | Motive |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The immediate purpose | The underlying reason |
| Element of the offence | Yes, where the definition requires it | No |
| Relevance to guilt | Essential | Irrelevant to guilt |
| Relevance to proof | Direct | May show identity or intention |
| Relevance to sentence | Yes | Yes (good motive may mitigate) |

*Examples.* A shoots B intending to kill; the motive may be revenge or greed. A good motive is no defence: a mercy-killing is still culpable homicide unless an Exception applies (BNS s. 101, Exception 5) ‡.

Absence of motive does not prevent conviction where the evidence is strong, though it can weaken a case based on circumstantial evidence.

**Conclusion.** The law punishes the intention and, in the absence of a direct proof, uses motive to prove it.

## B-10 · Explain "good faith" as defined in the Code

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q8 ("Good faith"). Set at 4 marks: Dec 2023 Q5 (A-12).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** definition 3 · explanation 3 · where used 2 · comparison 2

### Answer

**Definition.** BNS s. 2(11) (IPC s. 52): "Nothing is said to be done or believed in good faith which is done or believed **without due care and attention**." ‡

**Meaning.** Honesty is not enough: the belief or act must be the result of **due care and attention**. An honest but careless belief is not good faith. The test is objective in this sense. In *Harbhajan Singh v. State of Punjab* (AIR 1966 SC 97), on defamation, the Court said that good faith requires care and caution and reasonable inquiry †.

**Comparison.** The General Clauses Act, 1897, s. 3(22) treats a thing as done in good faith if done honestly, whether negligently or not †. The Penal Code is stricter.

**Where used.** Mistake of fact (BNS ss. 14, 17), necessity (s. 19), consent for the person's benefit (ss. 26, 27), excess of private defence (s. 101, Exception 2), public servant's acts (Exception 3), defamation Exceptions (s. 356) ‡.

**Illustration.** A, believing without inquiry that Z is a thief, seizes him: no good faith.

**Conclusion.** Good faith in the Code means honesty with reasonable care.

## B-11 · What are the kinds of punishment under the BNS? Explain community service

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q1 (A-22, solitary confinement). 20-mark form: C-04.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** six kinds 4 · solitary confinement 2 · community service 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**BNS s. 4 (IPC s. 53)** lists six punishments ‡:
1. **Death**;
2. **imprisonment for life**;
3. **imprisonment**, rigorous (with hard labour) or simple;
4. **forfeiture of property**;
5. **fine**;
6. **community service** (new).

**Solitary confinement (s. 11; IPC s. 73).** For an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment, up to **three months** in all: one month if the sentence is six months or less; two months if over six months and not over a year; three months if over a year. Not more than **14 days at a time**, with equal intervals (s. 12) ‡.

**Community service.** Unpaid work for the benefit of the community, ordered by the court as punishment for some petty offences: theft of property worth under ₹5,000 by a first offender who returns the property (s. 303(2) proviso) ‡; attempt to commit suicide to compel a public servant (s. 226); defamation (s. 356(2)) †. It reflects the reformative theory and reduces prison overcrowding. How it is to be implemented is left to rules (not checked).

**Conclusion.** The BNS keeps the IPC's five punishments and adds community service.

## B-12 · Discuss the death penalty and the doctrine of "rarest of rare"

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-04.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** BNS provisions 2 · *Bachan Singh* 3 · other cases 3 · debate 2

### Answer

**Provisions.** Death is a punishment under BNS s. 4(a). It is awarded for murder (s. 103(1): death or life), mob lynching (s. 103(2)), murder by a life-convict (s. 104: death or life, no longer mandatory), dacoity with murder (s. 310(3)), waging war (s. 147) ‡.

**Cases** †
- ***Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1980) 2 SCC 684 (Constitution Bench, 4:1): the death penalty is valid, but only in the **rarest of rare** cases, where life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed; aggravating and mitigating circumstances are weighed; "special reasons" must be recorded.
- ***Machhi Singh*** (1983): five categories: manner, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude, victim.
- ***Mithu v. State of Punjab*** (1983): mandatory death under IPC s. 303 struck down.
- ***Shatrughan Chauhan*** (2014): undue delay in deciding a mercy petition, and mental illness, may justify commutation.

**Safeguards.** Confirmation by the High Court (BNSS s. 407); mercy petitions (Arts. 72, 161).

**Debate.** The Law Commission's 262nd Report (2015) recommended abolition except for terrorism offences. Critics cite irreversible error and arbitrariness.

## B-13 · Explain the theories of punishment

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-04. Related: A-26.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** five theories 6 · Indian position 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

| Theory | Idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **Retributive** | The wrongdoer deserves punishment | Vengeful; ignores the future |
| **Deterrent** | Punish to deter the offender (special) and others (general) | Little proof it works |
| **Preventive** | Remove the offender from society | Does not reform; costly |
| **Reformative** | Reform through probation, parole, education | May be too lenient |
| **Expiatory** | The offender atones | Moral, not legal |

**Indian position.** A mix. Retribution and deterrence appear in the death penalty and enhanced punishments; reform in probation (Probation of Offenders Act, 1958), the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, parole, remission, and now community service (BNS s. 4(f)) ‡. The Supreme Court accepts reformation as a legitimate aim even in capital cases (*Bachan Singh*, 1980 †).

**Conclusion.** No single theory explains the Code; sentencing balances them according to the offence and the offender.

## B-14 · State the main changes made by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 in the Indian Penal Code, 1860

**Asked as:** not yet set. Both codes appear in the papers: IPC (Dec 2022, 2023), BNS (Dec 2024, 2025). Related: A-27.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** structure 2 · new offences 4 · omissions and replacements 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Structure.** The BNS (assent 25 December 2023; in force **1 July 2024**) has **358 sections in 20 chapters**; the IPC had 511 in 23 ‡. It repeals the IPC (s. 358). Offences before 1 July 2024 are tried under the IPC.

**New offences** ‡: organised crime (s. 111); petty organised crime (s. 112); terrorist act (s. 113); mob lynching (s. 103(2)); snatching (s. 304); hit-and-run (s. 106(2), not in force as far as I found); sexual intercourse by deceitful means including false promise of marriage (s. 69); hiring a child to commit an offence (s. 95).

**New punishment.** Community service (s. 4(f)) ‡.

**Omitted or replaced.** Sedition (IPC s. 124A) is replaced by s. 152, acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. Adultery (IPC s. 497, struck down) and IPC s. 377 are not carried over ‡. Murder by a life-convict no longer carries mandatory death (s. 104).

**Other changes.** Higher fines; "child" means a person under 18 (s. 2(3)); a five-year minimum for culpable homicide with intention (s. 105) ‡; the Standing Committee's recommendations were partly adopted.

**Conclusion.** The structure and core definitions are the IPC's, so IPC case law remains persuasive.

## B-15 · Explain common intention (BNS s. 3(5); IPC s. 34)

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-05 (Dec 2022 Q11, Dec 2023 Q9, Dec 2025 Q13, Dec 2024 Q13(a)).
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text 2 · essentials 3 · meaning of common intention 3 · cases 2

### Answer

**Text.** BNS s. 3(5): "When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone." ‡ The words "in furtherance of the common intention of all" were added in 1870 †.

**Nature.** It creates **no substantive offence**; it states a rule of joint liability †.

**Essentials.** (1) A criminal act; (2) by **several persons** (two or more); (3) in furtherance of the **common intention of all**; (4) the accused **participated**.

**Common intention** means a **prior meeting of minds** and a pre-arranged plan; it may arise on the spot. *Mahbub Shah v. King-Emperor* (1945): the intention must be shared; **similar** intention is not enough. It is inferred from conduct before, during and after the act.

**Participation.** *Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor* (1925): a person who stood guard outside while the postmaster was shot was liable: "they also serve who only stand and wait" †.

**Limit.** Liability extends only to acts in furtherance of the plan.

## B-16 · Explain common object (BNS s. 190; IPC s. 149)

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-05.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** text 2 · unlawful assembly 3 · essentials 3 · cases 2

### Answer

**Text.** BNS s. 190: if an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who at the time is a member of the same assembly is guilty of that offence ‡.

**Unlawful assembly (s. 189(1); IPC s. 141).** Five or more persons whose common object is: to overawe the Government or a public servant by criminal force; to resist the execution of law or legal process; to commit mischief, criminal trespass or other offence; to take possession of property or enforce a right by criminal force; to compel a person to do what he is not bound to do ‡.

**Essentials.** (1) Unlawful assembly of five or more; (2) offence by a **member**; (3) in prosecution of the **common object** or known to be **likely**; (4) accused a **member at the time**.

**Principles.** Membership suffices; no overt act is needed. Mere presence in a crowd does not make one a member; the court looks for reliable witnesses (*Masalti v. State of U.P.*, 1965 †). The "likely" limb needs proof of knowledge (*Mizaji*, 1959 †).

*Word count: 200*

## B-17 · Distinguish between section 34 and section 149 of the IPC (BNS ss. 3(5) and 190)

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q11 and Dec 2023 Q9 were set at 20 marks (C-05). Not yet set at 10 marks.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** table of differences 7 · overlap 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

| Point | Common intention (BNS s. 3(5); IPC s. 34) | Common object (BNS s. 190; IPC s. 149) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Rule of joint liability; creates no offence | Distinct liability attached to membership of an unlawful assembly |
| Number | Two or more | Five or more |
| What is shared | Common intention: prior meeting of minds | Common object: one of the five in s. 189(1) |
| Participation | Active participation needed | Membership enough; no overt act |
| Offence covered | Act in furtherance of the intention | Offence in prosecution of the object, **or known to be likely** |
| Unlawful assembly | Not required | Required |
| Formed | Before the act (may be on the spot) | At any stage |

**Overlap.** Where the evidence shows both, the court may convict under either. If s. 190 fails because fewer than five are proved to be members, s. 3(5) may be applied if a common intention is proved and there is no prejudice (*Willie (William) Slaney v. State of M.P.*, 1956; *Nanak Chand*, 1955 †).

**Conclusion.** Section 3(5) is about *planning and acting together*; section 190 about *membership of an unlawful assembly*.

## B-18 · What is constructive liability? Explain its forms under the Code

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 20 marks: C-05.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** meaning and reason 3 · forms 5 · criticism 2

### Answer

**Meaning.** **Constructive (joint) liability** makes a person liable for an offence he did not himself wholly commit, because he joined with others. Reason: in group crime it is often impossible to say who struck the blow.

**Forms in the Code** (BNS ‡ unless †)
1. **Common intention** (s. 3(5); IPC s. 34).
2. **Common object** of an unlawful assembly (s. 190; IPC s. 149).
3. **Abettor present** when the offence is committed (s. 54; IPC s. 114) †; abettor liable for a probable different act (s. 51; IPC s. 111) †.
4. **Dacoity with murder**: every dacoit liable (s. 310(3); IPC s. 396).
5. **Robbery with hurt**: every person jointly concerned (s. 309(6); IPC s. 394).
6. **Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking at night with grievous hurt or attempt at death**: every person jointly concerned (s. 331(8); IPC s. 460).
7. **New group offences**: murder (s. 103(2)) and grievous hurt (s. 117(4)) by five or more persons on identity grounds.

**Vicarious liability** makes one person liable for another's wrong (employer for employee) and is statutory in criminal law; constructive liability attributes the group's act to each member.

**Criticism.** It can convict those who did not themselves kill.

*Word count: 192*
