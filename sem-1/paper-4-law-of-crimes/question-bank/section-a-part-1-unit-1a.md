<a id="a-01"></a>
**A-01 · Crime** · *Related: B-01, B-02, C-01*

A crime is a wrong against the community, forbidden by law and punished by the State, which prosecutes the offender. Kenny: a wrong whose sanction is punitive and remissible only by the Crown. The Code defines "offence", not "crime" (BNS s. 2(24); IPC s. 40) ‡. Its elements are a human being, a guilty mind, a guilty act or omission, and injury to another.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-02"></a>
**A-02 · Offence (BNS s. 2(24); IPC s. 40)** · *Related: B-02, C-01*

"Offence" means a thing made punishable by the Sanhita ‡. In some provisions it also covers a thing punishable under a special or local law †. It is the Code's word for a crime. Offences are classified by gravity and procedure (cognizable or non-cognizable, bailable or non-bailable) in the BNSS.

*Word count: 48*

<a id="a-03"></a>
**A-03 · Elements of crime** · *Related: B-02, C-01*

Four elements: (1) a human being capable of being punished; (2) a guilty mind (*mens rea*); (3) a guilty act or illegal omission (*actus reus*); (4) injury to another, that is, harm to body, mind, reputation or property (BNS s. 2(14)) ‡. Strict-liability offences dispense with the second element.

*Word count: 48*

<a id="a-04"></a>
**A-04 · *Mens rea*** · *Related: B-04, C-03*

The guilty mind required by the definition of an offence: intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraud, rashness or negligence. It comes from the maxim *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. The Code has no general clause; each definition states its own mental element. It is excluded only by express words or necessary implication (*Nathulal v. State of M.P.*, 1966 †).

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-05"></a>
**A-05 · *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q2* · *Related: B-04, C-03*

An act does not make a man guilty unless his mind is also guilty: a crime needs a guilty act and a guilty mind together (*Fowler v. Padget*, 1798). The Code gives effect to the maxim through words such as "intending", "knowing" and "dishonestly" in each definition, and through the General Exceptions (infancy, insanity, mistake). Strict-liability offences are the exception.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-06"></a>
**A-06 · *Actus reus*** · *Related: B-05, C-03*

The external element of a crime: the conduct, its circumstances and the result which the law forbids. It is a voluntary act, or an illegal omission where there is a legal duty (BNS s. 3(4)) ‡. Reflex or involuntary movements are not enough. The act must cause the result and must coincide with the guilty mind.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-07"></a>
**A-07 · Omission** · *Related: B-05, C-03*

An omission is a failure to act. The Code treats an illegal omission like an act (BNS s. 3(4); IPC s. 32) ‡. It is punishable only where there is a legal duty arising from statute, contract, relationship, or the creation of danger (*R v. Pittwood*, 1902; *R v. Miller*, 1983 †). A jailer who starves a prisoner to death commits murder by omission.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-08"></a>
**A-08 · Motive and intention** · *Related: B-09, C-03*

Intention is the purpose with which an act is done; motive is the reason that moves a person to act. Intention is an element of the offence; motive is not. A good motive is no defence and a bad one adds nothing to guilt. Motive helps to prove intention, particularly where the case rests on circumstantial evidence.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-09"></a>
**A-09 · Dishonestly** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q5* · *Related: B-48*

BNS s. 2(7) (IPC s. 24): doing anything with the intention of causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another person ‡. Either is enough. Dishonesty is judged at the time of the act. It is the mental element of theft, extortion, misappropriation and breach of trust. A good-faith claim of right negatives it.

*Word count: 56*

<a id="a-10"></a>
**A-10 · Fraudulently** · *Related: B-53*

BNS s. 2(9) (IPC s. 25): a person does a thing fraudulently if he does it with intent to defraud, but not otherwise ‡. To defraud is to deceive so as to injure, or put at risk, another's interest, whether or not there is wrongful gain or loss (*Dr. Vimla v. Delhi Administration*, 1963 †). It is the mental element in cheating and forgery.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-11"></a>
**A-11 · Wrongful loss (and wrongful gain)** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q4* · *Related: B-48*

BNS s. 2(37) (IPC s. 23): wrongful loss is loss by unlawful means of property to which the person losing it is legally entitled. Wrongful gain (s. 2(36)) is gain by unlawful means of property to which the person gaining is not legally entitled. Both include wrongfully retaining, or being kept out of, property. Together they define "dishonestly".

*Word count: 58*

<a id="a-12"></a>
**A-12 · Good faith** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q5* · *Related: B-10*

BNS s. 2(11) (IPC s. 52): nothing is said to be done or believed in good faith which is done or believed without due care and attention ‡. Honesty alone is not enough; care and inquiry are needed (*Harbhajan Singh v. State of Punjab*, 1966 †). The General Clauses Act, 1897 uses a milder test: honesty, even if negligent †.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-13"></a>
**A-13 · Injury** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q2* · *Related: B-02*

BNS s. 2(14) (IPC s. 44): "injury" is any harm whatever illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation or property ‡. The harm must be "illegal", that is, an offence or a ground for a civil action. "Injury" is wider than "hurt", which is bodily pain, disease or infirmity (BNS s. 114).

*Word count: 53*

<a id="a-14"></a>
**A-14 · Movable property** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q1* · *Related: B-48*

BNS s. 2(21) (IPC s. 22): movable property includes corporeal property of every description, except land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth ‡. A thing severed from the earth becomes movable. Only movable property can be stolen: land cannot, but a severed tree can.

*Word count: 52*

<a id="a-15"></a>
**A-15 · Voluntarily** · *Related: B-40*

BNS s. 2(33) (IPC s. 39): a person causes an effect voluntarily when he causes it by means whereby he intended to cause it, or by means which, at the time of employing them, he knew or had reason to believe to be likely to cause it. Example: a man who sets fire to an inhabited house at night to facilitate a robbery, knowing death is likely, causes death voluntarily.

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-16"></a>
**A-16 · Stages of crime** · *Related: B-01, B-06, C-02*

Four stages: (1) intention, not punishable; (2) preparation, not punishable except where the Code says so (preparation to commit dacoity, BNS s. 310(4)) ‡; (3) attempt, punishable (BNS s. 62; IPC s. 511); (4) accomplishment, punished under the section defining the offence. The law punishes conduct from attempt onwards.

*Word count: 48*

<a id="a-17"></a>
**A-17 · Preparation** · *Related: B-06, B-07, C-02*

Arranging or collecting the means for an offence, such as buying poison. It is generally not punishable, because the act is equivocal and the offender may desist. It is punishable where the Code so provides: preparation to commit dacoity (BNS s. 310(4); IPC s. 399) ‡ and collecting arms to wage war (s. 149; IPC s. 122) †. It ends where attempt begins.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-18"></a>
**A-18 · Attempt (BNS s. 62; IPC s. 511)** · *Related: B-06, B-24, C-02*

An act done towards committing an offence that goes beyond preparation but falls short of completion. Section 62 punishes it, where no express provision exists, with up to one-half of the longest term, or of life imprisonment, or with the fine provided, or both ‡. Example: loading a pistol and aiming it at X (*Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar*, 1961).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-19"></a>
**A-19 · Impossible attempt** · *Related: B-07, B-24, C-02*

An attempt is complete even though the offence could not have been committed because the means or the object was not what the offender believed. The illustrations to IPC s. 511 treat putting a hand into an empty pocket, or breaking open a box with no jewels, as an attempt to steal †. English law reached the same view in *R v. Shivpuri* (1986) †.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-20"></a>
**A-20 · Strict liability** · *Asked: Dec 2023 Q3* · *Related: B-08, C-03*

A strict-liability offence needs no *mens rea* as to one or more elements of the offence; honest mistake is no defence. It is used in public-welfare statutes on food, drugs, customs and licensing (*Hobbs v. Winchester Corporation*, 1910; *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George*, 1965 †). The presumption is that *mens rea* is required unless the statute clearly excludes it.

*Word count: 59*

<a id="a-21"></a>
**A-21 · Kinds of punishment (BNS s. 4; IPC s. 53)** · *Related: B-11, C-04*

BNS s. 4 lists six punishments ‡: (a) death; (b) imprisonment for life; (c) imprisonment, rigorous (with hard labour) or simple; (d) forfeiture of property; (e) fine; (f) community service. Community service is new; the other five were in IPC s. 53.

*Word count: 41*

<a id="a-22"></a>
**A-22 · Solitary confinement** · *Asked: Dec 2025 Q1* · *Related: B-11, C-04*

BNS s. 11 (IPC s. 73): for an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment, the court may order solitary confinement up to three months in all: one month if the term is six months or less, two months if over six months and not over a year, three months if over a year. Not more than 14 days at a time, with intervals (s. 12) ‡.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-23"></a>
**A-23 · Community service** · *Related: B-11, C-04*

A new punishment (BNS s. 4(f)): unpaid work for the benefit of the community, ordered by the court; the convict receives no remuneration †. It is prescribed for petty offences: theft of property worth under ₹5,000 by a first offender who returns it (s. 303(2) proviso) ‡, attempted suicide to compel a public servant (s. 226) † and defamation (s. 356(2)) †. It supports reform and eases prison overcrowding.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-24"></a>
**A-24 · Life imprisonment** · *Related: B-12, C-04*

Imprisonment for life means imprisonment for the remainder of the convict's natural life, subject to remission by the appropriate Government (*Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra*, 1961 †). Only for calculating fractions of terms is it reckoned as twenty years (BNS s. 6; IPC s. 57) †. A court may fix a term without remission (*Swamy Shraddananda*, 2008 †).

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-25"></a>
**A-25 · Rarest of rare** · *Related: B-12, C-04*

*Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980, Constitution Bench) upheld the death penalty but only in the rarest of rare cases, where life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed. The court weighs aggravating and mitigating circumstances and must record special reasons (BNSS s. 393(3) †). *Machhi Singh* (1983) listed categories: manner, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude and victim †.

*Word count: 54*

<a id="a-26"></a>
**A-26 · Theories of punishment** · *Related: B-13, C-04*

Retributive (the wrongdoer deserves punishment); deterrent (to deter him and others); preventive (to disable him from repeating the offence); reformative (to reform him through probation and education); expiatory (atonement). Indian law mixes them: the death penalty is deterrent and retributive; probation, parole and community service are reformative.

*Word count: 47*

<a id="a-27"></a>
**A-27 · BNS and IPC: main changes** · *Related: B-14*

The BNS (358 sections, 20 chapters; in force 1 July 2024) replaces the IPC ‡. New: organised crime, terrorist act, mob lynching, snatching, hit-and-run (not in force as far as I found), sexual intercourse by deceitful means, community service. Sedition is replaced by s. 152; adultery is omitted. The structure is largely the same, so IPC case law remains persuasive.

*Word count: 59*
