# Paper IV · Cases and Statutes Digest (facts · issue · holding · principle · what happened next)

> **Last updated:** 2026-10-29 (IST) · **Law stated as on:** 2026-10-29 · **Branch:** `paper/04-law-of-crimes`
> **What this is.** Part 1 lists the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) provisions, with the Indian Penal Code (IPC) equivalent, and the other statutes that the model answers use, with a note of how sure I am of each. Part 2 is a digest of the cases: **material facts**, **issue**, **holding**, **principle** to quote, **subsequent developments** where I know them, and the answers that use the case. Part 3 gives the best cases for each topic.
> **How certain is each entry?** Three levels.
> - **‡ checked**: confirmed this session against a source on the web (the URLs were not kept; see the [source-verification log](source-verification-log.md)).
> - **†**: written from memory and I am less sure of it. Check the report or a textbook before you rely on it. In the exam, write only what you are sure of.
> - **Unmarked**: standard textbook knowledge. The name, year and holding are reliable, but I did not re-check the detail.
> **The case facts are in outline.** Where I could not recall the facts of a Supreme Court case with confidence I say "the facts are not reproduced here" instead of guessing; the holding is what matters for the examination. I did not check any of the cases against the law reports.
> **Key to "used in".** The list is built by searching the answers for the section or case name, so it finds every answer that uses it, but for a section number it may also list an answer that cites an IPC section with the same number. `A-nn` = [Section A](README.md#files) (seven parts) · `B-nn` = Section B · `C-nn` = Section C. ## 2A · Unit 1A · Concept of crime, mens rea, strict liability, stages of crime, punishment

### 1. *Fowler v. Padget* (1798) 7 TR 509 · King's Bench
- **Facts.** A question whether a trader had committed an act of bankruptcy by keeping to his house to avoid his creditors; he had no intention of defeating them †.
- **Issue.** Is an act enough to make a person liable if he had no guilty intention?
- **Held.** No. Lord Kenyon said that the intent and the act must both concur to constitute the crime.
- **Principle.** *Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*: a crime has a physical element and a mental element.
- **After.** The English courts and the Indian Supreme Court start from a presumption that *mens rea* is required (*Sherras*; *Sweet v. Parsley*; *Nathulal*). The Indian Penal Code does not state the maxim; it puts the mental element in each definition ("intentionally", "knowingly", "dishonestly", "fraudulently"), and the BNS does the same.
- **Used in:** A-05, B-02, B-04, C-01, C-03.

### 2. *Sherras v. De Rutzen* [1895] 1 QB 918 · Queen's Bench Division
- **Facts.** A licensed victualler served liquor to a police constable who was on duty but had taken off his armlet. The Licensing Act forbade supplying liquor to a constable on duty. The publican honestly believed that the constable was off duty.
- **Issue.** Is knowledge of the constable's status an element of the offence?
- **Held.** Yes. The conviction was quashed. Wright J. said that there is a presumption that *mens rea* is an ingredient of every offence, displaced only by clear words or by the subject matter of the statute (public nuisances, revenue and similar matters).
- **Principle.** The presumption of *mens rea*, and the narrow classes in which it is displaced.
- **After.** Followed in *Sweet v. Parsley* (1970) and *Gammon* (1985); in India in *Nathulal* (1966).
- **Used in:** B-08, C-01, C-03.

### 3. *Hobbs v. Winchester Corporation* [1910] 2 KB 471 · Court of Appeal
- **Facts.** A butcher sold meat that was unfit for human food. He did not know that it was diseased.
- **Issue.** Is knowledge of the unfitness a necessary element?
- **Held.** No. He was liable under the Public Health legislation without proof of knowledge, because the statute protects the public from a social danger.
- **Principle.** A statute may impose liability without *mens rea* where the words, the subject matter and the mischief show that Parliament intended it.
- **After.** The leading example of strict liability in food and health legislation. In India see *M.H. George* (1965).
- **Used in:** A-20, B-08, C-03.

### 4. *Sweet v. Parsley* [1970] AC 132 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** A householder let a farmhouse to students and rarely visited. Cannabis was smoked in it. She was convicted of being concerned in the management of premises used for smoking cannabis †.
- **Issue.** Does the offence need proof that she knew of the use?
- **Held.** Yes. The conviction was quashed. Lord Reid said that whenever a section is silent on *mens rea*, the court must read in words such as "intentionally" or "knowingly", unless the statute clearly or by necessary implication excludes them.
- **Principle.** The presumption of *mens rea* is strong and the exceptions are narrow.
- **After.** Applied in *B v. DPP* (2000) †.
- **Used in:** C-03.

### 5. *Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v. A-G of Hong Kong* [1985] AC 1 · Privy Council
- **Facts.** Builders departed from the approved plans in building work in Hong Kong. The ordinance did not mention a mental element.
- **Issue.** Is the presumption of *mens rea* displaced?
- **Held.** Yes. The convictions were upheld. The presumption is strong where the offence is truly criminal, but can be displaced where the statute deals with an issue of social concern (such as public safety) and strict liability will encourage greater vigilance.
- **Principle.** Strict liability is permissible where the statute deals with an issue of social concern and the penalty promotes its object.
- **After.** Often cited in Indian cases on economic and regulatory offences.
- **Used in:** B-08, C-01, C-03.

### 6. *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* AIR 1965 SC 722 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) †
- **Facts.** The accused flew from Zurich to Manila with gold in his possession. He did not declare it on landing at Bombay. An exchange-control notification of the Reserve Bank forbade bringing gold into India without permission.
- **Issue.** Is knowledge of the notification (and so *mens rea*) required for the offence?
- **Held.** No. The majority held him guilty: the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act and the notification were enacted to protect the national economy, and the offence did not depend on knowledge of the law.
- **Principle.** Where a statute is concerned with a grave social evil and its object would be defeated, the offence may be one of strict liability.
- **After.** Contrasted with *Nathulal* (1966), where the Court insisted on *mens rea*.
- **Used in:** A-20, B-04, B-08, C-01, C-03.

### 7. *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* AIR 1966 SC 43 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A grain dealer was prosecuted for stocking grain in contravention of a licensing order made under the Essential Commodities Act. He said that he had acted in good faith and had applied for a licence †.
- **Issue.** Is *mens rea* an ingredient of the offence under the Essential Commodities Act?
- **Held.** Yes. The Court held that *mens rea* is an essential ingredient of a criminal offence unless the statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication; the accused, having acted honestly, was acquitted †.
- **Principle.** The Indian presumption of *mens rea*: displaced only by the words of the statute, its subject matter and the social danger.
- **After.** Read with *M.H. George* to show both sides of the question.
- **Used in:** A-04, B-04, B-08, C-01, C-03.

### 8. *Pulicherla Nagaraju v. State of A.P.* (2006) 11 SCC 444 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A conviction for murder was challenged on the ground that the intention to kill was not proved.
- **Issue.** How does a court find the intention of an accused?
- **Held.** Intention is inferred from the circumstances: the nature of the weapon, the part of the body targeted, the force used, the occasion (sudden fight or premeditation), earlier enmity and conduct after the act.
- **Principle.** Intention is a state of mind proved by inference from the facts.
- **Used in:** C-12.

### 9. *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* AIR 1961 SC 1698 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A candidate applied to Patna University for admission to the M.A. examination as a private candidate. He claimed to be a graduate and a teacher and sent false certificates. The university discovered the truth and refused him admission.
- **Issue.** Is that an attempt to cheat, or only preparation?
- **Held.** An attempt. Buying the forms and preparing the papers were preparation, but sending the application was the last act within the offender's power, directly connected with the intended deception, and failed only because of the university's discovery.
- **Principle.** An attempt begins when preparation ends and the offender does an act towards the commission of the offence; it is punishable although the offence was not completed.
- **After.** Applied in later cases on attempt (BNS s. 62; IPC s. 511).
- **Used in:** A-18, B-01, B-07, B-24, C-02, C-21.

### 10. *Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab* (1991) 4 SCC 341 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A person carrying paddy by truck from Punjab towards Delhi was stopped at a checkpoint still inside Punjab. An order prohibited the export of paddy from the State.
- **Issue.** Was this an attempt to export, or only preparation?
- **Held.** Preparation. He had not reached the point from which the paddy would have left the State, so the act was not an attempt.
- **Principle.** Where the offender has not yet done an act directly connected with the commission, the conduct is preparation, which is not punishable.
- **After.** Compare *Mohd. Yakub* (1980): the dividing line is drawn case by case.
- **Used in:** B-07, B-24, C-02.

### 11. *State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub* (1980) 3 SCC 57 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Silver was loaded in a truck near the sea coast and was intercepted before it could be exported.
- **Issue.** Was that an attempt to export, or only preparation?
- **Held.** An attempt. The acts were proximate to the offence and, but for the interception, the export would have followed.
- **Principle.** An act is an attempt if it is proximate to the offence and, but for the interruption, would have resulted in it.
- **After.** Compare *Malkiat Singh* (1991).
- **Used in:** B-07, C-02.

### 12. *R. v. Eagleton* (1855) Dears CC 515 · Court for Crown Cases Reserved †
- **Facts.** A baker contracted to supply bread to the poor of a parish and delivered loaves that were underweight. The charge was attempting to obtain money by false pretences †.
- **Issue.** Had he attempted the offence, or only prepared it?
- **Held.** Parke B.: acts remotely leading towards the commission are not attempts; the acts must be immediately, and not remotely, connected with the commission. The delivery of the loaves was not an attempt †.
- **Principle.** The proximity test: remote acts are preparation; an act that is proximate to the offence is an attempt.
- **After.** Later English tests ("last act", "unequivocal act", "more than merely preparatory", Criminal Attempts Act, 1981) refine it. India has no general test.
- **Used in:** B-07, C-02.

### 13. *Haughton v. Smith* [1975] AC 476 and *R. v. Shivpuri* [1987] AC 1 · House of Lords †
- **Facts.** *Haughton*: police intercepted a van of stolen goods, and the goods were then delivered to the accused; they had ceased to be stolen. *Shivpuri*: the accused took a package which he believed to contain prohibited drugs; it held only harmless vegetable matter.
- **Issue.** Is there an attempt when the offence was impossible?
- **Held.** *Haughton*: no attempt, because the offence was legally impossible. *Shivpuri*: the House overruled that approach and held that the accused was guilty of attempting to deal in drugs.
- **Principle.** Whether an impossible attempt is punishable depends on the statute. Illustration (b) to IPC s. 511 (an empty pocket) shows that Indian law punishes it.
- **After.** English law now follows the Criminal Attempts Act, 1981. In India BNS s. 62 (IPC s. 511) applies to attempts to commit offences punishable by imprisonment.
- **Used in:** A-19, C-02.

### 14. *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) 2 SCC 684 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench, 4:1)
- **Facts.** Bachan Singh was sentenced to death for murder and challenged the constitutionality of the death penalty for murder (IPC s. 302) and of the sentencing procedure.
- **Issue.** Is the death penalty for murder valid under Arts. 14, 19 and 21?
- **Held.** Valid. But the court must impose it only in the **rarest of rare cases**, where the alternative of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed, after balancing aggravating and mitigating circumstances, and must give special reasons.
- **Principle.** The "rarest of rare" doctrine; life imprisonment is the rule and death the exception.
- **After.** *Machhi Singh* (1983) listed five categories; later benches have stressed that the categories are guidelines (*Shatrughan Chauhan*, 2014). BNS s. 103(1) keeps death or life for murder.
- **Used in:** A-25, A-75, B-12, B-13, C-04.

### 15. *Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab* (1983) 3 SCC 470 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A gang led by Machhi Singh killed seventeen persons in several villages in a series of attacks arising from a feud †.
- **Issue.** How should the "rarest of rare" test be applied?
- **Held.** The Court listed five categories as guidelines: the manner of the murder, the motive, the anti-social or socially abhorrent nature of the crime, the magnitude, and the personality of the victim.
- **Principle.** The categories help to apply *Bachan Singh*, but each case turns on its own aggravating and mitigating circumstances.
- **After.** Later decisions treat the categories as guidelines, not as a rigid list.
- **Used in:** A-25, B-12, C-04.

### 16. *Mithu v. State of Punjab* (1983) 2 SCC 277 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench)
- **Facts.** Mithu, who was serving a sentence of life imprisonment, committed murder in jail. IPC s. 303 made death the only punishment for murder by a life convict.
- **Issue.** Is a mandatory death sentence valid?
- **Held.** No. Section 303 was struck down as violating Arts. 14 and 21: a law that takes away the court's discretion and imposes death without considering the circumstances is arbitrary.
- **Principle.** A mandatory death sentence is unconstitutional; the court must be able to choose.
- **After.** BNS s. 104 (IPC s. 303) now gives the court a choice between death and imprisonment for life (meaning the remainder of natural life) ‡.
- **Used in:** A-76, B-12, C-04, C-12.

### 17. *Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014) 3 SCC 1 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Condemned prisoners whose mercy petitions had been rejected after long delay petitioned for commutation of their sentences.
- **Issue.** Does inordinate delay in deciding a mercy petition, or mental illness, justify commutation?
- **Held.** Yes. Undue delay in executing a death sentence, and mental illness or schizophrenia, are supervening circumstances that may justify commutation to life imprisonment; the Court laid down guidelines for the protection of death-row prisoners.
- **Principle.** Art. 21 protects a condemned prisoner until execution.
- **After.** Followed in later commutation cases.
- **Used in:** B-12, C-04, C-10.

### 18. *Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1961 SC 600 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Godse, convicted as a co-conspirator in the assassination of Mahatma Gandhi and sentenced to imprisonment for life, claimed release after he had served about fourteen years, counting remission †.
- **Issue.** Does a life sentence end after fourteen years?
- **Held.** No. A sentence of life imprisonment is imprisonment for the remainder of the convict's life, unless the appropriate Government remits or commutes it; no rule gives automatic release after fourteen years.
- **Principle.** Life imprisonment means imprisonment for life, not a fixed term.
- **After.** *Swamy Shraddananda (2)* (2008) and *Union of India v. V. Sriharan* (2016) allow the court to fix a term beyond fourteen years without remission, in place of death.
- **Used in:** A-24, C-04.

### 19. *Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka* (2008) 13 SCC 767 and *Union of India v. V. Sriharan* (2016) 7 SCC 1 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** *Swamy Shraddananda*: murder of a wife for her property; the Court doubted the death sentence but thought ordinary life imprisonment too light. *Sriharan*: a Constitution Bench considered the Rajiv Gandhi assassination convicts and the power to remit.
- **Issue.** May a court impose a fixed term of imprisonment longer than fourteen years, without remission, instead of death?
- **Held.** Yes. The Constitution Bench in *Sriharan* held that a High Court or the Supreme Court may impose a fixed term or imprisonment for the remainder of natural life without remission, as a middle path between death and ordinary life imprisonment.
- **Principle.** A "modified punishment" may be imposed; the executive's power of remission is subject to it.
- **After.** BNS s. 6 (fractions of terms) treats life imprisonment as twenty years only for computing fractions †.
- **Used in:** A-24, C-04.

### 20. *Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) †
- **Facts.** A prisoner under sentence of death was held in solitary confinement in Tihar jail, and wrote to a judge about it and about the treatment of prisoners.
- **Issue.** May solitary confinement be imposed on a prisoner as a matter of course?
- **Held.** No. Solitary confinement may be ordered only by the court as part of a sentence (IPC ss. 73–74) and within the statutory limits; it cannot be inflicted on a prisoner under sentence of death before execution.
- **Principle.** Prisoners keep their fundamental rights under Art. 21; punishments within prison must be authorised by law.
- **After.** A prison-reform line of cases follows. The BNS retains the limits on solitary confinement.
- **Used in:** C-04.

### 21. *Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement* (2005) 4 SCC 530 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) †
- **Facts.** A bank was prosecuted under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, which prescribed a minimum term of imprisonment and fine.
- **Issue.** Can a company be prosecuted and punished where the law prescribes imprisonment?
- **Held.** Yes. A company is a "person"; where imprisonment is mandatory, the court can impose a fine on the company.
- **Principle.** Corporate criminal liability: a company can be prosecuted for offences requiring *mens rea*, and the sentence of imprisonment is read as a fine.
- **After.** BNS s. 2(26) defines "person" to include a company or association.
- **Used in:** C-01.

## 2B · Unit 1B · Constructive liability: common intention and common object

### 22. *Queen v. Gorachand Gope* (1866) 5 WR (Cr) 45 · Calcutta High Court †
- **Facts.** Several persons took part in a joint attack in which death was caused. The facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** Is a participant liable for the act of another in a joint criminal enterprise?
- **Held.** The original IPC s. 34 was read narrowly. The decision exposed a gap in the Code.
- **Principle.** A group acting together may share liability for the result.
- **After.** Act XXVII of 1870 added the words "in furtherance of the common intention of all" to s. 34 (BNS s. 3(5)).
- **Used in:** C-05.

### 23. *Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor* (1925) 52 IA 40 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** A group went to a post office to rob it, and the postmaster was shot dead. The accused stood outside as a guard and had not fired the fatal shot †.
- **Issue.** Is a person who takes no part in the killing, but stands by, liable under s. 34?
- **Held.** Yes. He was liable for murder, because he was present as a participant in the common intention. Lord Sumner: "they also serve who only stand and wait".
- **Principle.** Section 34 requires participation in the criminal act, which may be by standing guard or by being ready to assist.
- **After.** Qualified by *Mahbub Shah* (1945) and *Pandurang* (1955): the common intention must be shown, not assumed.
- **Used in:** A-34, B-15, C-05.

### 24. *Mahbub Shah v. King-Emperor* (1945) 72 IA 148 · Privy Council †
- **Facts.** Several men took part in an armed attack in which a man was killed; the question was whether all of them shared the intention to kill †.
- **Issue.** What must be proved to apply s. 34?
- **Held.** A prior meeting of minds and a pre-arranged plan. Common intention is not the same as **similar** intention. The plan may be formed on the spot, shortly before the act. It is inferred from conduct before, during and after the act.
- **Principle.** Section 34 creates no offence; it states a rule of joint liability for an act done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all.
- **After.** Followed by the Supreme Court in *Pandurang* (1955) and many later cases.
- **Used in:** A-29, A-30, B-15, C-05.

### 25. *Pandurang v. State of Hyderabad* AIR 1955 SC 216 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Four accused were tried together for a killing. Facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** How is the common intention to be proved, and must the accused have done a criminal act?
- **Held.** The common intention must be proved to be shared by the accused, and the court must be satisfied that each took part in a way that furthered it; the accused who played no part may be acquitted though others are convicted.
- **Principle.** Section 34 needs a shared intention, plus participation.
- **After.** *Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1963) stressed participation in the commission of the offence †.
- **Used in:** C-05.

### 26. *Willie (William) Slaney v. State of M.P.* AIR 1956 SC 116 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Persons were convicted under s. 149 or s. 34 on charges that named one but not the other section.
- **Issue.** May an accused be convicted under s. 34 although charged only under s. 149, or the reverse?
- **Held.** Yes, if the facts proved establish a common intention and the accused has not been prejudiced, the omission to mention the section is an irregularity that is cured (the Code of Criminal Procedure provisions on errors in the charge).
- **Principle.** The sections overlap; the court asks whether the accused had notice of the case against him.
- **After.** Followed in *Nanak Chand v. State of Punjab* (1955) and *Krishna Govind Patil v. State of Maharashtra* (1963).
- **Used in:** B-17, C-05.

### 27. *Masalti v. State of U.P.* AIR 1965 SC 202 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A village clash between two groups led to several deaths. A large number of persons were tried on the basis that they were members of an unlawful assembly †.
- **Issue.** When is a person present in a crowd a member of an unlawful assembly sharing its common object?
- **Held.** Mere presence in a crowd does not make a person a member; but a person who is present and shares the common object is liable although he did no overt act. Where a large number of persons are accused, the court should as a rule of prudence insist on reliable evidence of several witnesses.
- **Principle.** Membership of an unlawful assembly, not an overt act, is the basis of s. 149; but the court must be careful not to convict bystanders.
- **After.** Followed in many riot cases.
- **Used in:** A-123, B-16, B-56, C-05, C-24.

### 28. *Mizaji v. State of U.P.* AIR 1959 SC 572 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Members of an unlawful assembly attacked a person, and one of them caused a death with a weapon †.
- **Issue.** Is each member liable for an offence committed by another that was not the common object?
- **Held.** Only if the offence was one that the members **knew to be likely** to be committed in prosecution of the object (the second limb of s. 149). Where knowledge is not proved, liability is limited.
- **Principle.** The two limbs of s. 149: offence committed in prosecution of the common object, and offence the members knew to be likely.
- **After.** Cited for the distinction between the limbs.
- **Used in:** A-33, B-16, C-05, C-24.

### 29. *Lalji v. State of U.P.* (1989) 1 SCC 437 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** An unlawful assembly attacked a victim and one of its members killed him. The question was the liability of the others †.
- **Issue.** Is a member of the unlawful assembly liable for murder committed by another member?
- **Held.** Yes. If the murder was committed in prosecution of the common object, every member is liable as if he had committed it, whether or not he did the act.
- **Principle.** Section 149 makes every member vicariously liable for the offence done in prosecution of the object.
- **Used in:** C-05.

### 30. *Dalip Singh v. State of Punjab* AIR 1953 SC 364 and *Bharwad Mepa Dana v. State of Bombay* AIR 1960 SC 289 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Convictions of fewer than five accused for rioting and murder were challenged because the number of identified and charged accused was below five.
- **Issue.** Does the conviction of fewer than five persons defeat s. 149?
- **Held.** No, if the evidence shows that others, who were not identified or charged, were also present and shared the common object, an assembly of five or more is made out.
- **Principle.** Unlawful assembly needs five or more members, but not five who are convicted.
- **After.** Cited with *Masalti*.
- **Used in:** C-05.

## 2C · Unit 2 · Inchoate crimes: abetment, conspiracy, abetment of suicide

### 31. *Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh* (2001) 9 SCC 618 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A husband, in a quarrel, told his wife "go and die" (or words to that effect), and she killed herself †.
- **Issue.** Are words uttered in anger, with no intention that the person should kill herself, abetment of suicide?
- **Held.** No. Instigation means to goad, urge or provoke a person to do an act; it needs a positive act and intention. A remark made in the heat of the moment is not instigation.
- **Principle.** Abetment by instigation needs a mental process of encouragement and an intention that the act should follow.
- **After.** Followed in *Sanju* (2002) and *Gangula Mohan Reddy* (2010).
- **Used in:** A-37, A-43, B-19, B-21, C-06.

### 32. *Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v. State of M.P.* (2002) 5 SCC 371 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** After a quarrel the accused told the deceased words to the effect "go and die". The deceased killed himself some time later †.
- **Issue.** Do those words amount to abetment of suicide?
- **Held.** No. A bare wish, a reprimand or an angry outburst is not instigation unless the speaker intends the act to follow; there must be a direct link between the words and the act.
- **Principle.** The words must be a direct incitement.
- **After.** Followed in later cases on suicide.
- **Used in:** A-43, B-21, C-06.

### 33. *Gangula Mohan Reddy v. State of A.P.* (2010) 1 SCC 750 and *Amalendu Pal @ Jhantu v. State of W.B.* (2010) 1 SCC 707 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Persons were charged with abetting suicide of a person who had been harassed. In *Gangula* the deceased had been quarrelling with relatives; in *Amalendu Pal* the allegation was harassment of a person who killed himself †.
- **Issue.** Is harassment alone abetment of suicide?
- **Held.** No. Abetment involves a mental process of instigating or intentionally aiding. The instigation must be of such a degree that it leaves the victim no other option; harassment alone, without a proximate act of incitement, is not enough.
- **Principle.** Abetment of suicide (BNS s. 108; IPC s. 306) needs instigation, conspiracy or intentional aid.
- **After.** BNS s. 108 keeps the offence; s. 107 (IPC s. 305) covers the suicide of a child or a person of unsound mind.
- **Used in:** B-21, C-06.

### 34. *Chitresh Kumar Chopra v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2009) 16 SCC 605 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A person was charged with abetting an offence; the question was what the prosecution must show †.
- **Issue.** What is needed to prove abetment?
- **Held.** Instigation, conspiracy or intentional aid must be shown; the words or acts must be a direct incitement, and a general expression is not enough.
- **Principle.** The three modes of abetment in BNS s. 45 (IPC s. 107).
- **Used in:** C-06.

### 35. *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (1996) 2 SCC 648 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) †
- **Facts.** The accused were convicted of abetment of suicide of a woman. They relied on *P. Rathinam* (1994), which had held that the right to life includes the right to die, so that IPC ss. 309 and 306 were void.
- **Issue.** Does the right to life under Art. 21 include the right to die?
- **Held.** No. *Rathinam* was overruled. Section 306 (abetment of suicide) is valid. The right to life does not include the right to extinguish life, and the "right to die with dignity" means the right to a dignified life up to the point of natural death.
- **Principle.** Suicide and its abetment are not protected by Art. 21.
- **After.** *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) allowed passive euthanasia with safeguards. BNS dropped the attempt-to-suicide offence (IPC s. 309) but keeps s. 226 (attempted suicide to compel a public servant) †.
- **Used in:** B-21.

### 36. *Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)* (1988) 3 SCC 609 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi on 31 October 1984 by her bodyguards, Beant Singh and Satwant Singh. Kehar Singh, a relative of Beant Singh and an official, was alleged to have been a conspirator.
- **Issue.** How is a criminal conspiracy proved?
- **Held.** The agreement is the gist of the offence and is seldom proved by direct evidence; it is inferred from the circumstances, acts and conduct, if they point unambiguously to an agreement. The Court upheld the conviction of Kehar Singh and Satwant Singh and acquitted Balbir Singh.
- **Principle.** The law of conspiracy (BNS s. 61; IPC ss. 120A–120B).
- **After.** Applied in *Navjot Sandhu* (2005) and many conspiracy cases.
- **Used in:** A-44, B-22, C-07.

### 37. *State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu @ Afsan Guru* (2005) 11 SCC 600 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The attack on Parliament on 13 December 2001, in which five armed terrorists were killed along with security personnel. Four persons were tried for conspiracy and waging war; all four were convicted by the trial court.
- **Issue.** Who were conspirators, and was there a waging of war against the Government?
- **Held.** The Court upheld the conspiracy and waging-war convictions and the death sentence of Mohd. Afzal (Afzal Guru) on circumstantial evidence, acquitted S.A.R. Geelani, and dealt separately with Shaukat Hussain Guru and Navjot Sandhu, whose sentences were lower †. It held that an attack on Parliament was waging war.
- **Principle.** The elements of conspiracy and the meaning of "waging war" (BNS s. 147; IPC s. 121).
- **After.** Afzal Guru's death sentence was confirmed and carried out in 2013 †.
- **Used in:** A-122, C-07.

### 38. *Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab* (1977) 4 SCC 540 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Several persons were tried for a conspiracy to commit offences in a series of dealings, and it was argued that there was one conspiracy.
- **Issue.** Is there a single conspiracy or several?
- **Held.** Where several agreements share a common design, there is one conspiracy; where each has a separate object and no common design, there are separate conspiracies.
- **Principle.** The test of unity of the conspiracy is the common design.
- **Used in:** C-07.

### 39. *Topandas v. State of Bombay* AIR 1956 SC 33 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** One of two alleged conspirators was acquitted, and the question was whether the other could be convicted of conspiracy.
- **Issue.** May one be convicted of conspiracy if the other conspirator is acquitted?
- **Held.** If the only two alleged conspirators are tried together and one is acquitted, the other cannot be convicted; but a person may be convicted of conspiring with others unknown or not tried.
- **Principle.** A conspiracy needs two or more persons.
- **Used in:** C-07.

### 40. *State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa* (1996) 4 SCC 659 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Accused were charged with a conspiracy connected with a terrorist offence (the Bombay bombings case under the TADA Act) †.
- **Issue.** What mental element must the conspirator have?
- **Held.** The accused must know of the plan and intend to aid it; knowledge of the object and intent to help are needed.
- **Principle.** The mental element of conspiracy.
- **Used in:** C-07.

## 2D · Unit 3 · General exceptions and private defence

### 41. *M'Naghten's Case* (1843) 10 Cl & F 200 · House of Lords
- **Facts.** Daniel M'Naghten shot Edward Drummond, secretary to the Prime Minister, believing him to be Sir Robert Peel, under a delusion that the Government was persecuting him. He was acquitted on the ground of insanity.
- **Issue.** What is the test of insanity as a defence?
- **Held.** The judges answered the Lords' questions: everyone is presumed sane; to establish the defence it must be clearly proved that, at the time of the act, the accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act, or that it was wrong.
- **Principle.** The cognitive test of legal insanity.
- **After.** India follows it in IPC s. 84 (BNS s. 22), which uses "incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law".
- **Used in:** A-54, B-28, C-10.

### 42. *Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat* AIR 1964 SC 1563 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The accused killed his wife with multiple stab wounds and pleaded insanity.
- **Issue.** On whom is the burden of proving insanity, and how is the plea tested?
- **Held.** The burden is on the accused, but only to a preponderance of probability (not beyond reasonable doubt). The court considers the whole conduct, including motive, planning, concealment, and conduct before and after the act; abnormality, a fit of anger or a family history does not suffice.
- **Principle.** Legal insanity is cognitive incapacity at the time of the act; the burden is on the accused to a preponderance of probability.
- **After.** Followed in later insanity cases.
- **Used in:** A-53, B-28, C-10.

### 43. *Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.* (2008) 16 SCC 109 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** An accused charged with murder pleaded unsoundness of mind. The facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** Is every mental disorder a defence?
- **Held.** No. Unsoundness of mind in IPC s. 84 means legal insanity, not medical insanity; the accused must show cognitive incapacity at the time of the act.
- **Principle.** Legal insanity and medical insanity differ; only legal insanity is a defence.
- **Used in:** A-55, B-28, C-10.

### 44. *Sheralli Wali Mohammed v. State of Maharashtra* (1972) 4 SCC 79 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** An accused who had killed pleaded insanity, relying on an absence of motive and some abnormal behaviour †.
- **Issue.** Is absence of motive and abnormal behaviour enough?
- **Held.** No. They do not by themselves prove legal insanity.
- **Principle.** Insanity must be proved at the time of the act.
- **Used in:** C-10.

### 45. *Basdev v. State of PEPSU* AIR 1956 SC 488 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A landowner, drunk at a gathering, shot a boy. He pleaded drunkenness.
- **Issue.** Is intoxication a defence to murder?
- **Held.** No. Drunkenness is no excuse, but where the offence needs a specific intent the court must consider whether he was so drunk as to be incapable of forming it; mere drunkenness is not enough. His conduct (walking, talking, giving orders) showed that he could form the intent, so he was convicted.
- **Principle.** Voluntary intoxication is no defence unless it renders the accused incapable of forming the specific intent.
- **After.** BNS ss. 23–24 (IPC ss. 85–86) †.
- **Used in:** A-57, B-29, C-10.

### 46. *DPP v. Beard* [1920] AC 479 and *DPP v. Majewski* [1977] AC 443 · House of Lords †
- **Facts.** *Beard*: a man raped and killed a girl while drunk. *Majewski*: a man, after taking drink and drugs, attacked people in a public house.
- **Issue.** Does intoxication excuse?
- **Held.** *Beard*: drunkenness is no defence unless it renders the accused incapable of forming the specific intent; insanity from drink is a defence. *Majewski*: voluntary intoxication is no defence to offences of basic intent because getting drunk is itself reckless.
- **Principle.** Voluntary intoxication: no defence, but relevant to specific intent.
- **After.** India follows the same approach in BNS ss. 23–24.
- **Used in:** B-29, C-10.

### 47. *Bratty v. A-G for Northern Ireland* [1963] AC 386 · House of Lords †
- **Facts.** A man strangled a girl and claimed that he had acted in a state of automatism.
- **Issue.** Is automatism a defence?
- **Held.** Only if the act is wholly involuntary; automatism resulting from disease of the mind is insanity; a mere impulse is not enough.
- **Principle.** Voluntary act is needed for *actus reus*.
- **Used in:** B-05, C-03.

### 48. *R. v. Tolson* (1889) 23 QBD 168 and *R. v. Prince* (1875) LR 2 CCR 154 · Court for Crown Cases Reserved †
- **Facts.** *Tolson*: a woman remarried after her husband had been absent for some years, honestly and reasonably believing him dead; he returned. *Prince*: the accused took an unmarried girl under sixteen out of her father's possession, believing that she was eighteen.
- **Issue.** Is honest belief in a state of facts a defence?
- **Held.** *Tolson*: honest and reasonable belief in facts which, if true, would make the act innocent, is a defence to bigamy. *Prince*: honest belief that the girl was over sixteen was no defence, because the statute fixed the age as a condition of the offence.
- **Principle.** Mistake of fact in good faith excuses (BNS ss. 14, 17; IPC ss. 76, 79), but not where the statute makes the fact irrelevant.
- **After.** In India a mistake of law is no defence; a mistake of fact must be in good faith (BNS s. 2(11)).
- **Used in:** B-08, B-25, C-03, C-11.

### 49. *R. v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884) 14 QBD 273 · Queen's Bench Division
- **Facts.** Shipwrecked sailors adrift in an open boat killed and ate the cabin boy after many days without food.
- **Issue.** Is necessity a defence to murder?
- **Held.** No. The accused were convicted of murder and sentenced to death, later commuted to six months' imprisonment.
- **Principle.** Necessity is not a defence to the intentional killing of an innocent person.
- **After.** In India BNS s. 19 (IPC s. 81) excuses an act done without criminal intent to prevent greater harm; it does not cover intentional killing †.
- **Used in:** A-50, B-26, C-11.

### 50. *Salil Bali v. Union of India* (2013) 7 SCC 705 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** The Juvenile Justice Act, 2000 fixed the age of a juvenile at eighteen. Petitioners argued that it should be lowered after the Delhi gang rape of December 2012.
- **Issue.** Is the age of eighteen valid?
- **Held.** Yes. The Court upheld eighteen as consistent with the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Beijing Rules.
- **Principle.** The age of a juvenile is a matter of legislative policy.
- **After.** The Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 allows a child aged sixteen to eighteen who commits a heinous offence to be tried as an adult after a preliminary assessment †.
- **Used in:** C-11.

### 51. *Poonai Fattemah v. Emperor* (1869) 12 WR Cr 7 · Calcutta High Court †
- **Facts.** A snake-charmer invited a person to be bitten by a snake that he said was harmless; the person was bitten and died.
- **Issue.** Does consent excuse an act that causes death?
- **Held.** No. Consent is no defence where the act is likely to cause death or grievous hurt.
- **Principle.** Consent is limited (BNS ss. 25–31; IPC ss. 87–93).
- **Used in:** C-11.

### 52. *Jai Dev v. State of Punjab* AIR 1963 SC 612 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** An armed clash over possession of land led to deaths; the accused pleaded private defence †.
- **Issue.** Is there a right of private defence where there is time to seek help?
- **Held.** No. The right is not available where there is time to have recourse to the public authorities, and it is not a right of retaliation.
- **Principle.** Section 40 (IPC s. 99): there is no right where there is time to have recourse to the protection of public authorities.
- **Used in:** B-31, C-08, C-09.

### 53. *Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab* (2010) 2 SCC 333 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A conviction for murder in which the accused pleaded private defence of the body †.
- **Issue.** What are the governing principles of private defence?
- **Held.** The right is defensive and not retaliatory; there is no duty to retreat; the defender is not judged in golden scales; the burden of proof is on the accused to a preponderance of probabilities; injuries on the accused are relevant.
- **Principle.** The ten principles of the right of private defence.
- **After.** Often cited together with *Vidhya Singh* and *Yogendra Morarji*.
- **Used in:** C-08, C-09.

### 54. *Yogendra Morarji v. State of Gujarat* (1980) 2 SCC 218 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A man killed in an encounter in which he said he was defending himself †.
- **Issue.** Must the defender retreat?
- **Held.** No. There is no duty to retreat; the question is whether he had a reasonable apprehension of danger and used only the force needed.
- **Principle.** The defender need not run away.
- **Used in:** C-08.

### 55. *Vidhya Singh v. State of M.P.* (1971) 2 SCC 1 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A fatal assault in which the accused pleaded private defence †.
- **Issue.** What is the nature of the right?
- **Held.** The right is one of defence, not retribution; the burden is on the accused; force in excess of what the need requires is an offence.
- **Principle.** Defence, not retaliation.
- **Used in:** C-08.

### 56. *Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1968 SC 702 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A dispute over land: the accused, who were in possession, resisted forcible dispossession, and injuries resulted.
- **Issue.** May a person in possession resist forcible dispossession by force?
- **Held.** Yes, but only with the force the need requires; the accused need not plead the right if the facts show it.
- **Principle.** Private defence of property, against a trespasser, extends to the harm needed to defend it.
- **Used in:** C-08, C-09.

### 57. *Mohinder Pal Jolly v. State of Punjab* (1979) 3 SCC 30 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The accused fired on a crowd that was causing a disturbance at his premises, with fatal result.
- **Issue.** Does the right of private defence of property extend to causing death by firing on a crowd?
- **Held.** No. It extends to causing death only in the cases listed in the Code; firing on a crowd that was trespassing or causing mischief, without a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt, was not justified.
- **Principle.** Section 41 (IPC s. 103): death may be caused in defence of property only in four cases.
- **Used in:** C-09.

### 58. *Puran Singh v. State of Punjab* (1975) 4 SCC 518 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A fatal affray in which the accused pleaded private defence and had injuries on his own person †.
- **Issue.** Is the plea established if it is not specifically taken?
- **Held.** It is enough if the facts show the right; the court looks at the injuries on the accused and the circumstances.
- **Principle.** The plea may be inferred from the evidence.
- **Used in:** C-08.

## 2E · Unit 4A-1 · Homicide and hurt

### 59. *Reg. v. Govinda* (1876) ILR 1 Bom 342 · Bombay High Court (Melvill J.)
- **Facts.** Govinda, a youth of eighteen, knocked down his wife, put his knee on her chest and struck her two or three violent blows with his closed fist. She died of a rupture of a blood vessel in the brain.
- **Issue.** Murder or culpable homicide not amounting to murder?
- **Held.** Culpable homicide not amounting to murder. Govinda intended to cause bodily harm, but the injury was not one that he knew to be likely to cause death, nor was it sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death.
- **Principle.** The difference between culpable homicide and murder is one of the degree of probability of death.
- **After.** Explained in *Virsa Singh* (1958) and *Punnayya* (1977).
- **Used in:** B-36, C-12.

### 60. *Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab* AIR 1958 SC 465 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Virsa Singh stabbed Khem Singh in the abdomen with a spear. The wound was deep and the intestines protruded; the deceased died. The defence argued that the accused did not intend to cause death.
- **Issue.** Does the third limb of s. 300 (IPC) (bodily injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death) require intention to cause death?
- **Held.** No. Bose J. laid down four conditions: (1) a bodily injury is present; (2) its nature is proved; (3) it was intended (that particular injury was intended, not an accidental one); (4) it was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. Once these are proved, the offence is murder; whether he intended death is irrelevant.
- **Principle.** The four-step test under the third limb of IPC s. 300 (BNS s. 101).
- **After.** Followed in *Punnayya* (1977) and in *Rajwant Singh v. State of Kerala* (1966) †.
- **Used in:** B-35, C-12.

### 61. *State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya* AIR 1977 SC 45 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The State appealed against a High Court decision that the accused was guilty only of culpable homicide not amounting to murder. The facts of the assault are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** How is the line between culpable homicide and murder drawn?
- **Held.** Culpable homicide is the genus and murder the species; the difference lies in the degree of probability of death. The Court laid down a three-step approach: (i) did the accused cause death by an act? (ii) is it culpable homicide under s. 299? (iii) if so, is it murder under s. 300, or does an Exception apply?
- **Principle.** The test of probability: "likely" (culpable homicide) against "sufficient in the ordinary course of nature" or "in all probability" (murder).
- **After.** Followed in many later decisions.
- **Used in:** A-70, B-36, C-12.

### 62. *K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1962 SC 605 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Commander Nanavati, a naval officer, was told by his wife that she had had an affair with Prem Ahuja. He went to his ship, took a revolver and shot Ahuja dead at his flat. A jury of the Bombay Sessions Court returned a verdict of not guilty by 8:1; the Judge referred the case to the High Court, which convicted him of murder.
- **Issue.** Was Nanavati deprived of self-control by grave and sudden provocation (Exception 1)?
- **Held.** No. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction: there was time to cool between the confession and the shooting (he drove to the ship, took the revolver and went to Ahuja's flat), so the provocation was not sudden.
- **Principle.** Grave and sudden provocation: the test is that of a reasonable man; the passion must not have cooled.
- **After.** The case led to the abolition of jury trials in Bombay †. Nanavati was later released on a pardon †.
- **Used in:** A-71, B-37, C-12, C-13.

### 63. *Mancini v. DPP* [1942] AC 1 · House of Lords †
- **Facts.** Mancini, a waiter in a Soho club, stabbed a man with a dagger-knife after a quarrel and was convicted of murder; he pleaded provocation †.
- **Issue.** How is the test of provocation applied?
- **Held.** The provocation must be such as to cause a reasonable man to lose self-control and to act as the accused did: the retaliation must bear a reasonable relation to the provocation.
- **Principle.** The reasonable-man test of provocation, applied in India.
- **After.** The English law was changed by the Homicide Act, 1957 and then by the Coroners and Justice Act, 2009.
- **Used in:** B-37, C-13.

### 64. *Surinder Kumar v. UT Chandigarh* (1989) 2 SCC 217 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A fight started suddenly and one party killed the other with a weapon †.
- **Issue.** Does Exception 4 (sudden fight) apply where the offender takes undue advantage?
- **Held.** No. A person who kills an unarmed man with a deadly weapon, or continues to strike after the other has fallen, takes undue advantage, and Exception 4 does not apply.
- **Principle.** Exception 4: no premeditation, a sudden fight, heat of passion, and no undue advantage or cruel or unusual manner.
- **Used in:** C-13.

### 65. *Om Prakash v. State of Punjab* AIR 1961 SC 1782 and *State of Maharashtra v. Balram Bama Patil* (1983) 2 SCC 28 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Persons convicted of attempt to murder (IPC s. 307) argued that the injuries caused were not sufficient to cause death †.
- **Issue.** Must hurt be caused, or must the injury be sufficient to cause death, in an attempt to murder?
- **Held.** No. Hurt need not be caused: the offence depends on the intention or knowledge with which the act is done and the circumstances; the act must be one that would have been murder if death had followed.
- **Principle.** Attempt to murder (BNS s. 109; IPC s. 307): the intention to kill, and an act towards it.
- **Used in:** A-81, B-24, B-40, C-14.

### 66. *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A patient with respiratory distress was admitted to a hospital in Ludhiana; the oxygen cylinder was said to be empty and the doctors were said to have arrived late; the patient died †. The complainant, the patient's son, filed a criminal complaint under IPC s. 304A against the doctors.
- **Issue.** What is the standard of negligence in criminal law against a doctor?
- **Held.** A doctor is liable under s. 304A only for **gross** negligence or recklessness; a mere error of judgment or lack of ordinary care is not enough. A private complaint should not be entertained without credible medical opinion. The Court laid down guidelines to prevent harassment of doctors.
- **Principle.** Criminal negligence is gross negligence (BNS s. 106(1); IPC s. 304A).
- **After.** BNS s. 106(1) prescribes up to two years for a registered medical practitioner for a medical procedure ‡.
- **Used in:** A-79, B-39, C-14.

### 67. *Empress of India v. Idu Beg* (1881) ILR 3 All 776 · Allahabad High Court †
- **Facts.** Idu Beg shot a person at night, believing him to be a ghost †.
- **Issue.** Is a death caused by a rash or negligent act punishable, and what is rashness?
- **Held.** Straight J. distinguished rashness (hazarding a dangerous or wanton act with the knowledge that it is so and may cause injury, but without intention to cause it, hoping that harm will not follow) from negligence (breach of duty by omission to do what a reasonable person would do). The accused was held liable for a rash act.
- **Principle.** Rashness is conscious risk-taking; negligence is want of the care a reasonable person would take.
- **After.** Applied in cases under IPC s. 304A (BNS s. 106).
- **Used in:** B-39, C-14.

### 68. *Kurban Hussein Mohammedali Rangwalla v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1965 SC 1616 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Fire broke out in a match factory in the course of manufacture and persons died; the manager was charged under IPC s. 304A †.
- **Issue.** What connection must exist between the negligence and the death?
- **Held.** The rash or negligent act must be the **proximate and efficient cause** of death, without the intervention of another's act or negligence; a remote or contributing cause is not enough.
- **Principle.** Death by negligence needs gross negligence and proximate causation.
- **Used in:** B-39, C-14.

### 69. *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) 5 SCC 1 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench) †
- **Facts.** A public-interest petition asked the Court to recognise a right to die with dignity, including the validity of advance directives ("living wills").
- **Issue.** Is passive euthanasia lawful?
- **Held.** Yes. The right to life includes the right to die with dignity; passive euthanasia and advance directives are lawful, with safeguards, until Parliament legislates.
- **Principle.** Withdrawal of life support at the end of life is not culpable homicide, but active euthanasia remains unlawful.
- **After.** It applied *Aruna Shanbaug* (2011) and developed *Gian Kaur* (1996). The safeguards were simplified by the Court in January 2023 †.
- **Used in:** C-11, C-13.

## 2F · Unit 4A-2 · Kidnapping, restraint and force, offences against women and children

### 70. *S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras* AIR 1965 SC 942 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A minor girl left her father's house of her own accord and met the accused, and went with him to register a marriage. The father complained of kidnapping from lawful guardianship.
- **Issue.** Is there a "taking" if the girl goes with the accused of her own accord?
- **Held.** No, on those facts. The Court distinguished **taking** from **allowing a minor to accompany**: where the girl leaves of her own accord and the accused takes no active part in her leaving, there is no taking. Some active participation or inducement is needed.
- **Principle.** Kidnapping from lawful guardianship needs taking or enticing; mere passive acquiescence is not enough.
- **After.** Qualified by *State of Haryana v. Raja Ram* (1973).
- **Used in:** A-93, B-44, C-15.

### 71. *State of Haryana v. Raja Ram* (1973) 1 SCC 544 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A minor girl left her father's house and was found with the accused. The High Court acquitted him, relying on *Varadarajan*; the State appealed †.
- **Issue.** How wide are the words "takes or entices"?
- **Held.** Wider than "allowing to accompany". The object of the section is to protect minors and the rights of guardians. Persuasion that overcomes the child's reluctance, or influence that brings about her leaving, is enough; the girl's consent is no defence.
- **Principle.** Enticement is an inducement which excites hope or desire; the child's consent is immaterial.
- **After.** Followed in *Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat* (1973).
- **Used in:** B-44, C-15.

### 72. *Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat* (1973) 2 SCC 413 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** The accused induced a minor girl to leave her parents' custody †.
- **Issue.** Is a minor's consent a defence?
- **Held.** No. Enticement is an act of inducement; the consent of the minor is immaterial.
- **Principle.** The offence protects the guardian's right.
- **Used in:** B-44, C-15.

### 73. *Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742 · Queen's Bench †
- **Facts.** Part of a public bridge was fenced off for spectators at a regatta. The plaintiff tried to pass through the fence, was stopped, and was told that he could go back.
- **Issue.** Is that wrongful confinement (false imprisonment)?
- **Held.** No. He was prevented from going in one direction but was free to go back: a partial obstruction is not a total confinement.
- **Principle.** Restraint is partial obstruction; confinement is total (BNS ss. 126–127; IPC ss. 339–340).
- **After.** Applied in the Indian cases on restraint and confinement.
- **Used in:** B-41, C-16.

### 74. *Stephens v. Myers* (1830) 4 C & P 349 · Nisi Prius †
- **Facts.** At a parish meeting the defendant, sitting some distance from the plaintiff, shook his fist at him and advanced, but was stopped before he could reach him.
- **Issue.** Is that an assault?
- **Held.** Yes, if he had the present means of carrying out the threat (the chairman, between them, stopped him); the Court left it to the jury.
- **Principle.** Assault needs a gesture or preparation causing apprehension of immediate criminal force (BNS s. 130; IPC s. 351).
- **After.** In *R. v. Ireland* (1998) silent telephone calls were held capable of amounting to an assault †.
- **Used in:** C-16.

### 75. *Kans Raj v. State of Punjab* (2000) 5 SCC 207 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A young wife died in her matrimonial home within seven years of marriage after allegations of harassment for dowry by her husband's family. Details of the facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** What does "soon before her death" in IPC s. 304B mean?
- **Held.** There is no fixed time limit; there must be a **proximate and live link** between the cruelty or harassment and the death. The Court examined the conduct of the husband's family on the evidence and convicted those against whom it was established.
- **Principle.** Dowry death (BNS s. 80; IPC s. 304B) needs cruelty or harassment for dowry soon before the death, within seven years of marriage.
- **After.** Followed in *Satbir Singh* (2021).
- **Used in:** B-45, C-17.

### 76. *Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana* (2021) 6 SCC 1 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A young married woman died in her marital home; the husband's family were charged with dowry death and murder in the alternative †.
- **Issue.** How are ss. 304B and 302 charged, and what does "soon before" mean?
- **Held.** The Court discussed the meaning of "soon before", the burden on the accused, the framing of an alternative charge under s. 302, and held that the presumption (Evidence Act s. 113B) operates once the foundational facts are proved.
- **Principle.** The presumption of dowry death applies on proof of the foundational facts (now BSA s. 118 †).
- **Used in:** C-17.

### 77. *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) 8 SCC 273 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A husband sought anticipatory bail in a case under IPC s. 498A and the Dowry Prohibition Act after arrest was threatened.
- **Issue.** Should the police arrest automatically on a complaint of cruelty?
- **Held.** No. There should be no automatic arrest; the police must apply the checklist under Cr.P.C. s. 41 and record reasons; magistrates must not authorise detention mechanically.
- **Principle.** Arrest is a serious step; the offence is cognisable and non-bailable but misuse is to be checked.
- **After.** *Rajesh Sharma v. State of U.P.* (2017) 10 SCC 472 directed family welfare committees; *Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 443 modified the directions †.
- **Used in:** B-46, C-17.

### 78. *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014) 4 SCC 427 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Laxmi was attacked with acid in Delhi in 2005 when she was fifteen, by a man who had been pursuing her. She petitioned for regulation of the sale of acid and for compensation.
- **Issue.** How should acid sales be controlled and how should victims be compensated?
- **Held.** The Court regulated the sale of acid, directed compensation and free medical treatment for victims and asked the States to make rules.
- **Principle.** Acid attack has its own offences (BNS s. 124; IPC ss. 326A–326B) with a minimum of ten years.
- **After.** The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 inserted ss. 326A and 326B into the IPC.
- **Used in:** A-85, C-14.

### 79. *Independent Thought v. Union of India* (2017) 10 SCC 800 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A petition challenged Exception 2 to IPC s. 375, which allowed sexual intercourse by a man with his wife aged fifteen or more.
- **Issue.** Is intercourse with a wife aged between fifteen and eighteen rape?
- **Held.** Yes. The Court read down the Exception, so that intercourse with a wife below eighteen is rape. It referred to the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act and the POCSO Act.
- **Principle.** A person below eighteen cannot consent; the law should be consistent.
- **After.** BNS s. 63 (Exception 2) now refers to a wife not under eighteen †. The Court did not decide on marital rape of adult wives.
- **Used in:** B-47, C-18.

### 80. *Pramod Suryabhan Pawar v. State of Maharashtra* (2019) 9 SCC 608 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A woman complained that the accused had had sexual relations with her on a promise of marriage that he did not keep. He was charged with rape.
- **Issue.** Is sexual intercourse on a false promise of marriage rape?
- **Held.** The Court distinguished a **false promise** (no intention to marry from the start: consent vitiated by misconception of fact) from a **breach of a promise** (a genuine promise later broken: not rape).
- **Principle.** Consent obtained by a misconception of fact is no consent.
- **After.** BNS s. 69 (new) punishes sexual intercourse by deceitful means including a false promise of marriage †.
- **Used in:** A-135, C-25.

### 81. *Vikram Singh v. Union of India* (2015) 9 SCC 502 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Challenge to IPC s. 364A (kidnapping for ransom), which prescribed death or life imprisonment.
- **Issue.** Is the penalty of s. 364A valid?
- **Held.** Yes. The Court upheld the section, including the punishment, as a response to a serious crime and not disproportionate.
- **Principle.** Kidnapping for ransom (BNS s. 140(2); IPC s. 364A) is punishable with death or life imprisonment.
- **Used in:** C-15.

### 82. *Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India* (2011) 5 SCC 1 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A public-interest petition on child trafficking for circuses and the conditions of children kept there.
- **Issue.** What should the State do to protect trafficked children?
- **Held.** The Court gave directions to the Government to stop the practice, rescue the children, and rehabilitate them.
- **Principle.** The State's duty to children under Art. 21 and Art. 23.
- **Used in:** C-18.

## 2G · Unit 4B · Offences against property

### 83. *K.N. Mehra v. State of Rajasthan* AIR 1957 SC 369 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Two trainee airmen took a training aircraft from an airfield and flew off; they were charged with theft.
- **Issue.** Was theft complete when they moved the aircraft with a dishonest intention, although they later abandoned it?
- **Held.** Yes. The dishonest intention existed when the aircraft started to move, and theft was complete then. The later abandonment did not matter.
- **Principle.** Theft needs a moving of movable property out of the possession of another with dishonest intention (BNS s. 303; IPC s. 378).
- **Used in:** B-48, B-49, C-19.

### 84. *Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan* AIR 1963 SC 1094 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** A public servant induced a clerk to take a file from the office so that it could be used for a private purpose, and it was later returned.
- **Issue.** Is a temporary removal for a dishonest purpose theft?
- **Held.** Yes. It is not necessary that the taker intends to keep the property permanently; the intention to cause wrongful gain or loss, even temporarily, suffices.
- **Principle.** Dishonest taking includes taking for a temporary purpose causing wrongful loss.
- **Used in:** B-48, C-19.

### 85. *Ram Jas v. State of U.P.* (1970) 2 SCC 740 and *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar* (2000) 4 SCC 168 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** Persons were prosecuted for cheating on the allegation that they had made promises that they did not keep.
- **Issue.** When does a breach of promise become cheating?
- **Held.** To convict of cheating, the prosecution must show a fraudulent or dishonest intention **at the time the promise was made**; failure to keep a promise does not by itself prove that intention.
- **Principle.** Cheating (BNS s. 318; IPC s. 415) needs deception and dishonest inducement; a breach of contract alone is a civil wrong.
- **After.** *S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar* (2002) 1 SCC 241 distinguished a breach of contract from cheating and breach of trust †.
- **Used in:** B-53, C-21.

### 86. *Dr. Vimla v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1963 SC 1572 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** The accused was prosecuted for cheating; the Court discussed the meaning of "defraud". The facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** What is meant by "defraud"?
- **Held.** To defraud involves deceit and injury, actual or likely, to a person or to the public; an advantage or injury to a person's right is enough.
- **Principle.** Fraudulently and dishonestly differ: defrauding needs deceit with injury.
- **Used in:** A-10, C-21.

### 87. *State of Gujarat v. Jaswantlal Nathalal* AIR 1968 SC 700 and *R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1962 SC 1821 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** *Dalmia*: a managing director of an insurance company was charged with misappropriating the company's funds. *Jaswantlal*: a charge of criminal breach of trust of property in the accused's hands; the facts are not reproduced here †.
- **Issue.** What does "entrustment" mean in criminal breach of trust?
- **Held.** *Jaswantlal*: ownership is not required, but there must be a trust, that is, a fiduciary handing-over. *Dalmia*: "property" includes funds, and a person with dominion over a company's funds is entrusted.
- **Principle.** Entrustment or dominion over property is the essence of criminal breach of trust (BNS s. 316; IPC s. 405).
- **Used in:** C-21.

## 2H · Unit 4C · Offences against the State and public tranquillity

### 88. *Queen-Empress v. Bal Gangadhar Tilak* (1897) ILR 22 Bom 112 · Bombay High Court
- **Facts.** Tilak, editor of the *Kesari*, was charged with sedition for articles on Shivaji's coronation and a poem. Strachey J. directed the jury on the meaning of "disaffection".
- **Issue.** What is disaffection?
- **Held.** Strachey J.: disaffection means the absence of affection and includes disloyalty and all feelings of enmity; it does not need an incitement to violence. Tilak was convicted and sentenced to eighteen months' rigorous imprisonment.
- **Principle.** The early broad reading of IPC s. 124A.
- **After.** Gandhi pleaded guilty to sedition in 1922 for articles in *Young India*. The Federal Court in *Niharendu Dutt Majumdar* (1942) took a narrower view; the Privy Council in *Sadashiv Narayan Bhalerao* (1947) restored the broad one.
- **Used in:** C-23.

### 89. *Niharendu Dutt Majumdar v. King-Emperor* (1942) FCR 38 and *King-Emperor v. Sadashiv Narayan Bhalerao* (1947) 74 IA 89 · Federal Court and Privy Council †
- **Facts.** Persons were prosecuted for seditious speeches; the courts disagreed on whether incitement to violence was essential.
- **Issue.** Must sedition involve incitement to violence?
- **Held.** The Federal Court said that sedition needs incitement to disorder or a tendency to create public disorder. The Privy Council disagreed: incitement to violence is not essential; it is enough that the words excite disaffection.
- **Principle.** Two competing views on the scope of s. 124A.
- **After.** The Supreme Court in *Kedar Nath* (1962) preferred the Federal Court's view.
- **Used in:** C-23.

### 90. *Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar* AIR 1962 SC 955 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench)
- **Facts.** Kedar Nath Singh, a member of the Forward Communist Party, made a speech in Bihar attacking the Government in strong language and urging people to overthrow it. He was convicted under IPC ss. 124A and 505(b).
- **Issue.** Is s. 124A valid under Art. 19(1)(a) and Art. 19(2)?
- **Held.** Valid, if read narrowly. (i) "Government established by law" is the visible symbol of the State; (ii) strong words expressing disapprobation of measures of the Government, with a view to their improvement, do not fall under the section; (iii) the section applies only to words or acts that have the **tendency or intention to create disorder or incite violence**.
- **Principle.** Sedition needs incitement to violence or a tendency to create public disorder.
- **After.** *Tara Singh Gopi Chand* (Punjab High Court, 1951) and *Ram Nandan v. State of U.P.* (Allahabad High Court, 1959) had held s. 124A void; *Ram Nandan* was overruled. Applied in *Balwant Singh* (1995) and *Vinod Dua* (2021).
- **Used in:** A-120, B-55, C-23.

### 91. *Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab* (1995) 3 SCC 214 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** On the day of Indira Gandhi's assassination the accused shouted slogans such as "Khalistan Zindabad" in the street. There was no reaction from the public.
- **Issue.** Is casual sloganeering sedition?
- **Held.** No. Without a call to violence or any reaction, the slogans did not amount to sedition or promoting enmity.
- **Principle.** Words must have a tendency to incite violence or disorder (*Kedar Nath*).
- **Used in:** B-55, C-23.

### 92. *Vinod Dua v. Union of India* (2021) · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** A Himachal Pradesh FIR for sedition against a journalist for criticising the Government in a video on his YouTube channel.
- **Issue.** Does criticism of the Government amount to sedition?
- **Held.** No. The Supreme Court quashed the FIR, applying *Kedar Nath*: every journalist is entitled to protection from sedition charges unless the words incite violence or disorder.
- **Principle.** Criticism of Government measures is not sedition.
- **Used in:** C-23.

### 93. *S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India* · Supreme Court order of 11 May 2022
- **Facts.** Petitions challenged the validity of IPC s. 124A after the Union said it would reconsider the provision.
- **Issue.** May prosecutions under s. 124A continue while the Government re-examines it?
- **Held.** The Supreme Court (CJI N.V. Ramana, Surya Kant and Hima Kohli JJ.) directed that the provision be **kept in abeyance**: no fresh FIRs, no investigation or coercive measures under s. 124A; pending cases and appeals to be kept in abeyance. The question of validity remained open.
- **Principle.** An interim suspension of s. 124A, not a decision on validity.
- **After.** The BNS (in force from 1 July 2024) has no sedition; s. 152 is challenged. The challenge was undecided on the last date I could check (22 May 2026, from secondary reports) †.
- **Used in:** B-55, C-23.

### 94. *Arup Bhuyan v. State of Assam* (2011) 3 SCC 377 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The accused was convicted under the TADA Act for being a member of a banned organisation (ULFA).
- **Issue.** Is mere membership of a banned organisation an offence?
- **Held.** No. Mere membership is not an offence unless the person resorts to violence or incites others to violence.
- **Principle.** Guilt by association is not enough; incitement or violence is needed.
- **Used in:** C-23.

### 95. *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) 5 SCC 1 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Challenge to s. 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, which punished offensive messages online.
- **Issue.** Is s. 66A valid under Art. 19(1)(a)?
- **Held.** No. The Court struck it down as vague and as a restriction going beyond Art. 19(2). It distinguished **discussion, advocacy and incitement**: only incitement with a proximate connection to public disorder can be restricted.
- **Principle.** The line between advocacy and incitement.
- **After.** Cited for the limits on speech offences.
- **Used in:** C-23.

### 96. *State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu* and *Mohd. Ajmal Amir Kasab v. State of Maharashtra* (2012) 9 SCC 1 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** *Navjot Sandhu*: the Parliament attack of 2001. *Kasab*: the attack by armed men on Mumbai on 26 November 2008; Kasab was caught alive.
- **Issue.** What is waging war against the Government (IPC s. 121; BNS s. 147)?
- **Held.** An organised use of force against the Government of India is waging war; an attack on Parliament or on a city in the circumstances of 26/11 qualifies. A mere riot or disturbance does not. Kasab's conviction and death sentence were upheld.
- **Principle.** Waging war needs an organised attempt to overawe or overthrow the Government by force, not a mere riot.
- **After.** Kasab was hanged on 21 November 2012 †.
- **Used in:** A-122.

### 97. *Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police, Ahmedabad* (1973) 1 SCC 227, *Ramlila Maidan Incident, In re* (2012) 5 SCC 1, and *Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police* (2020) 10 SCC 439 · Supreme Court †
- **Facts.** *Himat Lal*: rules requiring permission for meetings on public streets. *Ramlila Maidan*: a midnight police action on a sleeping crowd at a protest in Delhi in June 2011. *Amit Sahni*: a prolonged protest blocking a public road (Shaheen Bagh) in 2019–2020.
- **Issue.** How far does the right to assemble peacefully (Art. 19(1)(b)) extend?
- **Held.** *Himat Lal*: no right to hold a meeting on a public street without regulation; the State may regulate reasonably. *Ramlila*: peaceful protest is part of the right and police force must be proportionate. *Amit Sahni*: a protest cannot occupy a public way indefinitely.
- **Principle.** The right to assemble is subject to reasonable restrictions in the interests of public order.
- **After.** *Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India* (2020) 3 SCC 637 held that restrictions such as shutdowns of the internet must be proportionate †.
- **Used in:** C-24.

## 2I · Unit 4D · Offences relating to marriage

### 98. *Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1965 SC 1564 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** The accused, already married, was alleged to have gone through a second marriage. The evidence of ceremonies was weak.
- **Issue.** What does "solemnised" mean in IPC s. 494?
- **Held.** "Solemnised" means celebrated with proper ceremonies and in due form. Under Hindu law, the essential ceremonies (the *saptapadi*, or *datta homa* as custom requires) must be proved; mere admission is not enough. The conviction was set aside.
- **Principle.** The second marriage in bigamy (BNS s. 82; IPC s. 494) must be solemnised with the ceremonies required by the personal law.
- **After.** *Kanwal Ram v. Himachal Pradesh Administration* (1966) applies the same rule †. Strict proof of ceremonies lets many bigamists escape.
- **Used in:** A-130, B-59, C-25.

### 99. *Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India* (1995) 3 SCC 635 · Supreme Court
- **Facts.** Hindu husbands converted to Islam in order to marry again without dissolving their first marriage.
- **Issue.** Does conversion dissolve the first Hindu marriage and allow a second marriage?
- **Held.** No. The first Hindu marriage does not dissolve by the husband's conversion; it can be dissolved only under the Hindu Marriage Act. The second marriage is void and the husband is guilty of bigamy under IPC s. 494. The Court urged the Government to implement a Uniform Civil Code under Art. 44.
- **Principle.** Conversion is not a licence to commit bigamy.
- **After.** *Lily Thomas v. Union of India* (2000) 6 SCC 224 affirmed the rule; *John Vallamattom v. Union of India* (2003) 6 SCC 611 repeated the call for a Uniform Civil Code †.
- **Used in:** B-59, C-25.

### 100. *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2018) 2 SCC 189 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench)
- **Facts.** Joseph Shine, an Indian residing in Italy, filed a petition challenging IPC s. 497 (adultery) and Cr.P.C. s. 198(2). Under s. 497 a man who had intercourse with another man's wife without his consent or connivance was guilty of adultery; the wife was not punishable; only the husband could complain.
- **Issue.** Is s. 497 compatible with Arts. 14, 15 and 21?
- **Held.** No. On 27 September 2018 a Constitution Bench struck down s. 497 and s. 198(2) as unconstitutional: the section treated the wife as the husband's property, offended dignity (Art. 21), and discriminated on grounds of sex (Arts. 14, 15). Adultery remains a ground of divorce but is not a crime.
- **Principle.** Autonomy and equality within marriage; adultery is a civil wrong and not a crime.
- **After.** It overruled *Yusuf Abdul Aziz v. State of Bombay* (1954), *Sowmithri Vishnu v. Union of India* (1985) and *V. Revathi v. Union of India* (1988) †. The BNS (2023) has no adultery offence ‡; a Parliamentary Standing Committee recommended in November 2023 a gender-neutral adultery offence †, which has not been enacted as far as I found.
- **Used in:** A-132, A-133, B-60, C-01, C-25.

### 101. *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 · Supreme Court (Constitution Bench)
- **Facts.** A challenge to IPC s. 377, so far as it criminalised consensual sexual acts between adults.
- **Issue.** Is s. 377 valid as applied to consensual acts between adults?
- **Held.** No. The Court read down s. 377 so that it does not apply to consensual acts of adults in private, but kept it for non-consensual acts, acts with minors and bestiality.
- **Principle.** Dignity and autonomy in intimate relations.
- **After.** BNS (2023) does not carry s. 377 over ‡; the position of non-consensual acts is dealt with by other provisions †.
- **Used in:** C-01.
