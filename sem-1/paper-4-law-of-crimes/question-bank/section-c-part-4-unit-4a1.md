## C-12 · When does culpable homicide amount to murder? Distinguish culpable homicide from murder, with cases

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q11 (C, "culpable homicide amounting to murder"), Dec 2024 Q10 (C, "culpable homicide v. murder, with cases"), Dec 2023 Q10 (C, "murder v. culpable homicide"), Dec 2022 Q13 (C, "culpable homicide amounting to murder"). The most frequently asked topic in the paper: four times in four sittings. Related: A-68 to A-70, B-34 to B-36.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction (genus and species) 1 · culpable homicide s. 100 (text, essentials, Explanations) 4 · murder s. 101 (four limbs, illustrations) 5 · *Virsa Singh* 2 · difference table 4 · cases 3 · exceptions and punishment 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Homicide is the killing of a human being by a human being. It is lawful (private defence, judicial execution, accident) or unlawful. Unlawful homicide is (i) **culpable homicide amounting to murder**, (ii) **culpable homicide not amounting to murder**, or (iii) **death caused by a rash or negligent act**. In the scheme of the Code, **culpable homicide is the genus and murder is a species**: every murder is a culpable homicide, but not every culpable homicide is murder (*State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya*, AIR 1977 SC 45 †). The provisions are BNS **s. 100** (culpable homicide) and **s. 101** (murder), which re-enact IPC ss. 299 and 300 ‡.

**1. Culpable homicide (BNS s. 100; IPC s. 299).** "Whoever causes death by doing an act **with the intention of causing death**, or **with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death**, or **with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death**, commits the offence of culpable homicide." ‡

*Essentials.* (i) A human being is dead; (ii) death is caused by an act of the accused (not necessarily the sole or immediate cause); (iii) the act is done with one of three mental states: **intention to cause death**; **intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death**; or **knowledge that death is likely**.

*Explanations* ‡: (1) A person who causes bodily injury to another who is labouring under a disorder, disease or bodily infirmity, and thereby accelerates his death, is deemed to have caused it. (2) Where death is caused by bodily injury, the person causing it is deemed to have caused the death although proper remedies and skilful treatment might have prevented it. (3) The causing of death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide, but it may be culpable homicide to cause the death of a living child if any part of it has been brought forth, though the child has not breathed or been completely born.

*Illustration.* A lays sticks and turf over a pit, knowing that death is likely. Z, believing the ground firm, treads on it, falls in and dies: A has committed culpable homicide ‡.

**2. Murder (BNS s. 101; IPC s. 300).** Except in the cases excepted, culpable homicide is **murder** if the act by which the death is caused is done: ‡
1. **Firstly** — with the **intention of causing death**;
2. **Secondly** — with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender **knows to be likely to cause the death** of the person to whom the harm is caused;
3. **Thirdly** — with the intention of causing bodily injury to any person, and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death**;
4. **Fourthly** — if the person committing the act **knows that it is so imminently dangerous that it must, in all probability, cause death** or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, and commits it **without any excuse** for incurring the risk.

| Limb | Mental element | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Intention to cause death | Shooting to kill |
| 2nd | Intention to cause an injury which the offender **knows** is likely to cause death of **that** victim (special knowledge of his condition) | A blow to a man known to have a diseased spleen |
| 3rd | Intention to inflict a particular injury, **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature** to cause death | A spear thrust into the abdomen |
| 4th | **Knowledge** that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death, with no excuse | Firing into a crowd |

*Illustrations to s. 101* (as in IPC s. 300) †: A shoots Z intending to kill him: murder. A, knowing that Z has a disease such that a blow is likely to kill him, strikes him intending to hurt; Z dies: murder. A without any excuse fires a loaded cannon into a crowd and kills one: murder.

**3. The *Virsa Singh* test for the third limb.** In ***Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab*** (AIR 1958 SC 465) the accused thrust a spear into the abdomen of the victim, who died. Bose J. held that, to bring a case within the third limb, the prosecution must prove four things: (1) a **bodily injury is present**; (2) the **nature of the injury**; (3) an **intention to inflict that particular injury**, that is, that it was not accidental or unintended; and (4) that the injury is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death** (an objective question). If all are proved, it is murder; it is not necessary to prove that the offender intended to cause death or knew the injury would prove fatal †.

**4. Culpable homicide distinguished from murder**

| Point | Culpable homicide (s. 100) | Murder (s. 101) |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | **Genus** | **Species** |
| Intention to cause death | Yes | Yes (1st limb) |
| Bodily injury | Intention to cause an injury **likely** to cause death | Injury known to be likely to cause *that* victim's death (2nd), or **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature** (3rd) |
| Knowledge | The act is **likely** to cause death | The act is **so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability** cause death (4th), with no excuse |
| Probability of death | **Lower**: death is likely | **Higher**: death is very likely, almost certain |
| Exceptions | – | Five exceptions bring a murder back to culpable homicide |
| Punishment | s. 105: life, or 5 to 10 years + fine (intention); up to 10 years (knowledge) ‡ | s. 103(1): death or life + fine ‡ |

The correspondence is: (a) intention to kill = 1st limb; (b) intention to cause an injury likely to cause death = 2nd and 3rd limbs; (c) knowledge that death is likely = 4th limb. The difference is thus one of **degree of probability** of death.

**5. Leading cases**
- ***Reg. v. Govinda*** (1876) ILR 1 Bom 342. Govinda, a youth, knocked down his wife, put his knee on her chest and struck her two or three violent blows with his closed fist; she died of a rupture of a blood vessel in the brain. Melvill J. held it **culpable homicide not amounting to murder**: Govinda intended to cause bodily harm, but the injury was not one he knew to be likely to cause death and was not sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. He explained that the distinction is one of **degree of probability** †.
- ***State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya*** (AIR 1977 SC 45). The Court explained that culpable homicide is the genus and murder the species and that the difference lies in the **degree of probability** of death. It laid down a three-step approach: (i) did the accused cause death by an act? (ii) is the act culpable homicide under s. 299? (iii) if so, is it murder under s. 300, or does an exception apply? †
- ***Virsa Singh*** (1958): the third limb (above).
- ***Pulicherla Nagaraju v. State of A.P.*** (2006) 11 SCC 444. The court may infer intention from the nature of the weapon, the part of the body targeted, the amount of force used, the circumstances (sudden fight or premeditation), prior enmity, and the conduct after the act †.
- ***K.M. Nanavati*** (AIR 1962 SC 605): provocation refused, murder conviction upheld, because the passion had time to cool (C-13) †.

**6. Exceptions and punishment.** Culpable homicide is not murder if it falls in one of the **five Exceptions** to s. 101: grave and sudden provocation; excess of private defence; public servant exceeding powers; sudden fight; consent of a person above eighteen. It is then punished under s. 105 (see C-13). Murder is punished with **death or imprisonment for life, and fine** (s. 103(1) ‡); for a murder by five or more persons acting in concert on grounds of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief, each member is liable to death, life, or not less than seven years, and fine (s. 103(2), the "mob lynching" clause) ‡; and a person under sentence of imprisonment for life who commits murder is punished with death or imprisonment for life meaning the remainder of his natural life (s. 104) ‡. The IPC's mandatory death sentence for such a case (s. 303) was struck down in *Mithu v. State of Punjab* (1983) †.

**7. Example.** A strikes B once on the head with a lathi in a sudden quarrel and B dies. A did not intend to kill but knew a blow on the head was likely to cause death: culpable homicide (second part of s. 105). If A had used an iron rod, with several blows, intending to crush the skull, the injury would be sufficient in the ordinary course to cause death and it would be murder under the third limb, unless Exception 4 (sudden fight, no undue advantage) applies.

**Conclusion.** Culpable homicide becomes murder when the intention or knowledge reaches the higher degree of probability described in the four limbs of s. 101 and no Exception applies. The BNS has kept the IPC test unchanged, and the cases of *Govinda*, *Virsa Singh* and *Punnayya* continue to guide the courts.

## C-13 · Discuss the exceptions to murder: grave and sudden provocation, excess of private defence, public servant, sudden fight and consent

**Asked as:** not yet set separately at 20 marks; the Exceptions are an integral part of every "culpable homicide v. murder" answer (C-12). Related: A-71 to A-74, B-37, B-38.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and effect 2 · Exception 1 (provisos, tests, *Nanavati*) 6 · Exception 2 2 · Exception 3 2 · Exception 4 (conditions, cases) 4 · Exception 5 2 · comparison and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** BNS s. 101 (IPC s. 300) says that culpable homicide is murder "except in the cases hereinafter excepted". Five Exceptions follow. If a case falls within one of them, the offence is **culpable homicide not amounting to murder**, punishable under **s. 105** (IPC s. 304): imprisonment for life or for five to ten years and fine, if the act was done with intention to cause death or an injury likely to cause death; imprisonment up to ten years if with knowledge only ‡. The Exceptions recognise that certain mitigating circumstances reduce the offender's blame. The burden of proving them is on the accused, to a preponderance of probabilities (BSA s. 108 †).

**Exception 1: grave and sudden provocation** ‡
The offender, whilst **deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation**, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation or of any other person by mistake or accident. **Three provisos:** the provocation must not be (a) **sought or voluntarily provoked** by the offender as an excuse for killing or doing harm; (b) **given by anything done in obedience to the law**, or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of his powers; (c) **given by anything done in the lawful exercise of the right of private defence**. *Explanation:* whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough is a **question of fact**.

*Tests.*
1. **Provocation:** an act or words that cause the offender to lose self-control. In India, words and gestures can suffice.
2. **Grave:** such as would cause a **reasonable man**, of the same class of society and placed in the same situation as the accused, to lose self-control. The test is objective as well as subjective (did the accused actually lose control?).
3. **Sudden:** there must be no interval in which the passion could cool.
4. The death must be of the provoker, or of another by mistake or accident.

***K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra*** (AIR 1962 SC 605). Commander Nanavati was told by his wife that she had had an affair with Prem Ahuja. He went to his ship, took a revolver, drove to Ahuja's flat and shot him. The jury found him not guilty (8:1); the Bombay High Court set the verdict aside and convicted him of murder; the Supreme Court upheld the conviction and rejected the plea of grave and sudden provocation: the interval of some hours gave time to cool, and the conduct showed deliberation. The Court stated that (i) the test is that of the **reasonable man** of the accused's class in the same circumstances; (ii) words and gestures may amount to provocation in India; (iii) the **mental background** created by earlier acts of the victim may be considered; (iv) the act must be done **while the offender is deprived of self-control**, not after he has recovered. The case also led to the end of jury trials in Bombay †. *Mancini v. DPP* (1942) states the English reasonable-man test †.

**Exception 2: excess of private defence** ‡
The offender, in the exercise **in good faith** of the right of private defence of person or property, **exceeds the power** given to him by law and causes the death of the person against whom he exercises it, **without premeditation** and **without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary** for the purpose of defence. *Example:* A, attacked by a thief armed with a stick, defends himself in good faith, but uses a gun, which is more force than needed, and kills the thief. The right must have existed; if A had no right at all (for example, he was the aggressor), the exception does not apply (C-08, C-09).

**Exception 3: public servant exceeding powers** ‡
A public servant, or a person aiding him, acting for the **advancement of public justice**, **exceeds the powers** given to him by law and causes death by doing an act which he **in good faith believes to be lawful and necessary** for the due discharge of his duty, and **without ill-will** towards the person whose death is caused. *Example:* a police officer, in arresting a dangerous criminal, fires beyond what is necessary and causes death.

**Exception 4: sudden fight** ‡
The act is committed **without premeditation** in a **sudden fight**, in the **heat of passion** upon a **sudden quarrel**, and the offender has **not taken undue advantage** or acted in a **cruel or unusual manner**. *Explanation:* it is immaterial which party offers the provocation or commits the first assault.
- **Conditions** (all five): (i) sudden fight; (ii) no premeditation; (iii) heat of passion; (iv) sudden quarrel; (v) no undue advantage and no cruel or unusual manner. The position of the parties and the weapons used are looked at. A person who kills an unarmed man with a deadly weapon, or who continues to strike after the other has fallen, takes undue advantage (*Surinder Kumar v. UT Chandigarh*, (1989) 2 SCC 217 †).
- The exception applies only if the whole occurrence is one continuous quarrel; an accused who goes away and returns with a weapon shows premeditation (*Ghapoo Yadav v. State of M.P.*, (2003) 3 SCC 528 †).
- *Example:* two men quarrel over a debt; one strikes the other with a stick on the spot, and the other dies. This is Exception 4 if there was no undue advantage.

**Exception 5: consent** ‡
The person whose death is caused, being **above eighteen years**, **suffers death or takes the risk of death with his own consent**. *Example:* Z, above eighteen, agrees to be killed and A kills him: culpable homicide not amounting to murder. If Z is under eighteen, his consent is no consent, and A who abets his suicide is guilty of murder (the Act's own illustration, as in the IPC). A mercy killing with the consent of an adult is therefore, at best, culpable homicide not amounting to murder, while withdrawal of life support is lawful only under the safeguards in *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) †.

**Exception 1 compared with Exception 4**

| Point | Exception 1: provocation | Exception 4: sudden fight |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Provocation from the deceased (or another) | A sudden quarrel and fight between two or more persons |
| Offender's state | Deprived of self-control | Heat of passion |
| Reasonable-man test | Yes | Not in terms |
| Undue advantage / cruel manner | Not a condition | A condition |
| Provisos | Three | None |

**Punishment.** Under s. 105 (IPC s. 304): first limb – imprisonment for life, or for **five to ten years**, and fine (the BNS adds a five-year minimum; the IPC allowed up to ten years); second limb – up to ten years ‡.

**Critical note.** The Exceptions turn on questions of fact (gravity, suddenness, premeditation, undue advantage), which the court decides on the whole evidence and after the event, so outcomes vary from case to case. The BNS has kept them unchanged ‡.

**Conclusion.** The Exceptions reduce murder to culpable homicide where the killing arises from provocation, a sudden quarrel, a good-faith excess of defence or authority, or consent. Each has stated conditions which the accused must prove.

## C-14 · Define hurt and grievous hurt. Discuss causing death by negligence and attempt to murder under the BNS

**Asked as:** Dec 2022 Q1 (A-83, "grievous hurt", 4 marks). Not yet set at 20 marks. Related: A-79 to A-85, B-39, B-40.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** hurt 3 · grievous hurt (eight kinds) 5 · punishments (ss. 115, 117, 118, 124) 4 · death by negligence (s. 106) 4 · attempt to murder (s. 109) 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Chapter VI of the BNS deals with offences affecting the human body: culpable homicide and murder (ss. 100–113), hurt and grievous hurt (ss. 114–125). The BNS has kept the IPC's definitions of hurt and grievous hurt and has added new punishments and offences.

**1. Hurt (BNS s. 114; IPC s. 319).** Whoever causes **bodily pain, disease or infirmity** to any person is said to cause hurt ‡. "Bodily" is the key: mental distress alone is not hurt. **Voluntarily causing hurt** (s. 115(1); IPC s. 321): causing hurt with the **intention** of causing hurt or with the **knowledge** that hurt is likely. **Punishment** (s. 115(2); IPC s. 323): imprisonment up to **one year**, or fine up to **₹10,000**, or both ‡.

**2. Grievous hurt (BNS s. 116; IPC s. 320).** "The following kinds of hurt only are designated as grievous": ‡
- (a) **Emasculation**;
- (b) **permanent privation of the sight** of either eye;
- (c) **permanent privation of the hearing** of either ear;
- (d) **privation of any member or joint**;
- (e) **destruction or permanent impairing of the powers** of any member or joint;
- (f) **permanent disfiguration of the head or face**;
- (g) **fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth**;
- (h) **any hurt which endangers life**, or which causes the sufferer to be, during the space of **fifteen days**, in severe bodily pain or unable to follow his ordinary pursuits.

*Voluntarily causing grievous hurt* (s. 117(1); IPC s. 322): the offender intends or knows that he is likely to cause grievous hurt, and causes it. A person intending to cause one kind of grievous hurt and actually causing another is also liable (Explanation). Punishment: up to **seven years** and fine (s. 117(2); IPC s. 325) ‡.
- **New:** if the hurt results in **permanent disability or a persistent vegetative state**, rigorous imprisonment for not less than **ten years**, which may extend to imprisonment for life (the remainder of natural life) (s. 117(3)) ‡.
- **New:** where **five or more persons acting in concert** cause grievous hurt on the ground of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief, each member is punished with imprisonment up to **seven years** and fine (s. 117(4)) ‡.

**3. Hurt and grievous hurt compared**

| Point | Hurt | Grievous hurt |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Bodily pain, disease or infirmity (s. 114) | Eight kinds only (s. 116) |
| Seriousness | Less serious | More serious |
| Punishment (voluntary) | Up to 1 year, or ₹10,000, or both | Up to 7 years and fine |
| Intention test | Intended or knew hurt likely | Intended or knew grievous hurt likely |

**4. Dangerous weapons (BNS s. 118; IPC ss. 324, 326)** ‡. Hurt caused by means of an instrument for shooting, stabbing or cutting, or any instrument which, used as a weapon of offence, is likely to cause death; by fire or any heated substance; by poison or any corrosive substance; by any explosive; by any substance which is deleterious to the human body to inhale, swallow or receive into the blood; or by an animal: up to **three years**, fine up to ₹20,000, or both. Grievous hurt by such means: **life**, or imprisonment of either description for **one to ten years**, and fine.

**5. Acid attack (BNS s. 124; IPC ss. 326A, 326B)** ‡. Causing permanent or partial damage or deformity, burns, maiming, disfigurement, disability or grievous hurt by throwing or administering acid: imprisonment **not less than ten years**, which may extend to life, with fine to meet the victim's medical expenses; attempt: **five to seven years** and fine. In *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014) 4 SCC 427 the Supreme Court regulated the sale of acid and directed compensation and free treatment for victims †.

**6. Other sections of the group** (IPC order †). Hurt or grievous hurt to extort property or compel an illegal act (ss. 119; IPC 327, 329); to extort a confession (s. 120; IPC 330, 331); to deter a public servant (s. 121; IPC 332, 333); on grave and sudden provocation (s. 122; IPC 334, 335); hurt by poison (s. 123; IPC 328); acts endangering life or personal safety of others (s. 125; IPC 336–338).

**7. Causing death by negligence (BNS s. 106; IPC s. 304A)**
- **s. 106(1)** ‡: whoever causes the death of any person by a **rash or negligent act** not amounting to culpable homicide: imprisonment up to **five years** and fine; if the act is done by a **registered medical practitioner** while performing a medical procedure: up to **two years** and fine.
- **s. 106(2)** ‡ (new, "hit-and-run"): causing death by rash and negligent driving not amounting to culpable homicide, and **escaping without reporting** the incident to a police officer or Magistrate soon after: up to **ten years** and fine. This clause drew protests from transport operators in January 2024; I found no notification bringing it into force as of 23 July 2025 and have not rechecked since (verify).
- **Rashness** is a conscious taking of an unjustifiable risk, hoping that harm will not occur; **negligence** is failure to exercise the care a reasonable person would (*Empress of India v. Idu Beg*, (1881) ILR 3 All 776 †). For criminal liability the negligence must be **gross** and the act the **proximate cause** of death (*Kurban Hussein Mohammedali Rangwalla v. State of Maharashtra*, AIR 1965 SC 1616 †).
- **Medical negligence.** *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1: a doctor is liable only for **gross** negligence or recklessness; a mere error of judgment is not enough; a private complaint should not be entertained without credible medical opinion †.
- **Difference from culpable homicide:** no intention or knowledge that death is likely; the death is the result of carelessness.

**8. Attempt to murder (BNS s. 109; IPC s. 307)** ‡. Whoever does any act with such **intention or knowledge, and under such circumstances that, if he by that act caused death, he would be guilty of murder**, is punished with imprisonment up to **ten years** and fine; if **hurt** is caused to any person by such act, he is liable to **imprisonment for life** or the punishment above. A person under sentence of life imprisonment who does such an act and causes hurt may be punished with death or life imprisonment (the remainder of natural life).
- **Essentials:** (i) intention or knowledge such that, had death resulted, the offence would be murder; (ii) an act towards it, beyond preparation. **Hurt need not be caused**: the act itself suffices. The nature of the injury is a guide to the intention but not conclusive (*State of Maharashtra v. Balram Bama Patil*, (1983) 2 SCC 28; *Om Prakash v. State of Punjab*, AIR 1961 SC 1782 †).
- *Attempt to commit culpable homicide* is separately punished (s. 110; IPC s. 308) †.

**9. Illustrations**
1. A fires a pistol at B to kill him but misses: s. 109, up to ten years.
2. A strikes B with a rod and breaks his leg bone: grievous hurt, kind (g); s. 117(2).
3. A, a doctor, performs surgery with a rash and negligent act; the patient dies: s. 106(1), up to two years, subject to *Jacob Mathew* (gross negligence needed).
4. Five men assault X on account of his caste, causing grievous hurt: each liable under s. 117(4).

**Conclusion.** Hurt and grievous hurt are distinguished by the nature of the injury; grievous hurt is limited to the eight kinds. Negligent killing is punished less severely because there is no intention or knowledge. Attempt to murder is punished even if no hurt is caused, if the intention or knowledge is that of murder. The BNS has added higher penalties for permanent disability, group offences and hit-and-run.
