## C-23 · Discuss sedition under IPC s. 124A and its place after the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (s. 152)

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q11 (C, "sedition (IPC)"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2022 Q6 (B-55, "sedition"). Related: A-120 to A-122, B-55, B-58.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** history and text of s. 124A 4 · essentials 2 · *Kedar Nath* and other cases 5 · constitutional position and the order of 11 May 2022 3 · BNS s. 152 and comparison 4 · challenge and arguments 1 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** **Sedition** was the offence of bringing into hatred or contempt, or exciting disaffection towards, the Government established by law (IPC s. 124A). It was a colonial provision used against Tilak and Gandhi. The BNS, in force since 1 July 2024, does not contain the word; **s. 152** punishes acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India ‡. Both are under challenge before the Supreme Court.

**1. History** †. Section 124A was **not** in the IPC as enacted in 1860. It was inserted by Act XXVII of 1870 on the initiative of Sir James Fitzjames Stephen, in response to the Wahabi movement. Early cases: *Queen-Empress v. Jogendra Chunder Bose* (1891) (the Age of Consent Bill case); ***Queen-Empress v. Bal Gangadhar Tilak*** (1897), in which Strachey J. told the jury that "disaffection" means the absence of affection and includes disloyalty and all feelings of enmity; Tilak was convicted. In 1922 Mahatma Gandhi pleaded guilty to sedition for articles in *Young India* and described s. 124A as the prince among the political sections of the Code designed to suppress the liberty of the citizen (exact words not checked).

**2. Text of IPC s. 124A** (from memory †). "Whoever by **words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or otherwise**, **brings or attempts to bring into hatred or contempt, or excites or attempts to excite disaffection towards, the Government established by law in India**, shall be punished with **imprisonment for life**, to which fine may be added, or with imprisonment up to **three years**, to which fine may be added, or with **fine**." *Explanation 1:* "disaffection" includes disloyalty and all feelings of enmity. *Explanations 2 and 3:* comments expressing disapprobation of the measures or the administrative or other action of the Government, with a view to obtaining their alteration by lawful means, without exciting or attempting to excite hatred, contempt or disaffection, are not an offence.

**3. Essentials.** (1) Words, signs or visible representation (or otherwise); (2) bringing or attempting to bring into **hatred or contempt**, or exciting or attempting to excite **disaffection**; (3) towards the **Government established by law**, meaning the visible symbol of the State and not the persons in office; (4) after *Kedar Nath*, a **tendency or intention to create disorder, disturb law and order, or incite violence**.

**4. Constitutional position.** Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression. Article 19(2), as amended by the First Amendment (1951), permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of, among others, **public order**. *Tara Singh Gopi Chand v. State* (AIR 1951 Punj 27) and *Ram Nandan v. State of U.P.* (AIR 1959 All 101) had held s. 124A void under Art. 19(1)(a); the Supreme Court overruled *Ram Nandan* in *Kedar Nath* (1962) †.

**5. The test of disorder.** The Federal Court in *Niharendu Dutt Majumdar v. King-Emperor* (1942) held that sedition needs incitement to disorder or a tendency to create public disorder. The Privy Council in ***King-Emperor v. Sadashiv Narayan Bhalerao*** (1947) disagreed and held that incitement to violence is not essential; it is enough that the words excite disaffection †.

**6. Leading cases** (from memory †)
- ***Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar*** (AIR 1962 SC 955, Constitution Bench). Kedar Nath, a Forward Communist Party member, made a speech in Bihar attacking the Government in strong language and urging people to overthrow it. **Held:** s. 124A is **valid** if read narrowly. (i) "Government established by law" is the visible symbol of the State; (ii) strong words expressing disapprobation of the measures of Government, with a view to their improvement or alteration by lawful means, are not sedition; (iii) the section applies only to words or acts having the **intention or tendency to create disorder, or disturbance of law and order, or incitement to violence**; (iv) so read, it is a reasonable restriction in the interests of public order under Art. 19(2).
- ***Balwant Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1995) 3 SCC 214. Casual sloganeering ("Khalistan Zindabad") in the street on the day of Indira Gandhi's assassination, without any call to violence and with no reaction, is not sedition.
- ***Arup Bhuyan v. State of Assam*** (2011) 3 SCC 377. Mere membership of a banned organisation is not an offence unless the person resorts to violence or incites others to violence.
- ***Shreya Singhal v. Union of India*** (2015) 5 SCC 1. The Court struck down s. 66A of the Information Technology Act and distinguished **discussion, advocacy and incitement**: only incitement with a proximate connection to public disorder may be restricted.
- ***Vinod Dua v. Union of India*** (2021). A sedition FIR against a journalist for criticising the Government in a YouTube show was quashed, applying *Kedar Nath*.

**7. The order of 11 May 2022.** In ***S.G. Vombatkere v. Union of India***, the Supreme Court (CJI N.V. Ramana, Surya Kant and Hima Kohli JJ.) recorded the Union's statement that it would **re-examine** s. 124A and directed that the provision be **kept in abeyance**: no fresh FIRs, no investigation or coercive measure under s. 124A; pending cases and appeals to be kept in abeyance. The order did not strike down s. 124A. On 12 September 2023 the challenge was referred to a **larger Bench** ‡ (secondary report). The Law Commission (22nd Commission, 279th Report, 2023) recommended retaining s. 124A with procedural safeguards †.

**8. BNS s. 152** ‡. "Whoever, **purposely or knowingly**, by words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or by **electronic communication** or by **use of financial mean**, or otherwise, **excites or attempts to excite, secession or armed rebellion or subversive activities**, or **encourages feelings of separatist activities** or **endangers sovereignty or unity and integrity of India**; or indulges in or commits any such act shall be punished with **imprisonment for life** or with **imprisonment which may extend to seven years**, and shall also be liable to **fine**." *Explanation:* comments expressing disapprobation of the measures, or administrative or other action of the Government, with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means without exciting or attempting to excite the activities referred to, do not constitute an offence.

**9. IPC s. 124A and BNS s. 152 compared**

| Point | IPC s. 124A | BNS s. 152 |
|---|---|---|
| Heading | Sedition | Acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India |
| Target | Hatred, contempt or **disaffection** towards the **Government** | **Secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist feelings**, endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity |
| Mental element | Not stated | **Purposely or knowingly** |
| Means | Words, signs, visible representation, otherwise | Same, plus **electronic communication** and **use of financial means** |
| Acts | Words and attempts | Words and attempts, and **indulging in or committing** any such act |
| Punishment | Life ± fine; or up to 3 years ± fine; or fine only | Life, or up to 7 years, **and** fine; no fine-only option |
| Explanations | Three | One |

**10. Is s. 152 sedition by another name?** *The Government's case:* sedition, a colonial relic, was removed; s. 152 punishes acts endangering the country, not criticism of the Government. *Critics' case:* the substance of s. 124A remains in wider words; "subversive activities" and "separatist feelings" are undefined; the minimum punishment is higher; electronic and financial means widen the net. Because the Explanation protects criticism by lawful means, *Kedar Nath* (incitement to violence or disorder) is likely to guide the reading of s. 152.

**11. Challenge and current status** ‡ (secondary reports, last dated 22 May 2026; not rechecked). On **8 August 2025** a three-judge Bench (CJI B.R. Gavai, K. Vinod Chandran and N.V. Anjaria JJ.) issued notice on a PIL challenging s. 152 as "repackaged sedition" and tagged it with the pending challenge to s. 124A; no stay of s. 152 was reported. In February 2026 a Bench of CJI Surya Kant and Bagchi J. observed that judicial review of the provision was possible. On 21 May 2026 it was clarified that sedition-related proceedings can continue if the accused does not object. As of 22 May 2026, the validity of s. 124A was undecided and I found no judgment on s. 152.

**12. Arguments**

| For retaining | Against |
|---|---|
| The State's security needs protection from secessionist and violent speech | Vague ("disaffection", "contempt") and colonial; used against Tilak and Gandhi |
| *Kedar Nath* narrowed the offence enough | Misused against journalists, students and critics |
| Other democracies punish treason and seditious conspiracy | The UK repealed seditious libel in 2009 (†); incitement tests suffice |
| Other laws (UAPA) deal with violent acts | A free society must tolerate criticism of the Government |

**Conclusion.** Sedition was upheld in *Kedar Nath* only for speech with a tendency to disorder or violence. The BNS dropped the word but re-enacted a similar offence in s. 152, with a purposive or knowing mental element and a single protective Explanation. Whether either survives constitutional scrutiny is pending before the Supreme Court.

## C-24 · Explain unlawful assembly under the BNS. Distinguish lawful from unlawful gatherings, and rioting from affray

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q10 (C, "unlawful assembly (BNS); lawful v. unlawful gatherings"), Dec 2023 Q13(a) (C, "rioting v. affray"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2024 Q6 (B-56). Related: A-123 to A-129, B-56, B-57, C-05.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** unlawful assembly (definition, essentials) 5 · lawful v. unlawful gatherings 4 · punishments 2 · rioting 3 · affray 2 · comparison 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Public tranquillity is protected by BNS **ss. 189–197** (IPC ss. 141–160): unlawful assembly (s. 189), common object (s. 190), rioting (s. 191), provocation to riot (s. 192), liability of owner or occupier (s. 193), affray (s. 194), obstruction of public servants suppressing a riot (s. 195), and promoting enmity (ss. 196–197) ‡.

**1. Unlawful assembly (BNS s. 189(1); IPC s. 141).** An assembly of **five or more persons** is designated an unlawful assembly if the **common object** of the persons composing it is: ‡
- (a) to **overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force**, the Central or any State Government, Parliament or a State Legislature, or any public servant in the exercise of his lawful power;
- (b) to **resist the execution of any law or legal process**;
- (c) to commit any **mischief, criminal trespass or other offence**;
- (d) by criminal force or show of it, to **take or obtain possession of any property**, to deprive any person of the enjoyment of a right of way, water or other incorporeal right, or to **enforce any right or supposed right**;
- (e) by criminal force or show of it, to **compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or to omit what he is legally entitled to do**.

*Explanation:* an assembly not unlawful when it assembled may subsequently become unlawful †.

**Essentials.** (1) **Five or more** persons; (2) a **common object**, being one of the five listed, which is the object of the assembly and may be formed on the spot; (3) an unlawful object, shown by criminal force or show of it, resistance to law, or any offence; courts infer it from conduct, arms carried and language used (*Masalti v. State of U.P.*, AIR 1965 SC 202 †). Mere presence in a crowd does not make a person a member.

**2. Lawful and unlawful gatherings**

| Point | Lawful assembly | Unlawful assembly |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Any number | Five or more |
| Object | Lawful (a meeting, procession, peaceful protest) | One of the five objects in s. 189(1) |
| Means | Peaceful, without arms | Criminal force or show of criminal force |
| Constitutional position | Protected by **Art. 19(1)(b)** (assemble peaceably and without arms) | Not protected; Art. 19(3) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity or public order |
| Police power | Regulation only | May be **dispersed** (BNSS ss. 148–150) †; prohibitory orders (BNSS s. 163) † |
| Conversion | Becomes unlawful if the object changes or an order to disperse is defied | – |

The right in Art. 19(1)(b) is subject to reasonable restrictions: *Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police* (1973) (no right to hold a meeting on a public street without regulation); *Ramlila Maidan Incident, In re* (2012) (peaceful protest is part of the right; proportionate force); *Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police* (2020) (a protest cannot occupy a public way indefinitely) †.

**3. Punishments (BNS s. 189(2)–(9))** ‡

| Sub-section | Offence | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Knowingly joining or continuing as a member of an unlawful assembly | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Joining or continuing after being lawfully commanded to disperse | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Being a member while **armed with a deadly weapon** | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (5) | Joining an assembly of five or more likely to disturb the peace after a lawful order to disperse | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (6)–(9) | **Hiring** persons for an unlawful assembly; harbouring hired persons; being hired; being hired and going armed | Up to 6 months to 2 years, etc. |

**Common object (s. 190; IPC s. 149).** Every member of an unlawful assembly is guilty of an offence committed by any member in prosecution of the common object, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed (see C-05) ‡.

**4. Rioting (BNS s. 191; IPC ss. 146–148).** Whenever **force or violence** is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member thereof, in prosecution of the common object, every member is guilty of **rioting** †. **Punishment:** up to **two years**, or fine, or both (s. 191(2)); **rioting armed** with a deadly weapon: up to **five years**, or fine, or both (s. 191(3)) ‡. *Essentials:* an unlawful assembly; force or violence by the assembly or any member; in prosecution of the common object. **Every riot is an unlawful assembly; not every unlawful assembly is a riot.** Related: wantonly giving provocation with intent to cause riot (s. 192: up to 1 year if riot follows, 6 months if not); liability of the owner or occupier of land who knows of an unlawful assembly or riot and does not give notice (s. 193) ‡.

**5. Affray (BNS s. 194; IPC ss. 159, 160).** "When **two or more persons**, by **fighting in a public place**, **disturb the public peace**, they are said to commit an **affray**." (wording from the IPC †). **Punishment:** up to **one month**, or fine up to **₹1,000**, or both ‡. A fight in private, or one that does not disturb the public peace, is not an affray.

**6. Unlawful assembly, riot and affray compared**

| Point | Unlawful assembly (s. 189) | Rioting (s. 191) | Affray (s. 194) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | **Five or more** | Five or more | **Two or more** |
| Common object | Required | Required | **Not required** |
| Force or violence | Not necessary | **Necessary** | Fighting |
| Place | Anywhere | Anywhere | **Public place** |
| Disturbance of peace | Not necessary | – | **Necessary** |
| Punishment | 6 months (member); 2 years (armed or after dispersal order) | 2 years; 5 years (armed) | **1 month** or ₹1,000 |
| Example | Five persons gather to take possession of a field by show of force | They beat the owner with lathis | Two men fight in a market |

*Sequence:* **assembly** of five or more → **unlawful** if the common object is one of the five → **riot** when force is used → members liable for offences committed in prosecution of the object (s. 190).

**7. Problem.** Eight villagers meet with lathis to stop a surveyor measuring land. When he refuses to leave, they beat him, and one, X, stabs him to death. *Answer:* unlawful assembly (s. 189(1)(b), (c)), armed (s. 189(4)); rioting, armed (s. 191(3)); murder by X (s. 103(1)); the others are liable for murder under s. 190 if the murder was in prosecution of the common object or a member knew it likely (the court looks at the weapons, conduct and whether the knife was visible: *Mizaji* †); otherwise for the beating (hurt, riot); common intention (s. 3(5)) if a prior plan to kill is shown.

**8. Criticism.** The "common object" is wide ("any offence"); mere presence in a crowd may expose an innocent person (*Masalti* asks courts to be careful). Prohibitory orders (BNSS s. 163) are sometimes used to prevent peaceful protest; the Supreme Court has said such orders must be proportionate (*Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India*, (2020) 3 SCC 637 †). Affray is petty; the real violence is dealt with as hurt.

**Conclusion.** An unlawful assembly is five or more persons with one of the five unlawful objects; it becomes a riot when force is used; an affray is a fight in public by two or more persons. A lawful assembly is protected by Art. 19(1)(b) but can become unlawful.

## C-25 · Discuss the offence of bigamy under the BNS. What is the position of adultery after Joseph Shine?

**Asked as:** Set at 4 marks: Dec 2022 Q4 (A-130, "bigamy"). Not yet set at 10 or 20 marks. Related: A-130 to A-135, B-59, B-60.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** bigamy: text, essentials, exceptions 6 · proof of solemnisation and cases 3 · conversion and personal laws 3 · adultery (old law) 2 · *Joseph Shine* 3 · BNS and related offences 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Code protects the institution of marriage by punishing bigamy (BNS s. 82; IPC ss. 494–495). The IPC also punished adultery (s. 497), but the Supreme Court struck it down in 2018 and the BNS omits it ‡.

**A. BIGAMY**

**1. Text (BNS s. 82; IPC ss. 494, 495)** ‡. **s. 82(1):** "Whoever, having a husband or wife living, **marries in any case in which such marriage is void by reason of its taking place during the life of such husband or wife**, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to **seven years**, and shall also be liable to fine." **Exception:** the sub-section does not extend to (i) any person whose marriage with such husband or wife has been **declared void by a court of competent jurisdiction**; or (ii) any person who contracts a marriage during the life of a former husband or wife if such husband or wife, at the time of the subsequent marriage, shall have been **continually absent for seven years and not heard of as being alive**, provided the person contracting the subsequent marriage **informs the person with whom the marriage is contracted of the real state of facts**. **s. 82(2):** bigamy **with concealment** of the former marriage from the second spouse: imprisonment up to **ten years** and fine.

**2. Essentials.** (1) The accused has a **husband or wife living** (a valid first marriage subsisting); (2) he **marries again**, and the second marriage is **solemnised**; (3) the second marriage is **void by reason of the life of the first spouse** (under the personal law: Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, ss. 5(i), 11 †); (4) the case does not fall within the Exceptions. For s. 82(2): concealment of the first marriage.

**3. Proof of solemnisation.** The second marriage must be proved to have been performed with the **essential ceremonies**. In ***Bhaurao Shankar Lokhande v. State of Maharashtra*** (AIR 1965 SC 1564) the Supreme Court held that "solemnised" means celebrated with proper ceremonies and in due form; under Hindu law, essential ceremonies such as the *saptapadi* or *datta homa* (as custom requires) must be proved; mere admission is not enough; the conviction was set aside. ***Kanwal Ram v. Himachal Pradesh Administration*** (AIR 1966 SC 614) applies the same rule †.

**4. Conversion and personal laws**
- ***Sarla Mudgal v. Union of India*** (1995) 3 SCC 635. Hindu husbands converted to Islam in order to marry again without dissolving their first marriage. The Supreme Court held that the **first Hindu marriage does not dissolve** by the husband's conversion; it can be dissolved only under the Hindu Marriage Act; the second marriage is therefore void and the husband is guilty of bigamy. The Court called for a **Uniform Civil Code** under Art. 44 †. ***Lily Thomas v. Union of India*** (2000) 6 SCC 224 affirmed the rule †.
- A Muslim man may lawfully have up to four wives, so a second marriage by him is not void and not bigamy (as I understand it †). Christians and Parsis are bound by monogamy by statute (Indian Christian Marriage Act, 1872; Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936); the Special Marriage Act, 1954, s. 44 punishes bigamy by those married under it †. Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, s. 17 makes a second marriage during the first spouse's life void and punishable under the bigamy section †.
- The first spouse's consent is no defence; not a continuing offence (complete on solemnisation) †.

**5. Illustrations.** (a) A, a Hindu with a living wife, secretly marries B in a temple with all ceremonies, telling B he is unmarried: bigamy with concealment, s. 82(2), up to ten years. (b) A's wife left eight years ago and has not been heard of since; A tells C of this and marries her: within the Exception. (c) A converts to Islam and marries B while his first wife is alive and undivorced: bigamy (*Sarla Mudgal*). (d) The prosecution shows only that A and B lived as husband and wife: the second marriage is not proved solemnised; acquittal (*Bhaurao Lokhande*).

**B. ADULTERY**

**6. The old law (IPC s. 497)** †. "Whoever has sexual intercourse with a person who is and whom he knows or has reason to believe to be the wife of another man, without the consent or connivance of that man, such sexual intercourse not amounting to the offence of rape, is guilty of the offence of adultery, and shall be punished with imprisonment up to five years, or fine, or both. In such case the wife shall not be punishable as an abettor." *Features:* only a **man** could be the offender; the **wife** was never punishable; the **husband's consent or connivance** removed the offence; only the **husband** could complain (Cr.P.C. s. 198(2)).

**7. Earlier decisions upholding s. 497** †: *Yusuf Abdul Aziz v. State of Bombay* (AIR 1954 SC 321) (Art. 15(3) permits special provisions for women); *Sowmithri Vishnu v. Union of India* (1985) Supp SCC 137; *V. Revathi v. Union of India* (1988) 2 SCC 72.

**8. *Joseph Shine v. Union of India*** ((2019) 3 SCC 39, decided 27 September 2018; Constitution Bench of five judges) †. Joseph Shine, an Indian residing in Italy, challenged s. 497 IPC and s. 198(2) Cr.P.C. in a public interest petition. **Held:** both are **unconstitutional**. (i) The section treated a woman as the **property of her husband** (his consent removed the offence), which offends **dignity** under Art. 21; (ii) it was **discriminatory** (Art. 14) and rested on gender stereotypes (Art. 15); (iii) it denied a woman **autonomy and sexual privacy** (Art. 21); (iv) it punished a private moral wrong in a way not justified by any public interest; (v) Art. 15(3) cannot justify a provision that perpetuates stereotypes. *Yusuf Abdul Aziz*, *Sowmithri Vishnu* and *Revathi* were overruled. Adultery remains a **ground for divorce** under personal laws (Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, s. 13(1)(i)) but is **no longer a crime**.

**9. The BNS and adultery.** The BNS has **no** offence of adultery ‡. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, in its report on the Bill in November 2023, recommended restoring adultery as a **gender-neutral** offence; the Government did not accept it (from memory †). *For criminalising:* protects the sanctity of marriage; deters. *Against:* a matter of private morality and autonomy; the criminal law is a blunt instrument; the civil remedy of divorce suffices; *Joseph Shine*.

**10. Related offences** ‡ (headings; terms from the IPC †). Cohabitation caused by a man deceitfully inducing a belief of lawful marriage (s. 81; IPC s. 493: up to 10 years); marriage ceremony fraudulently gone through (s. 83; IPC s. 496: up to 7 years); enticing or detaining a married woman with criminal intent (s. 84; IPC s. 498); cruelty by husband or relatives (ss. 85–86: up to 3 years); sexual intercourse by deceitful means, including a false promise of marriage (s. 69, new). On false promises, *Pramod Suryabhan Pawar v. State of Maharashtra* (2019) 9 SCC 608 distinguished a **false promise** (no intention to marry from the start: consent vitiated) from a **breach of a promise** (a genuine promise later broken: not rape) †.

**11. Criticism.** Bigamy law is a patchwork of personal laws: a Hindu, Christian or Parsi man who marries a second wife is punished, a Muslim man generally is not; the Supreme Court has repeatedly asked for a Uniform Civil Code (*Sarla Mudgal*; *John Vallamattom v. Union of India*, (2003) 6 SCC 611 †) while the legislature has not acted. The strict proof of ceremonies lets many bigamists escape. Whether the State should punish consensual adultery at all is debated.

**Conclusion.** Bigamy is punished with up to seven years, or ten years if the first marriage is concealed, and requires proof that the second marriage was solemnised; conversion does not avoid it. Adultery, once an offence only for the man and only at the husband's instance, was struck down in *Joseph Shine* and is not in the BNS, though it remains a ground for divorce.
