## C-15 · Define kidnapping and abduction. Distinguish between them. Is abduction punishable by itself?

**Asked as:** Dec 2025 Q12 (C, "kidnapping; v. abduction; is abduction not punishable?"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2024 Q8 (B-43), Dec 2023 Q8 (B-43, "abduction v. kidnapping"). Related: A-91 to A-94, B-43, B-44.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** kinds of kidnapping (s. 137) 4 · essentials of kidnapping from guardianship and cases 5 · abduction (s. 138) 3 · comparison table 4 · punishment and aggravated forms 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** Kidnapping and abduction are offences against the person's liberty and against the guardian's right to custody. The BNS defines kidnapping in **s. 137** (IPC ss. 359–361) and abduction in **s. 138** (IPC s. 362) ‡. A person may be kidnapped or abducted, and the two sometimes overlap, but they are different offences.

**1. Kidnapping (BNS s. 137(1); IPC ss. 359–361).** Kidnapping is of **two kinds**: kidnapping **from India**, and kidnapping **from lawful guardianship** ‡.
- **Kidnapping from India** (s. 137(1)(a); IPC s. 360): whoever **conveys any person beyond the limits of India** without the consent of that person, or of some person legally authorised to consent on his behalf.
- **Kidnapping from lawful guardianship** (s. 137(1)(b); IPC s. 361): whoever **takes or entices any child or any person of unsound mind** out of the keeping of the **lawful guardian** of such child or person, without the consent of the guardian. *Explanation:* "lawful guardian" includes any person lawfully entrusted with the care or custody. *Exception:* a person who in good faith believes himself to be the father of an illegitimate child, or entitled to its lawful custody, is not covered, unless the act is for an immoral or unlawful purpose.
- **Age.** The IPC required a male under sixteen or a female under eighteen. The BNS says "child", which means a person under **eighteen** (s. 2(3)) ‡, so the age is now eighteen for both sexes (my reading of the two provisions; I found no case on the point).
- **Punishment (s. 137(2)):** imprisonment of either description up to **seven years**, and fine ‡.

**2. Essentials of kidnapping from lawful guardianship**
1. **Taking or enticing.** *Taking* means causing the child to go with the accused, by force or persuasion, and the accused must have played an **active part**; *enticing* means inducing the child by **exciting hope or desire** (*State of Haryana v. Raja Ram*, (1973) 1 SCC 544 †). The **consent of the child is immaterial**.
2. **A child or a person of unsound mind.**
3. **Out of the keeping of the lawful guardian**: the guardian must have care and custody, not necessarily physical presence.
4. **Without the guardian's consent.**

**Cases** (from memory †)
- ***S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras*** (AIR 1965 SC 942). A minor girl left her father's house of her own accord and met the accused, and went with him to register a marriage. The Supreme Court distinguished **taking** from **allowing a minor to accompany**: where the girl leaves of her own accord and the accused takes no active part in her leaving, there is no "taking"; some **active participation** by inducement or force is needed. The accused was acquitted.
- ***State of Haryana v. Raja Ram*** (1973): the object of the section is to protect minors and the **rights of guardians**; the words "takes or entices" are wider than "allowing to accompany"; persuasion that overcomes the child's reluctance, or influence that brings about her leaving, is enough; the girl's consent is no defence.
- ***Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat*** (1973) 2 SCC 413: enticement is an act of inducement; the consent of the minor girl is immaterial; the accused who induced a minor to leave her parents' custody was guilty.

**3. Abduction (BNS s. 138; IPC s. 362).** "Whoever **by force compels**, or **by any deceitful means induces**, any person to **go from any place**, is said to **abduct** that person." ‡
1. **Any person**, of any age.
2. By **force** or **deceitful means**. If the person goes of his own free will, there is no abduction.
3. The person goes **from a place**: movement is essential.
4. **Abduction is not by itself an offence.** Section 138 defines it but prescribes **no punishment**. It is punishable only when done with a particular intent: for example, to compel a woman's marriage (BNS s. 87; IPC s. 366), to murder (s. 140(1); IPC s. 364), for ransom (s. 140(2); IPC s. 364A), to secretly and wrongfully confine, or to subject to grievous hurt, slavery or unnatural lust (other sub-sections of s. 140; IPC ss. 365, 367) ‡ (ss. 87 and 140(1)–(2) read; the other sub-sections †).
5. Abduction is a **continuing** offence, since it continues as long as the abducted person is detained; kidnapping from guardianship is complete when the child is taken (the standard textbook view †).

**4. Kidnapping and abduction distinguished**

| Point | Kidnapping | Abduction |
|---|---|---|
| Section | BNS s. 137 (IPC 359–361) | BNS s. 138 (IPC 362) |
| Victim | From India: any person. From guardianship: a **child (under 18)** or a **person of unsound mind** | **Any person**, of any age |
| Means | **Taking or enticing**; no force necessary | **Force** or **deceitful means** |
| Consent | The **guardian's consent** matters; the child's consent is immaterial | The person's **free consent** removes the offence |
| Guardian | Out of the keeping of the **lawful guardian** | Guardianship is irrelevant |
| Intention | Not necessary | Specific intent needed to make it **punishable** |
| Punishable by itself? | **Yes**: up to 7 years and fine (s. 137(2)) | **No**: only with a specified intention |
| Nature | Not continuing | Continuing |
| Place | From India or from guardianship | From any place |

**Overlap.** If A, by deceit, induces a 15-year-old girl to leave her father's house, A has *kidnapped* her (enticing out of the guardian's keeping) and *abducted* her (deceitful means). If A forces an adult woman to go from her house, A has abducted her; there is no kidnapping, since she is not a child.

**5. Aggravated forms** (†, except where marked ‡)
- Kidnapping or maiming a child for begging (s. 139; IPC s. 363A).
- Kidnapping or abducting to **murder** (s. 140(1); IPC s. 364): life, or rigorous imprisonment up to ten years, and fine ‡.
- Kidnapping or abduction for **ransom** (s. 140(2); IPC s. 364A): **death or imprisonment for life**, and fine ‡; upheld in *Vikram Singh v. Union of India* (2015) 9 SCC 502 †.
- Kidnapping a woman to compel marriage (s. 87; IPC s. 366); importation of a girl or boy (s. 141; IPC s. 366B); wrongfully concealing a kidnapped person (s. 142; IPC s. 368).
- **Trafficking of person** (s. 143; IPC s. 370): recruiting, transporting or receiving a person by threats, force, fraud or inducement for exploitation; rigorous imprisonment of **seven to ten years** and fine for the basic offence, with higher tiers for children and several victims ‡ (higher tiers not checked).

**6. Illustrations**
1. A takes B, a 16-year-old girl, from her father's house by promising to marry her, without the father's consent. This is kidnapping (taking from lawful guardianship) and, because of the deceit, also abduction.
2. A, an adult, is forcibly pushed into a car by B and driven away. This is abduction by force, but punishable only if done for a purpose within the Code (for example, ransom).
3. A girl of 17 leaves her father's house on her own accord, A takes no part in her leaving and later marries her. Under *Varadarajan* A has not "taken" her; he is not guilty of kidnapping.

**Conclusion.** Kidnapping is an offence in itself and protects the guardian's custody of a child or person of unsound mind, or a person's liberty from being conveyed out of India. Abduction is a means by which a person is moved, by force or deceit, and is punishable only when done with one of the intents mentioned in the Code. Abduction without any such intent is not punishable as such, but the same facts may amount to wrongful confinement or kidnapping.

## C-16 · Distinguish between wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement. Explain force, criminal force and assault

**Asked as:** Dec 2023 Q13(b) (C, "restraint v. confinement"). Set at 10 marks: Dec 2025 Q8 (B-41). Set at 4 marks: Dec 2022 Q2 (A-89, "criminal force"). Related: A-86 to A-90, B-41, B-42.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** wrongful restraint 4 · wrongful confinement 4 · comparison table 4 · force, criminal force and assault 6 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Offences against personal liberty and bodily integrity short of hurt are in BNS ss. 126–136 (IPC ss. 339–358): wrongful restraint (s. 126), wrongful confinement (s. 127), force (s. 128), criminal force (s. 129), assault (s. 130) and their punishments ‡.

**1. Wrongful restraint (BNS s. 126; IPC ss. 339–341).** "Whoever voluntarily **obstructs** any person so as to **prevent** that person from **proceeding in any direction in which that person has a right to proceed**, is said wrongfully to restrain that person." ‡ *Exception:* obstructing a private way over land or water which a person in good faith believes he has a lawful right to obstruct is not an offence. *Illustration:* A obstructs a path along which Z has a right to pass, not believing in good faith that he has a right to stop it. A wrongfully restrains Z.
- **Essentials.** (i) Voluntary obstruction; (ii) the obstruction **prevents** the person from proceeding in **a direction** in which he has a right to proceed; it is enough that he is prevented in one direction though free to go in another; (iii) a **real** prevention (mere inconvenience is not enough); (iv) the person has a **right** to proceed.
- **Punishment (s. 126(2)):** simple imprisonment up to **one month**, or fine up to **₹5,000**, or both ‡.

**2. Wrongful confinement (BNS s. 127; IPC s. 340).** "Whoever **wrongfully restrains** any person in such a manner as to **prevent that person from proceeding beyond certain circumscribing limits**, is said wrongfully to confine that person." ‡ *Illustrations:* A causes Z to go within a walled space and locks him in; A places men with firearms at the outlets of a building and tells Z they will fire if he attempts to leave.
- **Essentials.** (i) Wrongful restraint; (ii) **total** prevention of movement beyond **circumscribing limits** (a room, an island, a town; but a person free to leave by any route is not confined); (iii) without lawful authority; (iv) by force, locking, threats or show of force.
- **Punishment ‡:** s. 127(2): up to **one year**, or fine up to ₹5,000, or both; s. 127(3), confined for **three days or more**: up to **three years**, or fine up to ₹10,000, or both; s. 127(4), confined for **ten days or more**: up to **five years** and fine not less than ₹10,000. Aggravated forms (confinement knowing that a writ for liberation has issued; in secret; to extort property or compel an illegal act; to extort a confession) carry heavier punishment (IPC ss. 345–348; BNS terms †).
- *Bird v. Jones* (1845) 7 QB 742: a part of a public bridge was fenced off; the plaintiff, stopped from proceeding but free to go back, was not imprisoned; the classic case on the difference between partial obstruction and total confinement †.

**3. Wrongful restraint and wrongful confinement compared**

| Point | Wrongful restraint (s. 126) | Wrongful confinement (s. 127) |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | **Genus** | **Species**: an aggravated form of restraint |
| Prevention | From proceeding in **some direction**; other directions are free | From proceeding in **all directions** beyond circumscribing limits |
| Limits | None | Circumscribing limits are essential |
| Obstruction | Partial | Complete |
| Punishment | Up to 1 month, or ₹5,000, or both | Up to 1 year, or ₹5,000, or both; more if three days or ten days or more |
| Aggravated forms | None | Many |
| Example | A blocks the road so Z's car cannot go on; Z can go back | A locks Z in a room |

Every confinement involves a restraint; not every restraint is a confinement.

**4. Force (BNS s. 128; IPC s. 349).** A person uses **force** to another if he causes **motion, change of motion or cessation of motion** to that other, or to any substance in such a way as to bring it into contact with that other's body or clothes or with anything so situated that the contact affects that other's sense of feeling, by (i) his own bodily power; (ii) disposing a substance so that the motion occurs without any further act; or (iii) inducing any animal to move or stop †.

**5. Criminal force (BNS s. 129; IPC s. 350).** Whoever **intentionally uses force** to any person, **without that person's consent**, in order to the committing of any offence, or intending by the use of such force to cause, or knowing it to be likely that by the use of such force he will cause, **injury, fear or annoyance** to the person to whom the force is used †. *Illustrations:* A unfastens the moorings of a boat in which Z is sitting, so that the boat drifts down the stream (force by disposing substances; criminal force if without consent and intending or knowing annoyance); A lashes the horses of Z's chariot (force by inducing animals).

**6. Assault (BNS s. 130; IPC s. 351).** Whoever makes any **gesture or any preparation**, intending or knowing it to be likely that it will cause any person **present** to **apprehend** that he who makes it is **about to use criminal force** to that person, is said to commit an **assault**. *Explanation:* **mere words do not amount to an assault**, but words may give to gestures or preparation such a meaning as to make them an assault †. *Illustrations:* A shakes his fist at Z, intending Z to believe A is about to strike him: assault. A begins to unloose the muzzle of a ferocious dog, intending Z to believe he is about to set the dog on him: assault.

**7. Punishment (BNS s. 131; IPC s. 352).** Assault or criminal force otherwise than on grave and sudden provocation given by the person assaulted: imprisonment up to **three months**, or fine up to **₹1,000**, or both ‡. Aggravated forms: to deter a public servant (s. 132; IPC s. 353); to outrage the modesty of a woman (s. 74; IPC s. 354); to disrobe a woman (s. 76; IPC s. 354B); with intent to dishonour (s. 133; IPC s. 355); in an attempt to commit theft of property carried (s. 134; IPC s. 356); in attempting wrongfully to confine (s. 135; IPC s. 357); on grave and sudden provocation (s. 136; IPC s. 358) † (BNS numbers worked from the IPC order).

**8. Force, criminal force and assault compared**

| Point | Force | Criminal force | Assault |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Causing motion, change or cessation of motion | **Intentional** force **without consent**, with an offence, or intending (or knowing) injury, fear or annoyance | A **gesture or preparation** causing **apprehension** of criminal force |
| Contact | Needed | Needed | **Not needed** |
| Punishable by itself | No | Yes (s. 131) | Yes (s. 131) |
| Words | – | – | Words alone not enough; they may explain a gesture |
| Victim | – | – | Must be **present** and apprehend |

**Assault is the threat; criminal force is the application.** An assault can occur without any force (shaking a fist); criminal force can occur without any earlier assault (a push from behind).

**9. Relationship with hurt.** Hurt (s. 114) requires bodily pain, disease or infirmity; criminal force need not cause pain (spitting at a man is criminal force). English authorities: *Stephens v. Myers* (1830): a threat with a clenched fist across a table was an assault only if the person had the present ability to carry it out; *R v. Ireland* (1998): silent telephone calls may amount to assault †.

**Conclusion.** Wrongful restraint is a partial obstruction of movement, and wrongful confinement a total one within circumscribing limits; confinement is an aggravated form of restraint. Force is the basic concept; criminal force is its wrongful use; assault is the threat of criminal force. The BNS has raised the fines (₹5,000 and ₹1,000) and introduced higher tiers of confinement by duration.

## C-17 · Dowry death (BNS s. 80) compared with the IPC; cruelty by husband or relatives and stalking

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q11 (C, "dowry death: BNS v. IPC"). Set at 4 marks: Dec 2025 Q2 (A-95, "dowry death"), Dec 2024 Q5 (A-97, "stalking"). Related: A-95 to A-97, B-45, B-46.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** dowry death: text and essentials 6 · presumption and cases 4 · BNS v. IPC 3 · cruelty (ss. 85–86) 3 · stalking (s. 78) 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 makes giving and taking dowry an offence. The IPC was amended to add **s. 498A (cruelty by husband or relatives)** in 1983, **s. 304B (dowry death)** in 1986 and **s. 354D (stalking)** in 2013 †. The BNS carries these as **s. 80**, **ss. 85–86** and **s. 78** respectively ‡.

**1. Dowry death: BNS s. 80 (IPC s. 304B)**

*Text (s. 80(1)).* Where the **death of a woman is caused by any burns or bodily injury or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances** within **seven years of her marriage**, and it is shown that **soon before her death** she was **subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or any relative of her husband for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry**, such death shall be called "**dowry death**", and such husband or relative shall be deemed to have caused her death. *Explanation:* "dowry" has the same meaning as in section 2 of the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 ‡.

*Punishment (s. 80(2)):* imprisonment for a term **not less than seven years**, which may extend to **imprisonment for life** ‡.

*Essentials.* (1) A woman's death by burns or bodily injury, or otherwise than under normal circumstances (suicide, poisoning, hanging and drowning qualify); (2) within **seven years of marriage**; (3) **soon before** her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment; (4) by her **husband or his relative**; (5) in connection with a **demand for dowry**.

*Presumption.* Under s. 113B of the Evidence Act, 1872 (now the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 118 †), where it is shown that soon before her death the woman was subjected by the accused to cruelty or harassment for or in connection with a dowry demand, the court **shall presume** that the accused caused the dowry death. The presumption is **mandatory** ("shall") and shifts the burden to the accused to rebut it.

*Cases* (from memory †)
- ***Kans Raj v. State of Punjab*** (2000) 5 SCC 207: "soon before" has no fixed time limit; there must be a **proximate and live link** between the cruelty and the death; the Court examined the conduct of the husband's family on the evidence.
- ***Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana*** (2021) 6 SCC 1: the Supreme Court discussed the meaning of "soon before", the burden on the accused, and the framing of charges under s. 304B IPC with an alternative charge under s. 302; the presumption operates once the foundational facts are proved.

**2. Dowry death: BNS and IPC compared**

| Point | IPC s. 304B | BNS s. 80 |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Chapter XVI (offences affecting the human body) | Chapter V, Of Offences Against Woman and Child ‡ |
| Definition | Death by burns or bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of marriage; soon before death subjected to cruelty or harassment for dowry | Substantially the **same** ‡ |
| Meaning of dowry | Section 2, Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 | The same |
| Punishment | Not less than 7 years, may extend to life | The same ‡ |
| Presumption | Evidence Act s. 113B | BSA, 2023, s. 118 † |
| Related presumption (suicide) | Evidence Act s. 113A | BSA s. 117 † |

The BNS has changed the number, the chapter and the source of the presumption, but not the elements or the punishment. Cases decided under s. 304B IPC therefore continue to guide the courts.

**3. Dowry death compared with murder and abetment of suicide**

| Point | Dowry death (s. 80) | Murder (s. 103) | Abetment of suicide (s. 108) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Victim | A married woman | Any person | Any person |
| Cause of death | Burns, bodily injury, or other than normal | Intentional or knowing killing | Suicide |
| Time limit | Within **7 years** of marriage | None | None |
| Presumption | Yes (BSA s. 118) | No | Evidence Act s. 113A / BSA s. 117 within 7 years of marriage |
| Minimum punishment | **7 years** | Life (or death) | None; up to 10 years |

**4. Cruelty by husband or relatives: BNS ss. 85–86 (IPC s. 498A)** ‡. Whoever, being the **husband or the relative of the husband** of a woman, subjects her to **cruelty** is punished with imprisonment up to **three years** and fine (s. 85). Section 86 defines **cruelty**: (a) any wilful conduct likely to drive the woman to commit suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to her life, limb or health, mental or physical; or (b) harassment of the woman with a view to coercing her or her relatives to meet any unlawful demand for property or valuable security, or on account of failure to meet such demand †. The Supreme Court has warned against misuse: in *Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar* (2014) 8 SCC 273 it directed that there be no automatic arrest and that the police follow the statutory checklist †. *Rajesh Sharma v. State of U.P.* (2017) 10 SCC 472 directed family welfare committees; the directions were modified in *Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 443 †.

**5. Stalking: BNS s. 78 (IPC s. 354D)** ‡. A **man** commits stalking who (1) **follows a woman** and **contacts, or attempts to contact**, such woman to foster personal interaction **repeatedly**, despite a **clear indication of disinterest** by her; or (2) **monitors the use by a woman of the internet, email or any other form of electronic communication**.
- **Provisos:** it is not stalking if the man proves that it was pursued to prevent or detect crime by a person entrusted with that responsibility by the State; under any law, or to comply with a condition imposed under law; or that in the particular circumstances the conduct was reasonable and justified.
- **Punishment (s. 78(2)):** first conviction up to **three years** and fine; second or subsequent conviction up to **five years** and fine ‡.
- **Notes.** Inserted into the IPC (s. 354D) by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, after the Nirbhaya case and the J.S. Verma Committee report †. The offence is **gender-specific**: the offender must be a man and the victim a woman. **Cyberstalking** is covered by clause (2). The key words are "repeatedly" and "clear indication of disinterest". Critics say the section leaves out stalking of men and by women, and that "foster personal interaction" is vague.

**6. Criticism.** Dowry deaths remain common, yet convictions are low because proof of "soon before" and of "demand for dowry" is difficult; courts therefore rely on the presumption. Section 498A is often alleged to be misused, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against automatic arrests.

**Conclusion.** Dowry death is a statutory offence created for a social evil: the BNS retains it, with a minimum of seven years, a mandatory presumption, and the same elements as the IPC. Cruelty and stalking are linked offences protecting women within and outside marriage.

## C-18 · Discuss the offences against children under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q13(b) (C, "offences against child under BNS"). Related: A-98 to A-101, B-47.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** meaning of "child" and approach 3 · BNS ss. 93–99 (headings and content) 6 · other BNS provisions touching children 5 · special laws and Constitution 3 · cases and criticism 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** The BNS defines **"child" as a person under eighteen years** (s. 2(3)) ‡, a uniform age for both sexes in place of the IPC's mixed ages. Offences against children are found in several places in the Sanhita (Chapter V, "Of offences against woman and child") and in special statutes, above all the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (POCSO).

**1. Offences in BNS ss. 93–99** (headings read ‡; the punishments below are from the IPC and **not checked** against the BNS, which changed several terms †)

| BNS | IPC | Offence | IPC punishment † |
|---|---|---|---|
| s. 93 | 317 | **Exposure and abandonment** of a child under twelve years by the parent or person having care of it | Up to 7 years, or fine, or both |
| s. 94 | 318 | **Concealment of birth** by secret disposal of the dead body of a child | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| s. 95 | – (new) | **Hiring, employing or engaging a child to commit an offence** | – |
| s. 96 | 366A | **Procuration of a child** | Up to 10 years and fine |
| s. 97 | 369 | **Kidnapping or abducting a child under ten** for the purpose of stealing from the child's person | Up to 7 years and fine |
| s. 98 | 372 | **Selling a child for prostitution** | Up to 10 years and fine |
| s. 99 | 373 | **Buying a child for prostitution** | Up to 10 years and fine |

**2. Other BNS provisions touching children**
1. **Kidnapping a child** from lawful guardianship (s. 137(1)(b)): now for both sexes under eighteen; up to seven years and fine ‡.
2. **Abetment of suicide of a child** (s. 107): death, imprisonment for life, or up to ten years, and fine ‡.
3. **Kidnapping or maiming a child for begging** (s. 139); **kidnapping for ransom** (s. 140(2): death or life) ‡; **importation of a girl or boy** (s. 141); **trafficking** (s. 143, higher tiers where children are trafficked) †.
4. **Sexual offences against children.** Mainly under POCSO; in the BNS, rape of a woman under sixteen or twelve attracts higher punishments than ordinary rape (s. 65 †), and gang rape of a girl under eighteen or twelve attracts heavier terms including death in some cases (s. 70 †). These sections were not checked.
5. **Acid attack, hurt and cruelty** where the victim is a child are punished under the general provisions, with the child's age as an aggravating circumstance.

**2A. Consent and capacity of a child.** A child under twelve cannot give valid consent (BNS s. 28; IPC s. 90), and in the sexual-offence provisions the BNS treats a girl under eighteen as unable to consent (s. 63, sixth description of rape) and, in the exception to rape, protects a wife under eighteen (Exception 2 to s. 63) †, in line with *Independent Thought* below. On the other side, a child under seven is incapable of committing an offence and a child of seven to twelve may be, if mature (ss. 20, 21; Unit 3).

**3. Special laws and the Constitution** †
- **POCSO Act, 2012**: penetrative and aggravated penetrative sexual assault, sexual assault, sexual harassment and use of a child for pornography, with special courts and child-friendly procedure.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015**: cruelty to a child (s. 75) and children in need of care and protection; also the treatment of children in conflict with law.
- **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**, as amended in 2016; **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006**; **Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009**.
- **Constitution:** Articles 15(3), 21A, 23, 24, 39(e) and (f), 45 and 51A(k).

**4. Supreme Court** (from memory †)
- ***Independent Thought v. Union of India*** (2017) 10 SCC 800: the exception to the offence of rape (sexual intercourse by a man with his wife) was read down so that intercourse with a wife aged fifteen to eighteen is rape.
- ***Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India*** (2011) 5 SCC 1: child trafficking, including trafficking for circuses; directions to the Government.

**5. Illustrations**

**6. Criticism.** The protection is spread across the BNS and special laws, which creates overlap and different age limits (twelve, sixteen, eighteen). The BNS has simplified one of the age problems by defining "child" as under eighteen, but the General Exceptions still use seven and twelve for criminal capacity (BNS ss. 20, 21).

**7. Current position.** I did not check the punishments in ss. 93–99 of the BNS, nor any decision since 1 July 2024 on these sections. The student should verify them with the current Act.

**Conclusion.** The BNS strengthens the protection of children by a single age of eighteen and new offences such as hiring a child to commit an offence; the heavier protection against sexual offences lies in POCSO and in the sexual-offence provisions of the BNS.
