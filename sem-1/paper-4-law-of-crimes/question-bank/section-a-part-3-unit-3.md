**A-46 · Mistake of fact** · *Asked: Dec 2022 Q3* · *Related: B-25, C-11*

BNS ss. 14 and 17 (IPC ss. 76, 79): nothing is an offence done by a person who, by reason of a mistake of fact and not of law, in good faith believes himself bound by law (s. 14), or justified by law (s. 17), to do it †. Maxim: *ignorantia facti excusat*. Example: a soldier fires on a mob by lawful order. Good faith means due care and attention (s. 2(11)) ‡.

*Word count: 71*

<a id="a-47"></a>
**A-47 · Mistake of law** · *Related: B-25, C-11*

Mistake of law is no defence: *ignorantia juris non excusat*. Everyone is presumed to know the law, and the General Exceptions protect only a good-faith mistake of fact (BNS ss. 14, 17). A person who believes that the law allows what it forbids is guilty. A mistake about a private right may amount to a mistake of fact, negativing dishonest intention †.

*Word count: 61*

<a id="a-48"></a>
**A-48 · Judicial acts** · *Related: B-25, C-11*

BNS s. 15 (IPC s. 77): nothing is an offence done by a Judge acting judicially in the exercise of a power given to him by law, or which he in good faith believes is given to him. BNS s. 16 (IPC s. 78): acts done under a court's judgment or order are protected even if the court lacked jurisdiction, if the actor in good faith believes it had jurisdiction †.

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-49"></a>
**A-49 · Accident** · *Related: B-26, C-11*

BNS s. 18 (IPC s. 80): nothing is an offence done by accident or misfortune, without criminal intention or knowledge, in the doing of a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means with proper care and caution †. Example: the head of a hatchet flies off and kills a bystander, with no want of care. If care was lacking, s. 106 (negligence) applies.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-50"></a>
**A-50 · Necessity** · *Related: B-26, C-11*

BNS s. 19 (IPC s. 81): an act known to be likely to cause harm is not an offence if done without criminal intention to cause harm, in good faith, to prevent or avoid other harm to person or property †. Whether the harm avoided justifies the risk is a question of fact. Necessity is no defence to murder (*R v. Dudley and Stephens*, 1884) †.

*Word count: 64*

<a id="a-51"></a>
**A-51 · Child under seven** · *Related: B-27, C-11*

BNS s. 20 (IPC s. 82): nothing is an offence done by a child under seven years of age. The presumption is conclusive (*doli incapax*): the child is incapable of crime, whatever his understanding. Any child under 18 in conflict with the law is dealt with under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 †.

*Word count: 57*

<a id="a-52"></a>
**A-52 · Child between seven and twelve** · *Related: B-27, C-11*

BNS s. 21 (IPC s. 83): nothing is an offence done by a child above seven and under twelve who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct on that occasion †. The presumption of incapacity is rebuttable: the prosecution must show his maturity. The Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 applies to him as to any child under 18.

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-53"></a>
**A-53 · Unsoundness of mind** · *Related: B-28, C-10*

BNS s. 22 (IPC s. 84): nothing is an offence done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is wrong or contrary to law †. The burden is on the accused, on a preponderance of probability (*Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat*, 1964 †).

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-54"></a>
**A-54 · M'Naghten rules** · *Related: B-28, C-10*

*M'Naghten's Case* (1843): every man is presumed sane; to establish insanity it must be clearly proved that, at the time of the act, the accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act, or that it was wrong. Section 22 follows these rules but says "unsoundness of mind" and adds "contrary to law" †.

*Word count: 69*

<a id="a-55"></a>
**A-55 · Legal and medical insanity** · *Asked: Dec 2024 Q1* · *Related: B-28, C-10*

Legal insanity is cognitive incapacity at the time of the act, by reason of unsoundness of mind, to know its nature or that it is wrong or contrary to law (BNS s. 22): it is a defence. Medical insanity is any mental disorder recognised by psychiatry: it is not a defence by itself. Only legal insanity excuses (*Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.*, 2008 †).

*Word count: 65*

<a id="a-56"></a>
**A-56 · Involuntary intoxication** · *Related: B-29, C-10*

BNS s. 23 (IPC s. 85): nothing is an offence done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of intoxication caused by a thing administered to him without his knowledge or against his will, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that it is wrong or contrary to law †. He is acquitted, as a person who is not responsible.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-57"></a>
**A-57 · Voluntary intoxication** · *Related: B-29, C-10*

BNS s. 24 (IPC s. 86): a person who acts in a state of voluntary intoxication is presumed to have the same knowledge as if he were sober. Where intention is an element, the court considers whether he in fact formed it; if the thing was given without his knowledge or against his will, intention is not presumed †. Drunkenness is no excuse in itself (*Basdev v. State of PEPSU*, 1956 †).

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-58"></a>
**A-58 · Consent** · *Related: B-30, C-11*

Harm caused with consent is not an offence, within limits (BNS ss. 25–31; IPC ss. 87–93). An act not intended, and not known to be likely, to cause death or grievous hurt, done with the consent of a person over eighteen, is excused (s. 25). Consent is invalid if given under fear or misconception, by a person of unsound mind or intoxicated, or by a child under twelve (s. 28) †.

*Word count: 70*

<a id="a-59"></a>
**A-59 · Act done in good faith for a person's benefit** · *Related: B-30, C-11*

BNS s. 26 (IPC s. 88): an act not intended to cause death, done in good faith for a person's benefit with his consent, is not an offence even if it causes harm; a surgeon operating with consent is the standard example. Section 30 (IPC s. 92) protects such an act without consent where consent cannot be obtained in time †.

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-60"></a>
**A-60 · Compulsion (duress)** · *Related: C-11*

BNS s. 32 (IPC s. 94): except murder and offences against the State punishable with death, nothing is an offence done by a person compelled by threats which reasonably cause the apprehension that instant death will otherwise be the consequence, provided he did not of his own accord, or from reasonable apprehension of harm short of instant death, place himself in that situation †.

*Word count: 63*

<a id="a-61"></a>
**A-61 · Acts causing slight harm (trifles)** · *Related: C-11*

BNS s. 33 (IPC s. 95): nothing is an offence by reason that it causes, or is intended or known to be likely to cause, harm so slight that no person of ordinary sense and temper would complain of it †. Maxim: *de minimis non curat lex*. Examples: a pat on the back, a push in a crowd. The test is objective and depends on the facts.

*Word count: 66*

<a id="a-62"></a>
**A-62 · Right of private defence** · *Related: B-31, C-08*

BNS s. 34 (IPC s. 96): nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private defence. Section 35 gives every person the right to defend his own body and property, and those of any other person, against specified offences, subject to s. 37. The right is preventive, not punitive †.

*Word count: 55*

<a id="a-63"></a>
**A-63 · Restrictions on private defence** · *Related: B-31, C-08*

BNS s. 37 (IPC s. 99): there is no right of private defence against an act of a public servant acting in good faith under colour of his office, nor against an act done on his direction; nor where there is time to have recourse to the public authorities; and the right never extends to the infliction of more harm than necessary †.

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-64"></a>
**A-64 · Private defence of the body extending to death** · *Related: B-32, C-08*

BNS s. 38 (IPC s. 100): the right extends to causing death if the assault causes reasonable apprehension of death or of grievous hurt, or is made with intention to commit rape, to gratify unnatural lust (IPC wording †), to kidnap or abduct, or to confine wrongfully without recourse to the authorities; and acid attack ‡. Otherwise only harm short of death (s. 39).

*Word count: 62*

<a id="a-65"></a>
**A-65 · Private defence of property extending to death** · *Related: B-32, C-09*

BNS s. 41 (IPC s. 103): the right extends to causing death in defence of property against robbery; house-breaking by night; mischief by fire to a dwelling or to a building used for the custody of property; and theft, mischief or house-trespass causing reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt †. In other cases only harm short of death (s. 42).

*Word count: 60*

<a id="a-66"></a>
**A-66 · Commencement and continuance of private defence** · *Related: B-33, C-08*

The right of private defence of the body begins with a reasonable apprehension of danger from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, though it has not been committed, and continues as long as the apprehension continues (BNS s. 40; IPC s. 102). Of property, it continues, against theft, until the offender retreats with the property, official help arrives, or the property is recovered (s. 43) †.

*Word count: 67*

<a id="a-67"></a>
**A-67 · Excess of private defence** · *Related: B-33, C-08*

If a person in good faith exceeds the power given by law in private defence and causes death, without premeditation and without intending more harm than necessary, he commits culpable homicide not amounting to murder (BNS s. 101, Exception 2; IPC s. 300, Exception 2) †. The right is for defence, not revenge.

*Word count: 52*
