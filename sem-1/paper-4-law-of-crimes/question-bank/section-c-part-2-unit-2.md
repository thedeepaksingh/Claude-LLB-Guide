## C-06 · Define abetment. Explain its kinds, who is an abettor, and the punishment for abetment, with reference to abetment of suicide

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2024 Q4 (A-36, "Abettor"). Related: A-35 to A-43, B-19 to B-21, B-23.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** introduction and meaning 2 · three modes of abetment (s. 45) 5 · abettor and Explanations (s. 46) 4 · punishment (ss. 49–60) 3 · abetment of suicide 4 · conclusion 2

### Answer

**Introduction.** Abetment, criminal conspiracy and attempt are **inchoate (incomplete) offences**: the law punishes a step towards another offence, whether or not that offence is finally committed. Abetment is dealt with in Chapter V of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), **ss. 45–60** (Chapter V of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (IPC), ss. 107–120) ‡.

**1. Abetment defined (BNS s. 45; IPC s. 107).** A person abets the doing of a thing who:
- **First** — **instigates** any person to do that thing; or
- **Secondly** — **engages with one or more other persons in any conspiracy** for the doing of that thing, if an act or illegal omission takes place **in pursuance of that conspiracy** and in order to the doing of that thing; or
- **Thirdly** — **intentionally aids**, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing. †

**Explanation 1.** Wilful misrepresentation, or wilful concealment of a material fact which one is bound to disclose, by which a person voluntarily causes or procures a thing to be done, is instigation. **Explanation 2.** Whoever, before or at the time of the act, does anything in order to facilitate the commission of the act, and thereby facilitates it, **aids** it. †

**2. The three modes**
1. **Instigation** means to goad, urge forward, provoke, incite or encourage. There must be a **positive act** of instigation, by words or conduct, express or implied (*Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh*, (2001) 9 SCC 618 †). A bare wish, a reprimand or an angry outburst is not instigation unless the speaker **intends** the act to follow (*Sanju @ Sanjay Singh Sengar v. State of M.P.*, (2002) 5 SCC 371 †). The instigation must be linked to the act abetted.
2. **Conspiracy as abetment.** Two requirements: (a) engaging in a conspiracy to do the thing; and (b) an **act or illegal omission in pursuance of it**. Without the act, there is no abetment by conspiracy (though there may be the separate offence of criminal conspiracy under s. 61: see C-07).
3. **Intentional aid** by an act or illegal omission, before or at the time of the offence, which actually facilitates it. The aid must be **intentional**: giving a lift to a man who then commits a crime, without knowing his purpose, is not abetment.

**3. Abettor (BNS s. 46; IPC s. 108).** An abettor is a person who abets (i) the commission of an offence, or (ii) the commission of an act which would be an offence if done by a person capable by law of committing an offence with the same intention or knowledge as the abettor. The Explanations make these points: †
- **Abetment of an illegal omission** is an offence even if the abettor is not himself bound to do the act.
- **It is not necessary that the act abetted be committed**, or that the effect be caused. A instigates B to murder C. B refuses. A is guilty of abetting murder.
- **It is not necessary that the person abetted be capable of committing an offence**, or that he have the same guilty intention. A, with a guilty intention, abets a child or a person of unsound mind to do an act which would be an offence if done by an adult: A is guilty.
- **Abetment of an abetment** is an offence. A instigates B to instigate C to murder Z; if C does it, A is liable.
- It is not necessary that the abettor **concert the offence with the person abetted**; it is enough if he engages in the conspiracy in pursuance of which the offence is committed.

**Abetment within and outside India.** A person who, **in India**, abets an act outside India which would be an offence if done in India is an abettor (BNS s. 47; IPC s. 108A). The BNS adds the converse: a person who, **outside India**, abets the commission in India of an act which would be an offence (s. 48 †, new).

**4. Essentials.** (i) A specific offence, or act that would be an offence, is abetted; (ii) the abettor has done one of the three acts (instigation, conspiracy followed by an act, intentional aid); (iii) the abettor has the **guilty mind**: he intends the offence to be committed or knows the circumstances; (iv) it is **not necessary** that the offence be committed.

**5. Punishment (BNS ss. 49–60; IPC ss. 109–120)** †

| Situation | BNS | IPC | Punishment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abetted act **committed** in consequence | s. 49 | 109 | As for the offence |
| Abettor's intention differs from the doer's | s. 50 | 110 | As if the act had been done with the abettor's intention or knowledge |
| **Different act** done | s. 51 | 111 | Liable if it was a **probable consequence** of the abetment |
| Cumulative punishment | s. 52 | 112 | Liable for both acts if they are separate offences |
| Different effect caused | s. 53 | 113 | Liable if he knew it was likely |
| Abettor **present** | s. 54 | 114 | Deemed to have committed the offence |
| Offence punishable with death or life, **not committed** | s. 55 | 115 | Up to **7 years** and fine; up to **14 years** if harm results |
| Offence punishable with imprisonment, **not committed** | s. 56 | 116 | Up to **one-fourth** of the longest term, or fine, or both |
| Abetting commission by the public or more than ten persons | s. 57 | 117 | Up to 3 years, or fine, or both |
| Concealing a design to commit an offence punishable with death or life | s. 58 | 118 | Up to 7 years and fine (3 years if the offence is not committed) |

(Ranges are from the IPC; the BNS section numbers are †.)

**6. Abetment of suicide**
- **Provisions.** BNS **s. 108** (IPC s. 306): if any person commits suicide, whoever abets the commission of such suicide is punishable with imprisonment up to **10 years** and fine ‡. BNS **s. 107** (IPC s. 305): if the person who commits suicide is a **child**, a person of **unsound mind**, a delirious person, an idiot or an intoxicated person, the abettor is punishable with **death, imprisonment for life or up to ten years**, and fine ‡. An attempt to commit suicide is no longer an offence under the BNS, except where made to compel or restrain a public servant (s. 226) †.
- **Presumption.** Where a married woman commits suicide within seven years of marriage and was subjected to cruelty by her husband or his relatives, the court may presume abetment (Evidence Act s. 113A; now Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 117 †).
- **Judicial approach.** The courts insist on a **positive act of instigation or aid**, close in time to the suicide, and on the **intention** to push the victim into it.
  - *Ramesh Kumar* (2001): a remark such as "go and die", uttered in anger in a quarrel, is not instigation; instigation needs a positive act and intention †.
  - *Sanju* (2002): words used in a quarrel ("go and die") do not by themselves amount to instigation; the suicide did not follow the words immediately, so there was no proximate link †.
  - *Gangula Mohan Reddy v. State of A.P.* (2010) 1 SCC 750: abetment involves a **mental process** of instigating or intentionally aiding; the instigation must be to a degree that left the victim no other option †.
  - *Amalendu Pal @ Jhantu v. State of W.B.* (2010) 1 SCC 707: **harassment alone**, without a proximate act of incitement, is not abetment of suicide †.
  - *Chitresh Kumar Chopra v. State (NCT of Delhi)* (2009) 16 SCC 605: instigation, conspiracy or aid must be shown; the words must be a direct incitement †.
- **Criticism.** Charges under s. 108 are often added as a matter of routine in cases of marital discord, family quarrels or workplace stress, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly quashed them where no positive act is shown.

**7. Illustrations**
1. A hands B a gun on the day of the murder, telling him where C will be. B kills C. A has abetted by aid and is punishable under s. 49 as for murder.
2. A instigates B to beat C. B kills C with a knife. A is liable under s. 51 if death was a **probable consequence** of the beating; if the killing was independent and unforeseeable, A is liable for the beating only.
3. A is present when B beats C to death, having instigated the beating. A is deemed to have committed the offence (s. 54).

**8. Abetment, conspiracy and attempt.** Abetment by conspiracy needs an act in pursuance; criminal conspiracy under s. 61 needs only the agreement (if the object is an offence); attempt needs an act towards the offence. The abettor is not the principal offender but is punished as if he were one when the offence is committed.

**Conclusion.** Abetment makes it an offence to instigate, conspire for or intentionally aid an offence, and it is complete even if the offence is not committed. Its limits are the need for a positive act, an intention that the offence be committed, and, in abetment of suicide, a close link between the accused's act and the death.

## C-07 · What is criminal conspiracy? Discuss its essentials, proof and punishment, and distinguish it from abetment

**Asked as:** not yet set at 20 marks. Related: A-39, A-44, A-45, B-22, B-23.
**Marks:** 20 · **Time:** about 30 minutes
**Model rubric (mine):** history and definition 4 · essentials (agreement; overt act) 5 · proof 3 · punishment 2 · cases 3 · conspiracy v. abetment (and common intention) 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Introduction.** A criminal conspiracy is an **agreement** between two or more persons to do an illegal act, or a legal act by illegal means. The IPC originally punished conspiracy only as a form of abetment (which needs an act in pursuance of the conspiracy). The **Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1913** added Chapter V-A (ss. 120A and 120B) and made the agreement itself an offence †. The BNS merges the two sections into one: **s. 61** ‡.

**1. Definition (BNS s. 61(1); IPC s. 120A).** When two or more persons agree to do, or cause to be done, (a) an **illegal act**, or (b) an act which is **not illegal by illegal means**, such an agreement is designated a **criminal conspiracy**.

- **Proviso.** No agreement except an agreement to commit an **offence** amounts to a criminal conspiracy **unless some act besides the agreement is done** by one or more parties in pursuance of it. †
- **Explanation.** It is immaterial whether the illegal act is the ultimate object of the agreement or is merely incidental to that object. †

**2. Punishment (BNS s. 61(2); IPC s. 120B)** †
- A party to a criminal conspiracy to commit an offence punishable with **death, imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for two years or upwards**, where no express provision is made for the punishment of the conspiracy, is punished **as if he had abetted that offence**.
- A party to any other criminal conspiracy is punished with imprisonment up to **six months**, or fine, or both.
- Special conspiracies carry their own punishments, for example conspiracy to wage war against the Government (BNS s. 148; IPC s. 121A) ‡.

**3. Essentials**
1. **An agreement between two or more persons.** A person cannot conspire with himself. The other parties need not be known, identified or convicted, but there must be at least two persons who actually agree.
2. **The agreement is to do an illegal act, or a legal act by illegal means.** "Illegal" includes an offence and also an act prohibited by law or ground for a civil action.
3. **Overt act.** If the object is an **offence**, the agreement alone is enough: the conspiracy is complete when the agreement is made. If the object is illegal but not an offence (a civil wrong), or the means are illegal and the act legal, some **act besides the agreement** is required (the proviso).
4. **A guilty mind**: knowledge of the agreement and intention to further its object (*State of Maharashtra v. Som Nath Thapa*, (1996) 4 SCC 659 †).

**4. Proof of conspiracy**
1. Conspiracy is hatched in **secrecy**, so direct evidence is rare. The agreement is usually **inferred from circumstances**: the conduct of the parties before, during and after the offence. The inference must be drawn from facts that point unambiguously to an agreement (*Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)*, (1988) 3 SCC 609 †).
2. **Things said, done or written by one conspirator in reference to the common design** are relevant against all (Evidence Act s. 10; Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s. 8 †).
3. **One conspiracy or several?** The Court asks whether there is one overall agreement or several separate ones (*Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab*, (1977) 4 SCC 540 †). Where the agreement is a "wheel" or a "chain", the question is whether each party knew of the larger design.
4. **A conspirator is liable for what is done in pursuance** of the conspiracy even if he took no part in each act; a person who joins later or leaves earlier is liable only for the period in which he was a member. A conspiracy continues until its object is achieved or abandoned.
5. **Acquittal of co-conspirators.** Where only two persons are charged and one is acquitted, the other cannot be convicted of conspiracy with him; but a person can be convicted of conspiring with persons unknown or not tried (*Topandas v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1956 SC 33 †).

**5. Leading cases** (all from memory †)
- ***Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration)*** (1988) 3 SCC 609. Assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi. The Court held that conspiracy is proved by inference from circumstances; the agreement is the gist of the offence. It upheld the conviction of Kehar Singh as a conspirator (and of Satwant Singh as an assassin) and acquitted Balbir Singh.
- ***State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu @ Afsan Guru*** (2005) 11 SCC 600. The Parliament attack case (2001). The Court examined the agreement, the overt acts and the role of each accused, and upheld conspiracy convictions on circumstantial evidence, while the accused S.A.R. Geelani was acquitted.
- ***Yash Pal Mittal v. State of Punjab*** (1977) 4 SCC 540. One conspiracy or several; where separate agreements share no common design, they are separate conspiracies.
- ***Som Nath Thapa*** (1996) 4 SCC 659. The mental element: the accused must know of the plan and intend to aid it.

**6. Conspiracy distinguished from abetment by conspiracy and from common intention**

| Point | Criminal conspiracy (s. 61) | Abetment by conspiracy (s. 45, second) | Common intention (s. 3(5)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | A **substantive offence** | A **mode of abetment** | A **rule of joint liability** |
| Agreement | Essential | Essential | A prior meeting of minds |
| Act beyond the agreement | **Not needed** if the object is an offence | An **act or illegal omission in pursuance** is necessary | The criminal act itself |
| Persons | Two or more | The abettor and the doer | Two or more |
| Punishment | As an abettor (offence of two years or more) or up to six months | As abetment | As for the offence |
| Offence completed? | Not necessary | Necessary under s. 49; not under ss. 55–56 | Yes |

**In one line:** conspiracy punishes the agreement; abetment by conspiracy punishes the agreement plus an act in pursuance of it; common intention makes each participant liable for the act done.

**7. Related provisions.** Organised crime (BNS s. 111) and terrorist act (s. 113) are conspiracy-like offences with their own punishments ‡.

**8. Criticism.** Conspiracy has been called "the darling of the modern prosecutor's nursery" (Judge Learned Hand †): it is easy to allege, hard to rebut, allows the acts and statements of one conspirator to be used against all, and can punish a mere agreement with no act. The 1913 amendment widened the reach of the Code. Courts therefore insist on proof of an agreement, not mere suspicion or association.

**Conclusion.** Criminal conspiracy makes the agreement to commit an offence itself punishable; an act beyond the agreement is needed only where the object is not an offence. It differs from abetment by conspiracy, which needs an act in pursuance, and from common intention, which is a rule of joint liability and not an offence.
