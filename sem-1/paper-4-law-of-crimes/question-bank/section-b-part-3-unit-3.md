## B-25 · Explain mistake of fact and mistake of law as defences

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 4 marks: Dec 2022 Q3 ("mistake of fact", A-46). 20-mark form: C-11. Related: A-47.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** maxim and sections 3 · essentials 3 · illustrations 2 · mistake of law 2

### Answer

***Ignorantia facti excusat; ignorantia juris non excusat.***

**Sections.** BNS **s. 14** (IPC s. 76): nothing is an offence done by a person who is, or who by reason of a **mistake of fact and not of law**, in **good faith** believes himself to be, **bound by law** to do it. **S. 17** (IPC s. 79): the same where he believes himself **justified by law** †.

**Essentials.** (1) A mistake of **fact**; (2) an honest belief in **good faith**, that is, with due care and attention (BNS s. 2(11)) ‡; (3) the belief is that he is **bound** (s. 14) or **justified** (s. 17) by law.

**Illustrations.** A soldier fires on a mob by lawful order: no offence. A seizes Z, who appears to be committing murder, to bring him before the authorities: no offence, even if Z acted in self-defence.

**Mistake of law** is no defence: everyone is presumed to know the law.

**Comparison.** *R v. Tolson* (1889): honest belief that the wife was dead excused bigamy; *R v. Prince* (1875): belief that the girl was over sixteen did not excuse taking her from her father †.

**Conclusion.** Mistake of fact negatives the guilty mind; mistake of law does not.

## B-26 · Explain the defences of accident and necessity

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-11. Related: A-49, A-50.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** accident 4 · necessity 4 · distinction and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Accident (BNS s. 18; IPC s. 80).** Nothing is an offence done by **accident or misfortune**, without criminal intention or knowledge, in the doing of a **lawful act**, in a **lawful manner**, by **lawful means**, with **proper care and caution** †.
*Illustration.* A is at work with a hatchet; the head flies off and kills a man standing nearby. If there was no want of caution, A is not guilty. If there was, the charge is death by negligence (s. 106) ‡.

**Necessity (s. 19; IPC s. 81).** An act known to be likely to cause harm is not an offence if done **without criminal intention** to cause harm and in **good faith** to prevent or avoid **other harm** to person or property. Whether the harm was imminent enough is a question of fact †.
*Illustration.* A steamer's captain, unable to stop, changes course to avoid a boat with twenty passengers and runs down a boat with two, without intending to.

**Murder.** Necessity is no defence to murder: shipwrecked sailors who killed the cabin boy were convicted (*R v. Dudley and Stephens*, 1884) †.

| | Accident | Necessity |
|---|---|---|
| Harm | Unforeseen | Foreseen but justified |
| Basis | No intention or knowledge | Lesser harm to avoid greater |

## B-27 · Explain the defence of infancy. What is the law on juvenile offenders?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-11. Related: A-51, A-52.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** ss. 20 and 21 4 · Juvenile Justice Act 4 · proof of age 1 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Under seven (BNS s. 20; IPC s. 82).** Nothing is an offence done by a child under seven. The presumption is **conclusive** (*doli incapax*) †.

**Seven to twelve (s. 21; IPC s. 83).** Nothing is an offence done by a child above seven and under twelve **who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding** to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct on that occasion. The presumption is **rebuttable**; the court must examine the child's maturity †.

**Above twelve.** Liable under the Code, but the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015** governs everyone under **18** (a "child in conflict with law"). Dealt with by the **Juvenile Justice Board**, not a criminal court. For a **heinous offence** by a child of **16 to 18**, the Board makes a **preliminary assessment** of capacity (s. 15), and the Children's Court may try him as an adult †. Death and life imprisonment without release are barred.

**Age** is proved by school or birth certificate, failing which by ossification test (s. 94 †); the plea can be raised **at any stage** (*Hari Ram v. State of Rajasthan*, 2009 †).

**Conclusion.** The Code sets a minimum age; the 2015 Act a separate scheme to 18.

## B-28 · Distinguish legal insanity from medical insanity. Explain section 22

**Asked as:** Dec 2024 Q1 set "legal and medical insanity" at 4 marks (A-55). 20-mark form: C-10. Related: A-53, A-54.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** section 22 3 · M'Naghten 2 · table 3 · burden and procedure 2

### Answer

**BNS s. 22 (IPC s. 84).** Nothing is an offence done by a person who, **at the time of doing it**, by reason of **unsoundness of mind**, is incapable of knowing the **nature of the act**, or that he is doing what is **wrong or contrary to law** †.

**M'Naghten rules (1843).** Every man is presumed sane; the defence needs proof that, by a **defect of reason from disease of the mind**, he did not know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong. The Code speaks of "unsoundness of mind" and adds "contrary to law" †.

| Point | Legal insanity | Medical insanity |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Law (s. 22) | Psychiatry |
| Test | Cognitive incapacity at the time of the act | Any mental disorder |
| Effect | Complete defence | None by itself |

Only legal insanity is a defence (*Hari Singh Gond v. State of M.P.*, 2008 †).

**Burden.** On the accused (Evidence Act s. 105; BSA s. 108 †), but only on a **preponderance of probability**, judged from his whole conduct (*Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat*, 1964 †).

**Procedure.** The accused is acquitted on the ground of unsoundness of mind and may be detained in safe custody (BNSS s. 369 †).

## B-29 · Explain the defence of intoxication

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-10. Related: A-56, A-57.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** two sections 4 · table or contrast 3 · cases 3

### Answer

Two sections deal with intoxication: **ss. 23 and 24** (IPC ss. 85 and 86) †.

| Point | Involuntary (s. 23; IPC s. 85) | Voluntary (s. 24; IPC s. 86) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Given without his knowledge or against his will | Taken of his own will |
| Effect | If, by reason of it, he is **incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it is wrong or contrary to law**, he is not guilty | **No defence** |
| Knowledge | – | Presumed: the knowledge he would have had if sober |
| Intention | – | A question of fact: the court decides whether he in fact formed it |

**Cases**
- ***Basdev v. State of PEPSU*** (1956) †: drunkenness is no excuse, but where a **specific intent** is an element of the offence, intoxication is considered in deciding whether he could form it.
- ***DPP v. Beard*** (1920) †: drunkenness is no defence to murder unless it makes the accused incapable of forming the specific intent.
- ***DPP v. Majewski*** (1977) †: voluntary intoxication is no defence to offences of basic intent.

**Delirium tremens.** Insanity caused by long drinking falls under s. 22, not s. 24 †.

**Conclusion.** The law excuses a man made drunk without fault, not one who chooses to drink.

## B-30 · Explain consent as a defence

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark form: C-11. Related: A-58, A-59.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** rule and limits 3 · sections 4 · illustrations 2 · conclusion 1

### Answer

*Volenti non fit injuria.* Harm suffered by consent is not an offence, within limits (BNS ss. 25–31; IPC ss. 87–93) †.

| Section (IPC) | Rule |
|---|---|
| s. 25 (87) | Act not intended, and not known to be likely, to cause **death or grievous hurt**, done by consent of a person **over 18** |
| s. 26 (88) | Act not intended to cause death, done in **good faith** for the person's **benefit**, with consent |
| s. 27 (89) | Good-faith benefit of a **child under 12 or person of unsound mind**, with guardian's consent |
| s. 28 (90) | **Consent invalid** if given under fear or misconception, by a person of unsound mind or intoxicated, or by a child under 12 |
| s. 29 (91) | Acts that are offences **independently of the harm** (miscarriage) not protected |
| s. 30 (92) | **No consent** needed in good faith for benefit where it is impossible to obtain |
| s. 31 (93) | Good-faith **communication** for benefit |

*Illustrations.* Two persons fence for amusement and consent to harm without foul play. A surgeon operates on a consenting patient who dies.

**Limit.** Consent cannot justify intentionally causing death or grievous hurt; consent to death only reduces murder to culpable homicide (BNS s. 101, Exception 5) ‡.

## B-31 · Explain the right of private defence and its restrictions

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 20 marks: Dec 2022 Q10 and Dec 2024 Q13(c) (C-08). Related: A-62, A-63.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** nature and basis 3 · scope 2 · restrictions 4 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Nature.** Everything done in the exercise of the right of private defence is **not an offence** (BNS s. 34; IPC s. 96). The right is **preventive, not punitive**: the State cannot protect everyone at every moment.

**Scope (s. 35; IPC s. 97).** Every person has the right to defend (a) the **body**, his own or another's, against any offence affecting the human body, and (b) **property**, movable or immovable, against theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass or their attempts. It exists even against acts of a child, a person of unsound mind or an intoxicated person (s. 36) †.

**Restrictions (s. 37; IPC s. 99)** †. No right against an act done, or attempted, by:
1. a **public servant** acting in good faith under colour of his office, though the act is not strictly justifiable by law;
2. a person acting on the **direction of a public servant** in the same way;
3. where there is **time to have recourse to the public authorities**;
4. the right **never extends to more harm than necessary**.

**Case.** The right is defensive; the defender is not weighed "in golden scales" (*Jai Dev v. State of Punjab*, 1963) †.

## B-32 · When does the right of private defence extend to causing death?

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. Set at 20 marks: Dec 2022 Q10, Dec 2024 Q13(c) (body, C-08) and Dec 2025 Q9 (property, C-09). Related: A-64, A-65.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** body 4 · property 4 · other harm and conclusion 2

### Answer

**Body (BNS s. 38; IPC s. 100).** The right extends to causing **death** if the assault is of one of **seven** descriptions ‡:
1. causing reasonable apprehension of **death**;
2. causing reasonable apprehension of **grievous hurt**;
3. with intention of committing **rape**;
4. with intention of gratifying **unnatural lust** (the IPC clause; the BNS wording is not checked †);
5. with intention of **kidnapping or abducting**;
6. with intention of **wrongful confinement** where the victim cannot seek help from the authorities;
7. **throwing or administering acid**, or attempting to, causing apprehension of grievous hurt.

**Property (s. 41; IPC s. 103)** †. Death may be caused to defend against: (1) **robbery**; (2) **house-breaking by night**; (3) **mischief by fire** to a building, tent or vessel used as a dwelling or for custody of property; (4) **theft, mischief or house-trespass** causing reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt.

**Other cases (ss. 39, 42).** The right extends to **any harm other than death** †.

**Limits.** All subject to s. 37: no more harm than necessary, and no right where there is time to approach the authorities.

**Conclusion.** Property alone never justifies killing, except in the listed grave cases.

## B-33 · Explain the commencement and continuance of the right of private defence, and the effect of exceeding it

**Asked as:** not yet set at 10 marks. 20-mark forms: C-08, C-09. Related: A-66, A-67.
**Marks:** 10 · **Limit:** ≤200 words
**Rubric (mine):** body 3 · property 3 · excess 3 · conclusion 1

### Answer

**Body (BNS s. 40; IPC s. 102).** The right **commences** as soon as there is a **reasonable apprehension of danger** from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, though it has not been committed, and **continues** as long as the apprehension continues †.

**Property (s. 43; IPC s. 105)** †. The right commences when a reasonable apprehension of danger to the property begins. It continues:
- against **theft**: until he retreats with the property, official help arrives, or the property is recovered;
- against **robbery**: while he causes or attempts death, hurt or restraint, or the fear of instant death, hurt or restraint continues;
- against **criminal trespass or mischief**: while the offender continues in it;
- against **house-breaking by night**: while the house-trespass continues.

**Excess.** A person who in good faith exceeds the power given by law and causes death, **without premeditation and without intending more harm than necessary**, is guilty not of murder but of **culpable homicide not amounting to murder** (BNS s. 101, Exception 2; IPC s. 300, Exception 2) †.

*Illustration.* A shoots dead a fleeing thief: the right never extended to death and had ended.

**Conclusion.** The right is for defence, not revenge: it lasts only as long as the danger.

*Word count: 200*
