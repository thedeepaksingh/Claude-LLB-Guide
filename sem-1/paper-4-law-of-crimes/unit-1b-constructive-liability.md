## 1. Constructive liability: the idea

1. **Meaning.** **Constructive (joint) liability** means that a person is held criminally liable for an offence which he did not himself commit, or did not commit completely, because he **joined with others** in the enterprise. Liability is "constructed" from his association with the group, not from his own act alone. It is an exception to the rule that a person is punished for his own act and mind.
2. **Why it exists.** In group crime it is often impossible to say which member struck the fatal blow. If each accused could say "I did not strike that blow", the group would escape. The law therefore treats the act of one as the act of all when they acted together with a common purpose.
3. **Where it appears in the Code.**

| Rule | BNS | IPC | Idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Common intention** | s. 3(5) ‡ | s. 34 | Criminal act done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all: each is liable as if he alone did it |
| Acts criminal by reason of the knowledge or intention | s. 3(6) † | s. 35 | If the act is criminal only because of a particular knowledge or intention, a person who joins with that knowledge or intention is liable |
| Effect caused partly by act, partly by omission | s. 3(7) ‡ | s. 36 | Same offence |
| Co-operation by doing one of several acts | s. 3(8) ‡ | s. 37 | Each who intentionally co-operates by doing any one of the acts is guilty |
| **Common object** | s. 190 ‡ | s. 149 | Member of an unlawful assembly is liable for an offence committed in prosecution of its common object |
| Abettor present | s. 54 † | s. 114 | Abettor present when the offence is committed is deemed to have committed it |
| Different act done | s. 51 † | s. 111 | Abettor liable for the probable consequence of what he abetted |
| Dacoity with murder | s. 310(3) ‡ | s. 396 | Every one of the five or more persons is liable |
| Robbery with hurt | s. 309(6) ‡ | s. 394 | Every person jointly concerned is liable |
| Lurking house-trespass at night with death or grievous hurt | s. 331(8) ‡ | s. 460 | Every person jointly concerned is liable |
| Group murder / grievous hurt on identity grounds | s. 103(2); s. 117(4) ‡ | – (new) | Each member of a group of five or more |
| Organised crime | s. 111 † | – (new) | Members of a syndicate |

4. **Constructive liability and vicarious liability.** In civil law, *vicarious liability* makes one person liable for another's tort. In criminal law the liability is **personal** but is attributed from the group's act. Criminal law has very few true vicarious offences, mostly statutory (for example the persons in charge of a company under special Acts).

## 2. Common intention: BNS s. 3(5) (IPC s. 34)

### 2.1 The text

BNS s. 3(5): "When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone." (Wording closely paraphrased from the Act ‡.) It is the IPC's s. 34 word for word, moved into the "General Explanations" section.

**History.** The original IPC s. 34 read "When a criminal act is done by several persons, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone." After *Queen v. Gorachand Gope* (1866) the section was amended by Act XXVII of 1870 to add the words "**in furtherance of the common intention of all**" †.

### 2.2 Nature of the section

Section 34 does **not create a substantive offence**. It states a **principle of joint liability**: it makes each participant liable for the criminal act of all, as if he alone had done it, but the offence is the one under the section that defines it (murder, hurt). The Supreme Court has repeatedly said so (for example in *Ramaswami Ayyangar v. State of T.N.* (1976) 3 SCC 779 †).

### 2.3 Essentials

1. **A criminal act** is done. "Act" includes a series of acts and, by s. 3(4), illegal omissions ‡.
2. It is done **by several persons**, that is, at least **two**.
3. It is done **in furtherance of the common intention of all**.
4. The accused **participated** in the act. Mere presence at the scene is not enough, but active physical participation at the spot is not always required.

### 2.4 Common intention

1. **Meaning.** Common intention implies a **pre-arranged plan** and a **prior meeting of minds**. The **Privy Council** in *Mahbub Shah v. King-Emperor* (1945) 72 IA 148, AIR 1945 PC 118 held that the common intention must be **shared by all** the participants; **similar intention** is not enough. It must have existed **before** the act, though it can be formed **on the spot**, just before the act, as in a sudden affray.
2. **How proved.** Direct proof of a plan is rare. It may be inferred from the conduct of the accused before, during and after the act, their arming, the way they acted together, and the nature of the injuries. The inference must be the only reasonable one.
3. **Difference between common intention and similar intention.** If two persons independently shoot at the same victim without any meeting of minds, they have the *same* or *similar* intention, but not a *common* one. Each is liable for his own act.
4. **Presence.** In ***Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. King-Emperor*** (1924) 52 IA 40, AIR 1925 PC 1, a group of young men went to a post office and the postmaster, who was counting money, was shot dead. Barendra Kumar's plea was that he had only stood outside as a guard and had not fired the shot (facts from memory †). The Privy Council held him liable for murder under s. 34 and said, in the often-quoted words of Lord Sumner, **"they also serve who only stand and wait"**: a person who waits and watches as part of the plan takes part in the act. Presence at the place is not always needed (a planner who is not present can be liable if he took part in an act in furtherance of the plan); courts differ and look to what he did †.
5. **Participation.** In *Pandurang, Tukia and Bhillia v. State of Hyderabad* AIR 1955 SC 216 the Supreme Court said that the common intention must be shown to be shared; the accused who played no part can be acquitted even though others were convicted †. In *Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab* AIR 1963 SC 174 the Court said that the participation must be in the commission of the offence, and that the prosecution must prove it †.
6. **Common intention may arise on the spot.** *Ramesh Singh v. State of A.P.* (2004) 11 SCC 305 and *Suresh v. State of U.P.* (2001) 3 SCC 673 hold that the common intention can be formed at the spot, but must be proved †.
7. **Liability for an act beyond the plan.** Each participant is liable for the criminal act done **in furtherance of** the common intention. If one participant does something that the others did not intend, the others are not liable for it. This is the limit of s. 34. Where the plan was to cause hurt and one member kills without the others' knowledge that he was likely to do so, the others are liable only for hurt, unless the facts show that they shared the intention to kill †.

### 2.5 Punishment

The same as for the offence committed. Murder: death or life imprisonment (s. 103(1)); so every participant in a murder under s. 3(5) can be sentenced to death or life.

## 3. Common object: BNS s. 190 (IPC s. 149) and unlawful assembly (s. 189; IPC s. 141)

### 3.1 The text

BNS s. 190: "If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence." (Paraphrased from the Act ‡.)

### 3.2 Unlawful assembly (s. 189(1); IPC s. 141) ‡

An assembly of **five or more** persons is **unlawful** if the **common object** of the persons composing it is:

1. to **overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force**, the Central or any State Government, Parliament or a State Legislature, or any public servant in the exercise of lawful power;
2. to **resist the execution of any law or legal process**;
3. to commit any **mischief, criminal trespass or other offence**;
4. by means of criminal force or show of criminal force, to **take or obtain possession of property**, to deprive a person of a right of way, water or other incorporeal right, or to **enforce a right or supposed right**;
5. by means of criminal force or show of criminal force, to **compel a person to do what he is not legally bound to do**, or to omit what he is legally entitled to do.

An assembly which was lawful when it assembled may become unlawful later (Explanation). See Unit 4C for the offences of being a member, rioting and affray.

### 3.3 Essentials of s. 190

1. There is an **unlawful assembly** of five or more persons.
2. An **offence** is committed by a **member** of the assembly.
3. It is committed **in prosecution of the common object**, or is one which the members **knew was likely** to be committed in prosecution of that object.
4. The accused was **a member of the assembly at the time**.

### 3.4 Key principles

1. **Common object, not common intention.** It may be formed at any stage, even on the spot. It is the object of the *assembly*, which is one of the five listed objects.
2. **Membership is enough.** A member who did nothing is liable if the offence was committed in prosecution of the common object. In ***Masalti v. State of U.P.*** AIR 1965 SC 202 the Court said that **mere presence in a crowd does not make a person a member**, but a person who is present and shares the common object is liable even without an overt act. The Court said that it is not a rule of law that a conviction in a large-crowd case needs a fixed number of witnesses, but as a **rule of prudence** it looks for support from several reliable witnesses, because innocent bystanders may be falsely implicated †.
3. **Five members are needed** at the time of the offence, but conviction under s. 190 is not defeated because fewer than five are convicted if the others are unidentified: *Dalip Singh v. State of Punjab* AIR 1953 SC 364 †; *Bharwad Mepa Dana v. State of Bombay* AIR 1960 SC 289 †. If only four are charged and the remaining are named "unknown", the section may apply where the evidence shows five or more were present.
4. **The "likely to be committed" limb.** A member is liable for an offence that went beyond the common object only if the members **knew** it was likely: *Mizaji v. State of U.P.* AIR 1959 SC 572 † (the section does not make every member liable for every act of one). The knowledge is proved from the nature of the assembly, the weapons carried, and the behaviour at the scene.
5. **A member who joined later or left earlier** is liable only for what happened while he was a member.
6. **No separate sentence is needed** for s. 190; the court convicts of the offence, and uses s. 190 to extend it to the members.
7. **Joint liability for murder.** All members of an unlawful assembly that commits murder in prosecution of its object are liable as principals for murder, even if only one struck the fatal blow (*Lalji v. State of U.P.* (1989) 1 SCC 437 †; *Yunis v. State of M.P.* (2003) 1 SCC 425 †).

## 4. Difference between common intention (s. 3(5)) and common object (s. 190)

| Point | Common intention (BNS s. 3(5); IPC s. 34) | Common object (BNS s. 190; IPC s. 149) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | A rule of joint liability; creates no offence | A distinct liability that attaches to **membership** of an unlawful assembly; conviction is for the offence committed, read with s. 190 (some judgments call it a specific liability or offence †) |
| Number of persons | **Two or more** | **Five or more** (an unlawful assembly) |
| What must be shared | A **common intention** (a prior meeting of minds, a pre-arranged plan; may arise on the spot) | A **common object**, one of the five objects in s. 189(1) |
| Participation | **Active participation** in the criminal act is needed | **Membership** is enough; no overt act is needed |
| Offence covered | The criminal act done **in furtherance of** the common intention | An offence committed **in prosecution of the common object**, or one the members **knew was likely** |
| "Knowledge" limb | No | Yes (second limb) |
| Unlawfulness | Not necessary that the group be unlawful | The assembly must be **unlawful** |
| Liability for other offences | Only those in furtherance of the intention | Also those members knew to be likely |
| Scope of "act" | A criminal act, including a series of acts | An offence |
| **Overlap** | Where the evidence shows both, a court may convict under either. Where **fewer than five** are convicted so s. 190 fails, s. 3(5) may be applied if the facts show a common intention and there is no prejudice (*Willie (William) Slaney v. State of M.P.* AIR 1956 SC 116; *Nanak Chand v. State of Punjab* AIR 1955 SC 274; *Krishna Govind Patil v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1963 SC 1413 †) | |

**The two sections in one line.** Section 3(5) punishes those who *plan and act together* (two or more); section 190 punishes *members of an unlawful assembly* for what is done *in pursuit of their object* (five or more).

## 5. Other forms of constructive liability

1. **Abettor present** (BNS s. 54; IPC s. 114): an absent abettor who is present when the offence is committed is deemed to have committed it ‡ (heading verified).
2. **Different act done** (s. 51; IPC s. 111): the abettor is liable for a different act that was a probable consequence of the abetment, as if he had abetted it ‡ (heading verified).
3. **Dacoity with murder** (s. 310(3)): if any one of five or more persons committing dacoity commits murder, every one of them is liable to death, life or rigorous imprisonment for not less than ten years ‡.
4. **Robbery with hurt** (s. 309(6)): every person jointly concerned is liable to life imprisonment or rigorous imprisonment for up to ten years ‡.
5. **Group offences on identity grounds** (new): five or more persons acting in concert who commit murder (s. 103(2)) or grievous hurt (s. 117(4)) on the grounds of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language or personal belief: each is liable ‡.
6. **Corporate and statutory vicarious liability**: a person in charge of the business of a company is liable under special Acts (for example the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, s. 141; the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, s. 10) †.
7. **Conspiracy and abetment** (Unit 2) are close to constructive liability: the conspirator or the abettor is liable for the act that follows.

## 6. Illustrations

1. **A, B and C** decide to rob D. A and B enter the house; C waits outside as a guard. A kills D. If the plan included violence, all three are liable for murder under s. 3(5). If B and C had agreed only to rob and did not know A carried a weapon, B and C are liable only for robbery.
2. **Twelve villagers** gather with lathis to take possession of a disputed field by force (object 4 of s. 189(1)). One of them kills a rival with a lathi. Every member present is liable for murder under s. 190, because murder was committed in prosecution of the object or was a likely consequence; a bystander who is only present and does not share the object is not a member.
3. **Two persons** quarrel with X separately; each fires at X without any meeting of minds. The intentions are similar, not common: each is liable for his own act only.

## 7. Criticism

1. **Wide reach.** The rule can convict persons who did not themselves do the killing. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against using s. 190 against bystanders in large crowds and has asked for careful scrutiny of the evidence (*Masalti*).
2. **Uncertainty.** The line between common intention and similar intention, and the question whether an act is "in furtherance", are matters of inference, which produce inconsistent decisions.
3. **Overlap.** The two sections overlap, and courts have allowed conviction under one when the other fails, even though the charge was under the other; this is permitted only if there is no prejudice.
4. **BNS.** The BNS keeps both rules unchanged and adds group offences (ss. 103(2), 117(4)), which apply a similar group-based liability to offences on identity grounds.

## 10. Quick-revision box

- **Constructive liability:** liability for an offence not wholly done by the accused, because of joining with others (ss. 3(5), 190, 310(3)).
- **s. 3(5):** criminal act + several persons (two or more) + common intention (prior meeting of minds; may arise on the spot) + participation. No separate offence. *Barendra Kumar Ghosh* (1925); *Mahbub Shah* (1945).
- **s. 190:** unlawful assembly (five or more; five objects in s. 189(1)) + offence by a member in prosecution of the common object or known to be likely + accused a member at the time. *Masalti* (1965).
- **Difference:** persons (2 vs 5) · intention vs object · participation vs membership · "likely" limb · overlap (*Willie Slaney*).
