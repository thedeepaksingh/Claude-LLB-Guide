## 2. The scheme: homicide

1. **Homicide** is the killing of a human being by a human being. It is **lawful** (excusable or justifiable: private defence, judicial execution, accident) or **unlawful**. Unlawful homicide is: (i) **culpable homicide amounting to murder**; (ii) **culpable homicide not amounting to murder**; (iii) **causing death by a rash or negligent act**.
2. **The relationship.** In the scheme of the Code, **culpable homicide is the genus and murder is a species**. All murders are culpable homicides but not all culpable homicides are murders (*State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya* AIR 1977 SC 45 †).
3. **Three steps in every case.** (i) Did the accused cause the death of a human being by an act? (ii) Does the act amount to culpable homicide under s. 100? (iii) If so, is it murder under s. 101, or does it fall within one of the Exceptions, so that it is punishable under s. 105? (*Punnayya*).

## 3. Culpable homicide: BNS s. 100 (IPC s. 299)

### 3.1 The text ‡

"Whoever causes death by doing an act **with the intention of causing death**, or **with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death**, or **with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death**, commits the offence of culpable homicide."

### 3.2 Three Explanations ‡

1. **Explanation 1.** A person who causes bodily injury to another who is labouring under a **disorder, disease or bodily infirmity**, and thereby accelerates the death of that other, shall be deemed to have caused his death.
2. **Explanation 2.** Where death is caused by bodily injury, the person who causes the injury shall be deemed to have caused the death, although **by resorting to proper remedies and skilful treatment the death might have been prevented**.
3. **Explanation 3.** The causing of the death of a child in the mother's womb is **not homicide**; but it may amount to culpable homicide to cause the death of a living child, if any part of that child has been brought forth, though the child may not have breathed or been completely born.

(A summary of the Act's page elsewhere on the web showed only one Explanation; the page I could read in full shows three, which agree with IPC s. 299. Check the Act.)

### 3.3 Three illustrations ‡ (paraphrased)

- (a) A lays sticks and turf over a pit, intending or knowing that death is likely. Z, believing the ground firm, treads on it, falls in and dies. A has committed culpable homicide.
- (b) A knows Z is behind a bush; B does not. A induces B to fire at the bush, intending or knowing Z's death is likely. B fires and kills Z. B may be guilty of no offence; A has committed culpable homicide.
- (c) A, by shooting at a fowl with intent to kill and steal it, kills B, who is behind a bush; A did not know B was there. Although A was doing an unlawful act, he is not guilty of culpable homicide, as he neither intended to kill B nor knew that the act was likely to kill.

### 3.4 Essentials

1. **A human being is dead.** "Human being" does not include a child in the womb (Explanation 3).
2. **Death is caused by an act of the accused.** Causation: the act must be a cause of death, not necessarily the sole or the immediate cause (Explanations 1 and 2). The accused takes his victim as he finds him (the "thin-skull" rule).
3. **A guilty mind of one of three kinds:** (i) **intention to cause death**; (ii) **intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death**; (iii) **knowledge that the act is likely to cause death**.

### 3.5 Causing death of a person other than the one intended (s. 102; IPC 301) †

If a person, by doing anything which he intends or knows to be likely to cause death, commits culpable homicide by causing the death of **a person whose death he neither intended nor knew himself to be likely to cause**, the culpable homicide committed is of the description of which it would have been if he had caused the death of the person whose death he intended or knew to be likely. This is the doctrine of **transferred malice**.

## 4. Murder: BNS s. 101 (IPC s. 300)

### 4.1 The four limbs ‡

"Except in the cases hereinafter excepted, culpable homicide is **murder** if the act by which the death is caused is done:

1. **Firstly**: with the **intention of causing death**; or
2. **Secondly**: with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender **knows to be likely to cause the death** of the person to whom the harm is caused; or
3. **Thirdly**: with the intention of causing bodily injury to any person, and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death**; or
4. **Fourthly**: if the person committing the act knows that it is **so imminently dangerous that it must, in all probability, cause death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death**, and commits such act **without any excuse** for incurring the risk of causing death or such injury as aforesaid."

(Wording from the page reproducing the Act ‡; I have followed the Act's order, which is the same as IPC s. 300.)

### 4.2 Illustrations to s. 101 (four; paraphrased from IPC s. 300, which the BNS repeats †)

- (a) A shoots Z dead intending to kill him: **murder**.
- (b) A, knowing that Z has a disease such that a blow is likely to cause his death, strikes him intending to cause bodily injury. Z dies of the blow. A is guilty of **murder**, although the blow would not have been sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to kill a healthy person. But if A did not know of the disease and gave a blow not sufficient to kill a healthy person, A is not guilty of murder if he did not intend to cause death or such injury as would in the ordinary course cause death.
- (c) A intentionally gives Z a sword-cut or club-wound sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature; Z dies: **murder**, though A had no premeditated design to kill any particular individual.
- (d) A without any excuse fires a loaded cannon into a crowd and kills one of them: **murder**, though A had no design to kill any particular individual.

### 4.3 Reading the four limbs

| Limb | Mental element | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st** | Intention to cause death | Subjective | Shooting to kill |
| **2nd** | Intention to cause an injury which the offender **knows** is likely to cause death of **that** victim | Subjective: special knowledge of the victim's condition | Blow to a person known to have a weak spleen |
| **3rd** | Intention to cause a **particular injury** that is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death** | Objective as to the injury; subjective as to the intention to inflict it | A spear thrust into the abdomen |
| **4th** | **Knowledge** that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death, with no excuse for the risk | Objective danger, subjective knowledge | Firing into a crowd; driving a vehicle into a crowd |

### 4.4 *Virsa Singh* test for the third limb

***Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab*** AIR 1958 SC 465 †. Virsa Singh and others attacked Khem Singh; Virsa Singh thrust a spear (a *barchha*) into his abdomen, which caused one injury, and Khem Singh died. The accused argued that there was no proof that he intended to cause an injury sufficient in the ordinary course to kill. Bose J. laid down that, to bring a case within the third limb, the prosecution must establish **four things**:

1. First, that a **bodily injury is present**;
2. Secondly, the **nature of the injury** must be proved (these are purely objective facts);
3. Thirdly, it must be proved that there was an **intention to inflict that particular injury**, that is, that it was not accidental or unintended (this is subjective);
4. Fourthly, it must be proved that the injury of the type just described is **sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature** (this is wholly objective, and it does not matter that the offender did not intend, or know, that it would be so sufficient).

If these are proved, the offence is murder under the third limb. It is **not necessary** to prove that the offender intended to cause death, or that he knew the injury would be fatal.

### 4.5 The five Exceptions ‡

Culpable homicide is **not murder** if it falls within one of the Exceptions. The offender is then punished under s. 105.

| Exception | Conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Grave and sudden provocation** | The offender, whilst **deprived of the power of self-control** by **grave and sudden provocation**, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or of any other person by mistake or accident. Three provisos: the provocation must not be (a) **sought or voluntarily provoked** by the offender as an excuse for killing or harm; (b) **given by anything done in obedience to law**, or by a public servant in lawful exercise of power; (c) **given by anything done in the lawful exercise of the right of private defence**. *Explanation:* whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough is a **question of fact** | A man finds his wife in the act of adultery and kills her on the spot |
| **2. Excess of private defence** | The offender, in the exercise in **good faith** of the right of private defence of person or property, **exceeds the power** given by law and causes the death of the person against whom he exercises it, **without premeditation** and **without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary** for the purpose of such defence | A, attacked by a thief, defends in good faith but uses more force than was needed and kills him |
| **3. Public servant** | A **public servant** (or one aiding him), acting for the **advancement of public justice**, exceeds the powers given by law and causes death by doing an act which he in good faith believes to be lawful and necessary for the due discharge of his duty, and **without ill-will** towards the person whose death is caused | A police officer fires beyond what is necessary to arrest |
| **4. Sudden fight** | The act is committed **without premeditation** in a **sudden fight**, in the **heat of passion** upon a **sudden quarrel**, and the offender has **not taken undue advantage** or acted in a **cruel or unusual manner**. *Explanation:* it is immaterial which party offers the provocation or commits the first assault | Two men quarrel; one strikes the other with a stick, who dies |
| **5. Consent** | The person whose death is caused, being **above eighteen**, **suffers death or takes the risk of death with his own consent** | Z, above eighteen, agrees to be killed and A kills him: culpable homicide, not murder. (The Act's illustration: A instigates Z, a person under eighteen, to commit suicide; Z's youth made him incapable of consenting, so A has abetted **murder**) |

(Section 101 has **twelve illustrations in all**: four to the section, six to Exception 1, one to Exception 2 and one to Exception 5 ‡.)

### 4.6 Exception 1: grave and sudden provocation

**The tests.**

1. **Provocation:** an act or words that cause the offender to lose self-control. In India words and gestures can suffice (unlike the old English rule that words alone were not enough). 
2. **Grave:** the provocation would cause a **reasonable person** in the offender's position to lose self-control. The test is **objective** ("the reasonable man") as well as subjective ("did the accused actually lose self-control?").
3. **Sudden:** it must not have been preceded by a **cooling-off period**. The question is whether the passion had time to cool between the provocation and the killing.
4. The death is of the person who provoked, or of another by mistake or accident.

***K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra*** AIR 1962 SC 605 †. Commander Nanavati, a naval officer, was told by his wife Sylvia that she had had an affair with Prem Ahuja. He went to his ship, took a revolver, drove to Ahuja's flat and shot him dead. A jury returned a verdict of "not guilty" by 8:1; the Bombay High Court, on a reference, set the verdict aside and convicted him of murder; the Supreme Court upheld the conviction and rejected the plea of grave and sudden provocation, holding that the three hours between the confession and the shooting gave time to cool and that the conduct showed deliberation. The Court stated these principles: (i) the test of "grave and sudden" provocation is whether a **reasonable man**, belonging to the same class of society as the accused and placed in the same situation, would be so provoked as to lose self-control; (ii) in India, words and gestures may amount to provocation; (iii) the **mental background** created by previous acts of the victim may be taken into consideration; (iv) the act must have been done **while the offender was deprived of self-control**, not after he had recovered. The case is also remembered for the end of jury trials in the Bombay (and later other) courts †.

***Mancini v. DPP*** [1942] AC 1: the reasonable man test in English law †.

### 4.7 Exception 4: sudden fight

**Conditions** (all five must be present): (i) **sudden fight**; (ii) **no premeditation**; (iii) **heat of passion**; (iv) **sudden quarrel**; (v) **no undue advantage and no cruel or unusual manner**. The position of the parties and the weapons used are considered. A person who kills an unarmed man with a deadly weapon, or who continues to strike after the other has fallen, takes undue advantage (*Surinder Kumar v. Union Territory, Chandigarh* (1989) 2 SCC 217 †). The Supreme Court has said that the exception is available only if the whole of the occurrence is one continuous quarrel; if the accused returns with a weapon after the quarrel, premeditation is shown (*Ghapoo Yadav v. State of M.P.* (2003) 3 SCC 528 †).

**Exception 1 and Exception 4 compared**

| Point | Exception 1: provocation | Exception 4: sudden fight |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Provocation from the deceased (or from another) | A sudden quarrel and fight between two or more persons |
| Parties | Offender kills the provoker (or another by mistake) | Both parties fight; it does not matter who struck first |
| Offender's state | Deprived of self-control | Heat of passion |
| Reasonable-man test | Yes | Not in terms |
| Undue advantage / cruel and unusual manner | Not a condition | A condition |
| Proviso | Three provisos | None (Explanation: who provoked is immaterial) |

## 5. Culpable homicide and murder: the distinction

### 5.1 Table

| Point | Culpable homicide (s. 100) | Murder (s. 101) |
|---|---|---|
| Relation | **Genus** | **Species**: culpable homicide with the extra features of s. 101 |
| Intention to cause death | Yes | Yes (1st limb) |
| Intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death | Yes ("**likely**" to cause death) | The injury is one that the offender knows is likely to cause *that victim's* death (2nd) or is **sufficient in the ordinary course of nature** to cause death (3rd) |
| Knowledge | Knowledge that the act is **likely** to cause death | Knowledge that the act is **so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability** cause death, with no excuse (4th) |
| Degree of probability | **Lower**: the death is likely | **Higher**: the death is *very* likely, in the ordinary course of nature, or in all probability |
| Exceptions | – | Five exceptions bring it back to culpable homicide |
| Punishment | s. 105: life, or 5–10 years (intent); up to 10 years (knowledge) | s. 103: death or life, and fine |

### 5.2 The correspondence

| Culpable homicide (s. 100) | Murder (s. 101) |
|---|---|
| (a) Intention to cause death | 1st limb |
| (b) Intention to cause an injury likely to cause death | 2nd limb (offender knows the injury is likely to cause *that* person's death) **and** 3rd limb (injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death) |
| (c) Knowledge that the act is likely to cause death | 4th limb (knowledge that the act is so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death) |

### 5.3 Leading cases

1. ***Reg. v. Govinda*** (1876) ILR 1 Bom 342 †. Govinda, a youth of eighteen, knocked down his wife, put his knee on her chest and struck her two or three violent blows on the face with his closed fist. A rupture of a blood-vessel in the brain caused her death. Melvill J. held that the offence was **culpable homicide not amounting to murder**: Govinda intended to cause bodily harm but the injury was not one that he knew was likely to cause death, and it was not sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; it fell under the second part of s. 299 and not under s. 300. He explained the difference as one of **degree of probability**.
2. ***Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab*** AIR 1958 SC 465 †: the four-step test for the third limb (§4.4).
3. ***State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya*** AIR 1977 SC 45 †. The Court explained that the difference between culpable homicide and murder is the **degree of probability of death**: in culpable homicide the death is a likely result; in murder, it is highly probable. It laid down the three-step approach (§2), and it said that the court must first decide whether the act is culpable homicide, and then, whether it falls within s. 300.
4. ***Pulicherla Nagaraju v. State of A.P.*** (2006) 11 SCC 444 †: the court may infer intention from (i) the nature of the weapon used, (ii) the part of the body targeted, (iii) the amount of force used, (iv) the circumstances (whether the incident was a sudden fight or premeditated), (v) whether there was a prior enmity, (vi) the conduct of the accused after the act.

### 5.4 How to decide a problem question

1. Establish that the **act caused the death**.
2. Ask what the accused **intended or knew**. Use the weapon, the part of the body, the number of blows, the manner, the motive and the conduct after the act.
3. Fit the mental state to the limb of s. 100.
4. If it fits one of the four limbs of s. 101, it is murder.
5. Then ask whether any of the five **Exceptions** applies (provocation, private defence, public servant, sudden fight, consent). If so, s. 105 applies.
6. Apply s. 3(5) (common intention) or s. 190 (common object) if there are several accused (Unit 1B).

## 6. Punishments for homicide

### 6.1 Murder: s. 103 ‡

- **s. 103(1)**: **death or imprisonment for life**, and fine.
- **s. 103(2) (new)**: when a group of **five or more persons acting in concert** commits murder on the ground of **race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground**, each member of the group shall be punished with **death or imprisonment for life, or imprisonment for a term not less than seven years**, and fine. This is the "mob lynching" provision.

### 6.2 Murder by a life-convict: s. 104 ‡

Whoever, being under sentence of imprisonment for life, commits murder, shall be punished with **death or with imprisonment for life, which shall mean the remainder of that person's natural life**. The IPC's s. 303 made death the **mandatory** punishment for this offence; the Supreme Court struck it down in ***Mithu v. State of Punjab*** (1983) 2 SCC 277, holding that a mandatory death sentence violates Articles 14 and 21 †. The BNS gives the court a choice.

### 6.3 Which punishment? The death penalty

1. **Constitutional validity.** *Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) 2 SCC 684 (Constitution Bench, 4:1) upheld the death penalty but limited it to the **"rarest of rare"** cases; life imprisonment is the rule, death the exception; the court must record special reasons (BNSS s. 393(3) †; Cr.P.C. s. 354(3)); the court weighs aggravating and mitigating circumstances †.
2. ***Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab*** (1983) 3 SCC 470 †: guidelines by categories: the manner of the commission (brutal, diabolical), the motive (extreme depravity), the anti-social or socially abhorrent nature of the crime (for example caste- or community-based killings), the magnitude of the crime (multiple murders), and the personality of the victim (child, helpless woman, public figure). The "balance-sheet" approach was criticised later for being too rigid.
3. **Life imprisonment** means imprisonment for the **whole of the remaining natural life** unless remitted (*Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1961 SC 600 †). The Court may fix a **fixed term of not less than 14 years or 20 or 30 years** without remission (*Swamy Shraddananda v. State of Karnataka* (2008) 13 SCC 767; *Union of India v. V. Sriharan* (2016) 7 SCC 1, Constitution Bench) †.
4. **Procedure.** A death sentence needs **confirmation by the High Court** (BNSS s. 407 †; Cr.P.C. s. 366). Mercy petitions go to the Governor (Art. 161) and the President (Art. 72). The Supreme Court has held that undue delay in deciding a mercy petition can be a ground for commutation (*Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India* (2014) 3 SCC 1 †).
5. **Law Commission.** The 262nd Report (2015) recommended abolishing the death penalty for all offences except terrorism-related offences †.

### 6.4 Culpable homicide not amounting to murder: s. 105 ‡

- **First limb** (the act is done with the **intention** of causing death, or of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death): **imprisonment for life, or imprisonment of either description for a term not less than five years which may extend to ten years**, and fine. (The IPC said "life, or up to ten years, and fine"; the BNS adds a **minimum of five years**.)
- **Second limb** (the act is done with the **knowledge** that it is likely to cause death, but without any intention to cause death or such bodily injury): imprisonment of either description up to **ten years**, with fine (the page I read shows "with fine"; the IPC said "or fine, or both"; check the Act).

**Illustrations.** A, in a sudden quarrel, strikes B on the head with a lathi once; B dies. A did not intend to kill but knew that a blow on the head was likely to cause death: second limb. A strikes B on the head with an iron rod intending to cause a fracture likely to cause death, in a sudden fight: first limb (with Exception 4).

## 7. Causing death by negligence: BNS s. 106 (IPC s. 304A)

1. **s. 106(1) ‡.** Whoever causes the death of any person by doing any **rash or negligent act** not amounting to culpable homicide shall be punished with imprisonment up to **five years** and fine. If the act is done by a **registered medical practitioner** while performing a medical procedure, the imprisonment may extend to **two years**, and fine.
2. **s. 106(2) ‡ (new).** Whoever causes death of any person by **rash and negligent driving** of a vehicle not amounting to culpable homicide, and **escapes without reporting** it to a police officer or a Magistrate soon after the incident, shall be punished with imprisonment up to **ten years**, and fine. This is the "hit-and-run" clause. It was widely protested by transport operators in January 2024, and the Government is reported to have said it would not be implemented until after consultation (from memory †); **I found no notification bringing s. 106(2) into force as of 23 July 2025** (not rechecked since; verify).
3. **Rashness and negligence.** *Rashness*: a conscious taking of an unjustifiable risk, with knowledge of the possible harm but hoping it will not occur. *Negligence*: failure to exercise the care that a reasonable person would, without foresight of the harm (*Empress of India v. Idu Beg* (1881) ILR 3 All 776 †). For criminal liability the negligence must be **gross** and the act must be the **proximate cause** of the death (*Kurban Hussein Mohammedali Rangwalla v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1965 SC 1616 †).
4. **Medical negligence.** *Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab* (2005) 6 SCC 1: a doctor is criminally liable only for **gross** negligence or recklessness; mere lack of care or an error of judgment is not enough; a private complaint should not be entertained without credible medical opinion; *Dr. Suresh Gupta v. Government of NCT of Delhi* (2004) 6 SCC 422 †.
5. **Difference from culpable homicide.** In s. 106 there is **no intention or knowledge** of the likelihood of death; the harm is the result of carelessness.

## 8. Attempt to murder and attempt to commit culpable homicide

1. **s. 109 (IPC 307) ‡.** Whoever does any act with such **intention or knowledge, and under such circumstances that, if he by that act caused death, he would be guilty of murder**, shall be punished with imprisonment up to **ten years** and fine; if **hurt is caused** to any person by such act, the offender shall be liable to **imprisonment for life**, or the punishment above. A **life-convict** who does such an act, if hurt is caused, may be punished with **death** or with imprisonment for life (the remainder of natural life).
2. **Essentials.** (i) An **intention or knowledge** such that, had death resulted, the offence would be murder; (ii) an **act** towards it that goes beyond preparation. **Hurt need not be caused**: the act itself suffices (*State of Maharashtra v. Balram Bama Patil* (1983) 2 SCC 28 †; *Om Prakash v. State of Punjab* AIR 1961 SC 1782 †). The nature of the injury is a guide to the intention but is not conclusive.
3. **s. 110 (IPC 308) †.** Attempt to commit culpable homicide: up to three years, or fine, or both; if hurt is caused, up to seven years, or fine, or both (IPC rule; verify the BNS numbers).

## 9. Hurt and grievous hurt

### 9.1 Hurt: s. 114 (IPC 319) and s. 115 (IPC 321, 323) ‡

- **Definition.** Whoever causes **bodily pain, disease or infirmity** to any person is said to cause **hurt**. "Bodily" is the key: mental distress alone is not hurt.
- **Voluntarily causing hurt** (s. 115(1)): causing hurt with the **intention** to cause hurt or with the **knowledge** that he is likely to cause hurt. **Punishment** (s. 115(2)): imprisonment up to **one year**, or fine up to **₹10,000**, or both.

### 9.2 Grievous hurt: s. 116 (IPC 320) ‡

"The following kinds of hurt only are designated as **grievous**":

| (a) | **Emasculation** |
|---|---|
| (b) | **Permanent privation of the sight** of either eye |
| (c) | **Permanent privation of the hearing** of either ear |
| (d) | **Privation of any member or joint** |
| (e) | **Destruction or permanent impairing of the powers** of any member or joint |
| (f) | **Permanent disfiguration of the head or face** |
| (g) | **Fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth** |
| (h) | **Any hurt which endangers life**, or which causes the sufferer to be, during the space of **fifteen days**, in severe bodily pain, or unable to follow his ordinary pursuits |

(The eight kinds are the same as IPC s. 320, whose clauses were numbered First to Eighthly.)

### 9.3 Punishment: s. 117 (IPC 322, 325) ‡

- **s. 117(1)**: voluntarily causing grievous hurt: the offender intends or knows he is likely to cause grievous hurt, and grievous hurt is caused. *Explanation (as in IPC s. 322 †):* a person is said voluntarily to cause grievous hurt if, intending or knowing himself to be likely to cause grievous hurt of one kind, he actually causes grievous hurt of another kind. *Illustration:* A intending to permanently disfigure Z's face strikes Z so as to cause him severe bodily pain for fifteen days: A has voluntarily caused grievous hurt.
- **s. 117(2)**: up to **seven years** and fine.
- **s. 117(3) (new)**: if the hurt causes **permanent disability or a persistent vegetative state**: **rigorous imprisonment not less than ten years**, which may extend to life imprisonment (the remainder of natural life).
- **s. 117(4) (new)**: **five or more persons acting in concert** cause grievous hurt on identity grounds: each member up to **seven years** and fine.

### 9.4 Dangerous weapons: s. 118 (IPC 324, 326) ‡

- **Hurt** by means of an instrument for shooting, stabbing or cutting, or any instrument which, used as a weapon of offence, is likely to cause death, or by fire, heated substance, poison, corrosive substance, explosive, or by any substance deleterious to the human body to inhale, swallow or receive into the blood, or by an animal: up to **three years**, fine up to ₹20,000, or both.
- **Grievous hurt** by such means: imprisonment for **life**, or imprisonment of either description for **one to ten years**, and fine.

### 9.5 Acid attack: s. 124 (IPC 326A, 326B) ‡

- **s. 124(1)**: causing **permanent or partial damage or deformity**, or burns, or maiming, disfiguring, disabling, or grievous hurt, by **throwing or administering acid**: imprisonment **not less than ten years** which may extend to **life** (the remainder of natural life), with fine to meet the medical expenses of the victim.
- **s. 124(2)**: **attempt** to throw or administer acid: **five to seven years** and fine.
- *Laxmi v. Union of India* (2014) 4 SCC 427 †: the Supreme Court regulated the sale of acid, directed compensation, and made the treatment of acid-attack victims free in hospitals. The 2013 amendment inserted ss. 326A and 326B in the IPC after the Nirbhaya case (J.S. Verma Committee) †.

### 9.6 Other sections of the group (from the IPC order †)

- Hurt or grievous hurt **to extort property or a valuable security**, or to **compel an illegal act** (ss. 119; IPC 327, 329).
- Hurt or grievous hurt **to extort a confession** or information (s. 120; IPC 330, 331).
- Hurt or grievous hurt **to deter a public servant** (s. 121; IPC 332, 333).
- Hurt or grievous hurt on **grave and sudden provocation** (s. 122; IPC 334, 335): the offender is punished less than for ordinary hurt.
- Hurt by **poison** to commit an offence (s. 123; IPC 328).
- Act **endangering life or personal safety of others** (s. 125; IPC 336, 337, 338): negligent or rash acts causing hurt or grievous hurt.

### 9.7 Hurt and grievous hurt compared

| Point | Hurt | Grievous hurt |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Bodily pain, disease or infirmity (s. 114) | Eight kinds only (s. 116) |
| Kinds | One definition | Eight listed kinds |
| Seriousness | Less serious | More serious |
| Punishment (voluntary) | Up to 1 year, or ₹10,000, or both | Up to 7 years and fine |
| Compounding | Compoundable (BNSS s. 359; Cr.P.C. s. 320) † | Compoundable only in some cases, with the court's permission † |
| Intention test | Intended or knew likely to cause hurt | Intended or knew likely to cause grievous hurt |

## 10. Illustrations and problems

1. **A, in a heated quarrel with B over a debt, picks up a hammer and hits B once on the head. B dies.** Mental state: A intended to hurt, and a blow on the head with a hammer is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death. If A intended that injury, it is murder under the 3rd limb (*Virsa Singh*); if the quarrel was sudden, without premeditation, and A took no undue advantage (a hammer on the head of an unarmed man may be undue advantage), the court examines Exception 4.
2. **A discovers his wife in bed with C and, in a rage, shoots her dead on the spot.** Exception 1 (grave and sudden provocation) applies, if A had not planned it. A is liable under s. 105, not s. 103.
3. **A, a doctor, performs a surgery with a rash and negligent act; the patient dies.** Section 106(1), up to two years, subject to *Jacob Mathew* (gross negligence needed).
4. **A fires a pistol at B in order to kill him, but misses and B is unhurt.** Section 109 (attempt to murder): up to ten years.
5. **A intends to kill B, but kills C by mistake.** Transferred malice: s. 102; A is guilty of murder of C.
6. **A, B and C beat D with sticks, intending to teach him a lesson. D dies of a blow by B.** Whether all are liable for murder depends on common intention (s. 3(5)): if all intended to cause hurt only, A and C may be liable for hurt or culpable homicide, not murder, unless the intention to kill was shared (Unit 1B).

## 11. Criticism

1. **Hard line between culpable homicide and murder.** The "likely" versus "sufficient in the ordinary course" difference is a matter of degree; courts rely on the nature of the weapon, the part of the body and the number of blows; critics say the result depends on how the judge perceives probability. The Law Commission's 42nd Report (1971) examined the scheme; the BNS has not changed it †.
2. **"Knowledge" in the 2nd and 4th limbs.** The limbs mix intention and knowledge in a way that confuses even lawyers.
3. **Death penalty.** *Bachan Singh*'s "rarest of rare" test has been applied inconsistently; the Supreme Court itself has said so (*Mithu*; *Shatrughan Chauhan*). The 262nd Law Commission Report (2015) recommended abolition except for terrorism †.
4. **Section 105 minimum.** The BNS adds a five-year minimum to the first limb; critics say that it limits judicial discretion in cases of sudden fights.
5. **Mob lynching (s. 103(2)).** A response to a real problem (*Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India* (2018) 9 SCC 501 directed measures against mob violence †), but critics say the clause's list of grounds is closed ("or any other similar ground") and its minimum is only seven years against death or life.
6. **Hit-and-run (s. 106(2)).** Protests from drivers; deferred (see §7).

## 14. Quick-revision box

- **CH (s. 100):** death by an act done (1) with intention to cause death, (2) with intention to cause injury likely to cause death, (3) with knowledge that death is likely. Three Explanations.
- **Murder (s. 101):** CH is murder if done (1) with intention to kill, (2) with intention to cause injury known to be likely to kill that victim, (3) with intention to cause injury sufficient in the ordinary course to cause death, (4) with knowledge of imminent danger, no excuse. **Five Exceptions:** provocation · private defence excess · public servant · sudden fight · consent.
- **Punishment:** murder s. 103(1) death or life + fine; 103(2) mob lynching; s. 104 life-convict; CH not amounting to murder s. 105 (life or 5–10 years + fine / up to 10 years); negligence s. 106 (5 years; 2 years for doctors; hit-and-run 10 years); attempt s. 109 (10 years; life if hurt).
- **Hurt (s. 114):** pain, disease, infirmity; 1 year / ₹10,000. **Grievous hurt (s. 116):** eight kinds; 7 years. Dangerous weapons s. 118. Acid s. 124 (10 years to life).
- **Cases:** *Govinda*; *Virsa Singh*; *Punnayya*; *Nanavati*; *Mithu*; *Bachan Singh*; *Machhi Singh*.
