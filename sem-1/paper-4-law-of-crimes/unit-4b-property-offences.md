## 2. Theft: BNS s. 303 (IPC ss. 378, 379)

### 2.1 The text ‡

**s. 303(1).** "Whoever, **intending to take dishonestly any movable property out of the possession of any person without that person's consent, moves that property in order to such taking**, is said to commit theft."

**Five Explanations ‡:**

1. A thing so long as it is **attached to the earth**, not being movable property, is not the subject of theft; but it becomes capable of being the subject of theft as soon as it is **severed** from the earth.
2. A **moving effected by the same act which affects the severance** may be a theft.
3. A person is said to **cause a thing to move** by removing an obstacle which prevented it from moving or by separating it from any other thing, as well as by actually moving it.
4. A person who by any means **causes an animal to move** is said to move that animal, and to move everything which, in consequence of the motion so caused, is moved by that animal.
5. The **consent** mentioned in the definition may be **express or implied**, and may be given either by the person in possession, or by any person having for that purpose authority either express or implied.

**Illustrations:** there are **sixteen**, (a) to (p) ‡. The important ones (taken from the IPC's illustrations to s. 378, paraphrased; I have left out the letters because the BNS lettering may differ †):

- A cuts down a tree on Z's ground with the intention of dishonestly taking it out of Z's possession. As soon as A has **severed** the tree in order to such taking, he has committed theft.
- A meets a bullock carrying a box of treasure and drives it so that he can dishonestly take the treasure. As soon as the bullock begins to move, A has committed theft of the treasure.
- A, Z's servant, entrusted with the care of Z's plate, dishonestly runs away with it. A has committed theft (the plate remained in Z's possession; a servant's custody is not possession).
- Z, going on a journey, entrusts his plate to A, a warehouse-keeper. A sells it. The plate was **not in Z's possession** (it was in A's), so there is no theft; A may have committed **criminal breach of trust**.
- A finds a ring belonging to Z on a table in Z's house. The ring is in Z's possession; A commits theft if he dishonestly removes it.
- A finds a ring lying on the high road, not in the possession of any person. By taking it A commits no theft, though he may commit criminal **misappropriation**.
- A sees a ring on a table in Z's house. Not venturing to take it away at once, he hides it where Z is unlikely to find it, meaning to take it later. A commits theft **at the time of first moving the ring**.
- A, in good faith believing property of Z to be his own, takes it out of B's possession. A does not take dishonestly and commits no theft.

### 2.2 Essentials

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1. Dishonest intention** | Intention to cause **wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another** (s. 2(7), 2(36), 2(37)); the intention must exist **at the time of the moving** |
| **2. Movable property** | Corporeal movable property (s. 2(21)); land and things attached to the earth are not, until severed. Things not in anyone's possession (wild animals at large, lost or abandoned things) cannot be stolen; electricity is movable property for theft by the Electricity Act, 2003 (s. 135) † |
| **3. Out of the possession of another** | The property is in the **possession** of a person (actual or constructive), not just owned by him. A servant's custody is the master's possession (the servant illustration); a thing in a house is in the occupier's possession |
| **4. Without consent** | Consent may be express or implied (Explanation 5). Where the owner is made to hand the property over by fear or deceit, the offence is more likely extortion or cheating than theft (§4.5, §8) |
| **5. Moving** | Some **moving** of the property to effect the taking (Explanations 1–4). It is not necessary that the thief takes it away; a mere moving suffices |

### 2.3 Leading cases

- ***K.N. Mehra v. State of Rajasthan*** AIR 1957 SC 369 †. Two young airmen (cadets) took a trainer aircraft from the airfield and flew off; they were caught and charged with theft. The Supreme Court held that the **dishonest intention** existed when the aircraft started to move (the intention to take it permanently, or to use it so as to cause wrongful loss, was shown by the way they took and flew it); the later abandonment did not matter, and **theft was complete** when the aircraft moved with that intention.
- ***Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan*** AIR 1963 SC 1094 †. A public servant induced a clerk to take a file out of the office so that it could be used for a private purpose, and it was later returned. The Court held that **temporary removal** for a dishonest purpose is theft: it is not necessary that the taker intends to keep the property permanently; the intention of causing wrongful gain or loss, **even temporarily**, is enough, and the moving of the file out of the custody was the moving required.

### 2.4 Theft, minimum community service

The first proviso introduced by the BNS: for a **first conviction** for theft where the **value of the stolen property is less than ₹5,000** and the property is **returned or its value restored**, the punishment is **community service** (s. 303(2) proviso ‡). Community service was added as a punishment (s. 4(f)) ‡.

### 2.5 Aggravated theft (from the IPC order †)

- **s. 305 (IPC 380, 381…):** theft in a **dwelling house**, means of transport, place of worship, or of an idol, or of Government property, etc.: up to seven years and fine.
- **s. 306 (IPC 381):** theft by a **clerk or servant** of property in the possession of the master: up to seven years and fine.
- **s. 307 (IPC 382):** theft after **preparation for causing death, hurt or restraint** in order to the committing of the theft, or to retain the stolen property: RI up to ten years and fine.

## 3. Snatching: BNS s. 304 (new) ‡

**s. 304(1).** Theft is "**snatching**" if, in order to commit theft, the offender **suddenly or quickly or forcibly seizes or secures or grabs or takes away** from any person, or from his possession, any movable property.

**s. 304(2).** Punishment: imprisonment of either description up to **three years**, and fine.

**Note.** In the IPC, snatching was dealt with as theft (or as robbery if hurt was caused). The BNS makes it a separate offence with the same three-year maximum as ordinary theft ‡. It is on the list of new BNS offences ‡.

## 4. Extortion: BNS s. 308 (IPC ss. 383–389)

### 4.1 The definition ‡

**s. 308(1).** "Whoever **intentionally puts any person in fear of any injury** to that person, or to any other, and **thereby dishonestly induces the person so put in fear to deliver** to any person any **property, or valuable security, or anything signed or sealed which may be converted into a valuable security**, commits extortion."

**Five illustrations ‡:** (a) A threatens to publish a defamatory libel concerning Z unless Z gives him money: A commits extortion. (b) A threatens to confine Z's child wrongfully unless Z signs a promissory note: extortion. (c) A threatens to send men to plough up Z's field unless Z signs a bond: extortion; (d) A, by putting Z in fear of grievous hurt, induces Z to sign a blank paper and deliver it: extortion; (e) (added in the BNS) A threatens a child's death by an electronic message to obtain money: extortion ‡.

### 4.2 Essentials

1. **Intentionally putting a person in fear of injury** (injury as defined in s. 2(14): any harm illegally caused to body, mind, reputation or property).
2. The fear may relate to the person put in fear or **any other person**.
3. **Dishonest** inducement.
4. **Delivery** of property, valuable security, or a signed or sealed thing, by the person put in fear.
5. Delivery is the essence: **without delivery there is only an attempt** (s. 308(3)).

### 4.3 Punishments ‡

| s. 308 | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Extortion | Up to **7 years**, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Putting a person in fear of injury (or attempting) to commit extortion | Up to **2 years**, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Putting a person in fear of **death or grievous hurt** (or attempting) to commit extortion | Up to **7 years** and fine |
| (5) | Extortion by putting a person in fear of death or grievous hurt | Up to **10 years** and fine |
| (6) | Putting a person in fear of an **accusation** of an offence punishable with death, life or up to 10 years, to commit extortion | Up to **10 years** and fine |
| (7) | Extortion by fear of such an accusation | Up to **10 years** and fine |

### 4.4 From extortion to robbery to dacoity (Dec 2025 B-question)

1. **Extortion** (s. 308): fear of injury; delivery by the victim; no need for the offender to be present.
2. **Extortion becomes robbery** (s. 309(3)) if the offender, at the time of committing the extortion, is **in the presence** of the person put in fear, and commits the extortion by putting that person, or another, in fear of **instant death, instant hurt or instant wrongful restraint** to that person or another, and by so putting in fear **induces the person so put in fear then and there to deliver up the thing extorted**. (Explanation to IPC 390: the offender is said to be present if he is sufficiently near to put the other person in fear of instant death, hurt or restraint.)
3. **Robbery becomes dacoity** (s. 310(1)) when **five or more persons** conjointly commit or attempt to commit a robbery, or where the whole number of persons conjointly committing or attempting to commit a robbery, and persons present and aiding such commission or attempt, amount to five or more. So extortion amounting to robbery, when committed by five or more, is dacoity.

### 4.5 Extortion and theft

| Point | Theft | Extortion |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | **Without** the owner's consent | Consent is obtained, but by **putting in fear** |
| Property | **Movable** only | **Any** property, valuable security, or anything signed or sealed |
| How obtained | Offender **takes** (moves) | Victim **delivers** |
| Force or fear | None | **Fear of injury** is essential |
| Possession | Taken out of another's possession | Delivered by the person put in fear |
| Punishment (s. 303(2) / s. 308(2)) | 3 years | 7 years |

## 5. Robbery: BNS s. 309 (IPC ss. 390–394) ‡

1. **s. 309(1).** In all robbery there is either **theft or extortion**.
2. **s. 309(2): theft is robbery** if, in order to the committing of the theft, or in committing the theft, or in carrying away or attempting to carry away property obtained by the theft, the offender, for that end **voluntarily causes or attempts to cause to any person death or hurt or wrongful restraint**, or fear of **instant** death, **instant** hurt, or **instant** wrongful restraint.
3. **s. 309(3): extortion is robbery** (see §4.4).
4. **s. 309(4).** Punishment: **rigorous imprisonment up to ten years** and fine; if the robbery is committed on the **highway between sunset and sunrise**, up to **fourteen years**.
5. **s. 309(5).** Attempt: RI up to **seven years** and fine.
6. **s. 309(6).** Voluntarily causing hurt in committing or attempting robbery: the offender and **any other person jointly concerned** are liable to **life imprisonment**, or RI up to **ten years**, and fine. (This is an instance of constructive liability, Unit 1B.)

**Theft and robbery compared (Dec 2022 C-question: "Theft and when robbery")**

| Point | Theft | Robbery |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Dishonest moving, without consent | Theft or extortion **plus** violence or fear of instant violence |
| Force/fear | Not needed | **Voluntary causing (or attempting) of death, hurt or wrongful restraint**, or fear of **instant** death, hurt or restraint |
| Presence of the victim | Not needed | Needed |
| Punishment | Up to 3 years | RI up to 10 years (14 on the highway at night) |
| Relationship | Robbery is **aggravated theft or extortion** | – |

## 6. Dacoity: BNS s. 310 (IPC ss. 391, 395, 396, 399, 400, 402) ‡

1. **s. 310(1).** When **five or more persons conjointly commit or attempt to commit a robbery**, or where the whole number of persons conjointly committing or attempting to commit a robbery, and persons present and aiding such commission or attempt, amount to five or more, every person so committing, attempting or aiding is said to commit **dacoity**.
2. **s. 310(2).** Punishment: **imprisonment for life, or RI up to ten years**, and fine.
3. **s. 310(3).** If any one of five or more persons conjointly committing dacoity **commits murder**, **every one** of those persons is punishable with **death, life imprisonment, or RI not less than ten years**, and fine. (Constructive liability.)
4. **s. 310(4).** Making **preparation** to commit dacoity: RI up to **ten years** and fine.
5. **s. 310(5).** Being one of **five or more persons assembled** for the purpose of committing dacoity: RI up to **seven years** and fine.
6. **s. 310(6).** Belonging to a **gang** of persons associated for the purpose of **habitually committing dacoity**: life, or RI up to ten years, and fine.

**Robbery and dacoity compared**

| Point | Robbery | Dacoity |
|---|---|---|
| Number | One or more | **Five or more** (including those present and aiding) |
| Nature | Theft/extortion + violence | **Robbery by five or more** |
| Punishment | RI up to 10 years (14 on highway at night) | Life, or RI up to 10 years |
| Murder in the course | s. 309 (hurt: life or 10 years) | s. 310(3): **every** member liable to death/life |

## 7. Misappropriation and criminal breach of trust

### 7.1 Dishonest misappropriation: s. 314 (IPC 403) †

"Whoever **dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use any movable property**" shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than six months but may extend to two years, and fine (the minimum is the BNS's addition) †. *Explanations and illustrations (IPC):* a dishonest misappropriation for a time only is a misappropriation (Explanation 1); a person who finds property not in the possession of another, and takes it for his own use, commits misappropriation if he knows the owner or has the means of finding him and does not make reasonable efforts (Explanation 2); an illustration: A finds a Government promissory note endorsed in blank and sells it to Z; if A knew the owner and did not try, he is guilty.

**Misappropriation of a deceased person's property** (s. 315; IPC 404) †: dishonestly misappropriating property possessed by a deceased person at the time of death and not afterwards in the possession of any person entitled to it.

### 7.2 Criminal breach of trust: s. 316 (IPC 405–409) ‡

**s. 316(1).** "Whoever, being in any manner **entrusted with property, or with any dominion over property**, **dishonestly misappropriates or converts to his own use that property**, or **dishonestly uses or disposes of that property in violation of any direction of law** prescribing the mode in which such trust is to be discharged, or of any **legal contract**, express or implied, which he has made touching the discharge of such trust, or **wilfully suffers any other person so to do**, commits criminal breach of trust."

**Explanations ‡:** (1) an employer who deducts the employee's contribution to a provident fund or family pension fund from his wages is deemed to be entrusted with it; failing to pay it in violation of the law is dishonest use; (2) the same for the Employees' State Insurance Fund.

**Illustrations ‡ (six; summarised):** (a) an executor who disobeys the law and appropriates the effects: CBT; (b) a warehouse-keeper who sells the furniture entrusted to him: CBT; (c) an agent in Kolkata, to invest Z's ₹1 lakh in company paper, uses the money in his own business: CBT; (d) in good faith A buys bank shares thinking it benefits Z: no dishonesty, so no CBT (only a civil remedy); (e) a revenue officer who dishonestly appropriates public money he should pay into the treasury: CBT; (f) a carrier who dishonestly misappropriates goods entrusted to him: CBT.

**Punishments ‡:** (2) basic: up to **5 years**, or fine, or both; (3) **carrier, wharfinger or warehouse-keeper**: up to **7 years** and fine; (4) **clerk or servant**: up to **7 years** and fine; (5) **public servant, banker, merchant, factor, broker, attorney or agent** in the way of business: **life, or up to 10 years**, and fine.

**Essentials.**

1. **Entrustment**: the accused is entrusted with property or with dominion over it (ownership is not necessary; it means the property is handed over in a fiduciary capacity, with the owner's confidence). *State of Gujarat v. Jaswantlal Nathalal* AIR 1968 SC 700 †; *R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1962 SC 1821 †: "property" in CBT includes money and funds; a company's funds are property; the accused who has dominion over them is entrusted. *Velji Raghavji Patel v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1965 SC 1433 †: the rule I recall is that a partner's misuse of partnership property is largely a matter of accounts between partners, and a partner is guilty of CBT only if a specific entrustment and a dishonest misappropriation are shown (details unchecked).
2. **Dishonest** misappropriation, conversion, use, or disposal in violation of a direction or contract, or wilfully suffering another to do so.

### 7.3 Theft, misappropriation and criminal breach of trust compared (Dec 2025 A-question: "Theft v. misappropriation")

| Point | Theft (s. 303) | Misappropriation (s. 314) | CBT (s. 316) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Possession at the start | Property is **in another's possession**; taken **without consent** | Possession acquired **innocently** or **lawfully** (finding, delivery by mistake); no consent issue | Property is **entrusted** to the accused (possession or dominion with consent, in a fiduciary capacity) |
| Dishonest intention | At the time of moving | Arises **after** possession is obtained | Arises **after** entrustment |
| Moving | Needed | Not needed | Not needed |
| Entrustment | No | No | **Yes** |
| Illustration | Ring lying on a table in Z's house | Ring lying on the high road | Plate left with a warehouse-keeper who sells it |
| Punishment | Up to 3 years | 6 months to 2 years (†) | Up to 5 years; up to 10 / life (public servant, banker, etc.) |

**One line.** *Theft takes against consent; misappropriation keeps what came innocently; breach of trust betrays what was entrusted.*

## 8. Cheating: BNS s. 318 (IPC ss. 415–420) and cheating by personation: s. 319 (IPC ss. 416, 419)

### 8.1 Definition ‡

**s. 318(1).** "Whoever, by **deceiving any person**, **fraudulently or dishonestly induces the person so deceived** to **deliver any property to any person**, or to **consent that any person shall retain any property**, or **intentionally induces the person so deceived to do or omit to do anything which he would not do or omit if he were not so deceived**, and which act or omission **causes or is likely to cause damage or harm to that person in body, mind, reputation or property**, is said to **cheat**."

**Explanation ‡:** a **dishonest concealment of facts** is a deception within the meaning of this section.

**Illustrations ‡ (nine; summarised):** (a) A falsely pretends to be in the Civil Service and obtains goods on credit with no intention to pay: cheating; (b) A counterfeits a mark on an article to make Z believe a famous maker made it: cheating; (c) A shows a false sample to sell goods that do not match: cheating; (d) A pays with a bill drawn on an account with no funds, knowing it will be dishonoured: cheating; (e) A pledges articles as diamonds to obtain a loan: cheating; (f) A obtains a loan by assuring repayment with no intention to repay: cheating; (g) A promises to deliver indigo plant, with no intention to deliver, to obtain an advance: cheating; if A intended to deliver at the time but later broke the contract, it is only a civil breach; (h) A falsely claims to have performed a contract to receive payment: cheating; (i) A sells or mortgages property after already selling it, without disclosing the earlier sale: cheating.

### 8.2 Essentials

1. **Deception** of a person (by words, conduct, or dishonest concealment of facts).
2. **Fraudulent or dishonest inducement** (the intention to deceive, with wrongful gain/loss or fraud).
3. The person deceived is induced to (a) **deliver property**, (b) **consent to the retention of property**, or (c) **do or omit to do** something he would not otherwise do.
4. The act or omission **causes or is likely to cause damage or harm** to that person in body, mind, reputation or property.
5. **The deception must be present at the beginning**: the dishonest intention must exist **at the time of the inducement** (*Ram Jas v. State of U.P.* (1970) 2 SCC 740 †; *Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar* (2000) 4 SCC 168 †). In *Hridaya Ranjan* the Court said that, to convict of cheating, the prosecution must show a fraudulent or dishonest intention at the time the promise was made, and that failure to keep a promise does not by itself prove that intention.

### 8.3 Punishment ‡

| s. 318 | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Cheating | Up to **3 years**, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Cheating with the knowledge that the offender is likely thereby to cause **wrongful loss to a person whose interest in the transaction he is bound by law or contract to protect** | Up to **5 years**, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Cheating and thereby **dishonestly inducing the person deceived to deliver any property**, or to make, alter or destroy a valuable security (or anything signed or sealed which may be converted into a valuable security) | Up to **7 years** and fine |

### 8.4 Cheating by personation: s. 319 ‡

A person is said to **cheat by personation** if he cheats by **pretending to be some other person**, or by knowingly **substituting one person for another**, or representing that he or any other person is a person other than he or such other person really is. **Explanation:** the offence is committed whether the person personated is a **real or imaginary** person. **Illustrations ‡:** (a) A cheats by pretending to be a certain rich banker of the same name; (b) A cheats by pretending to be B, a person who is dead. **Punishment:** up to **five years**, or fine, or both ‡ (IPC s. 419: three years; check — the BNS figure was read ‡).

*Dec 2025 A-question "Cheating by personation".*

### 8.5 Cheating compared

| Point | Cheating | Theft | Criminal breach of trust | Extortion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consent of the owner | Obtained by **deception** | None | Given in entrustment | Obtained by **fear** |
| Intention at the start | Dishonest at the inducement | At the moving | After the entrustment | At the inducement |
| Property | Any property, valuable security | Movable only | Any property | Any property, valuable security |
| Deception | **Yes** | No | No | No (fear) |

### 8.6 Breach of contract and cheating

A mere breach of contract is not cheating. The dishonest intention must be shown from the beginning: false representation of an existing fact, a false promise made with no intention of keeping it, or a deliberate concealment. *S.W. Palanitkar v. State of Bihar* (2002) 1 SCC 241 †: the Court distinguished a mere breach of contract from cheating and criminal breach of trust, and held that a person who intended to perform at the time of the promise but failed is not guilty.

*Dr. Vimla v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1963 SC 1572 † on "defraud" and "deceit": deceit involves injury or likelihood of injury to the person deceived, and to deprive someone of what he is entitled to.

## 9. Mischief: BNS s. 324 (IPC ss. 425–432) ‡

### 9.1 Definition

**s. 324(1).** "Whoever, with **intent to cause, or knowing that he is likely to cause, wrongful loss or damage to the public or to any person**, **causes the destruction of any property, or any such change in any property or in the situation thereof as destroys or diminishes its value or utility, or affects it injuriously**, commits mischief."

**Explanation 1:** it is not essential that the offender should intend to cause loss or damage to the owner of the property injured or destroyed; it is sufficient if he intends or knows himself to be likely to cause wrongful loss or damage to **any person** by injuring any property, whether it belongs to that person or not. **Explanation 2:** mischief may be committed by an act affecting property belonging to the person who commits the act, or to that person and others jointly.

**Illustrations ‡ (eight):** burning a valuable security; melting ice in an ice-house; letting cattle into a field to damage the crop; etc.

### 9.2 Essentials

1. **Intent or knowledge of causing wrongful loss or damage** to the public or any person.
2. **Destruction** of property, or a **change** in the property or its situation that **destroys or diminishes** its value or utility or affects it injuriously.
3. Property need not be the victim's: mischief may be committed against the offender's own property if it causes wrongful loss to another (Explanation 2).

### 9.3 Punishment ‡

| s. 324 | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (2) | Mischief | Up to 6 months, or fine, or both |
| (3) | Mischief causing loss or damage to any property, including Government or local-authority property | Up to 1 year, or fine, or both |
| (4) | Loss or damage of **₹20,000 or more** but less than ₹1 lakh | Up to 2 years, or fine, or both |
| (5) | Loss or damage of **₹1 lakh or more** | Up to 5 years, or fine, or both |
| (6) | Mischief after **preparation to cause death, hurt, wrongful restraint**, or fear of these | Up to 5 years and fine |

(In the IPC, mischief causing damage of ₹50 or more was punishable with up to two years; the BNS changes the values.) Aggravated forms in ss. 325–328 †: killing or maiming animals; injury by fire or explosives; mischief to rail, aircraft or vessel; running a vessel aground (IPC 428–438).

**Mischief compared with theft and criminal trespass.** In mischief, the offender **destroys or damages**; in theft he **takes**; in trespass he **enters**.

*Dec 2025 A-question "Mischief".*

## 10. Criminal trespass, house-trespass and house-breaking (BNS ss. 329–334; IPC ss. 441–462)

1. **Criminal trespass** (s. 329(1); IPC 441) †: whoever **enters into or upon property in the possession of another** with intent to commit an offence or to **intimidate, insult or annoy** any person in possession, or, having lawfully entered, **unlawfully remains** there with such intent, commits criminal trespass.
2. **House-trespass** (s. 329(2); IPC 442) †: criminal trespass by entering into or remaining in any building, tent or vessel used as a **human dwelling**, or any building used as a place of **worship** or as a place for the **custody of property**.
3. **Lurking house-trespass** (s. 330; IPC 443): house-trespass **after taking precautions to conceal** it from a person who has a right to exclude or eject the trespasser †.
4. **House-breaking** (s. 330; IPC 445): house-trespass **by one of six modes of entry** listed in the section: through a passage made by the trespasser or an abettor; through a passage not meant for human entrance, or reached by scaling a wall; through a passage he or an abettor has opened; by opening a lock; by criminal force, assault or threat of assault; or through a passage fastened against entry and unfastened by him †.
5. **House-breaking by night** (IPC 446): house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise †.

**Punishments for house-trespass, lurking house-trespass and house-breaking (s. 331) ‡:**

| s. 331 | Case | Punishment |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking | Up to **2 years** and fine |
| (2) | Same, **after sunset and before sunrise** | Up to **3 years** and fine |
| (3) | Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking **in order to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment** | Up to **3 years** and fine; if the offence intended is **theft**, up to **10 years** |
| (4) | Same, **at night** | Up to **5 years** and fine; if the offence is theft, up to **14 years** |
| (5) | Lurking house-trespass or house-breaking **after preparation for hurt, assault, wrongful restraint**, or fear of these | Up to **10 years** and fine |
| (6) | Same, **at night** | Up to **14 years** and fine |
| (7) | While committing lurking house-trespass or house-breaking, **causing grievous hurt** or attempting to cause death or grievous hurt | **Life**, or up to 10 years, and fine |
| (8) | Same, **at night**, by any person; **every person jointly concerned** is liable | **Life**, or up to 10 years, and fine |

Other sections: **s. 332** (house-trespass to commit an offence, IPC 449–452) † and **s. 333** (house-trespass after preparation to cause hurt) †; **s. 334** (breaking open a receptacle) †.

## 11. Problems

### 11.1 The box in the courtyard (Dec 2023 B-question 7, rewritten as an exercise)

**Facts.** A enters B's house at night, moves a heavy box to the courtyard, opens it, finds nothing in it, and leaves.

**Approach (IRAC).**

1. **Issue.** What offences, if any, has A committed? In particular, is there theft, or only an attempt, given that the box held nothing?
2. **Rule.** Theft (s. 303(1)): dishonest intention to take movable property out of another's possession, without consent, and **moving** it in order to such taking; Explanation 3 and the bullock and hidden-ring illustrations (any moving is enough). Attempt (s. 62; IPC 511 with its first illustration: A tries to steal jewels by breaking open a box and finds that there are no jewels; he is guilty of **attempt**). House-trespass by night with intent to commit theft (ss. 329–331).
3. **Application.** *Theft:* A intended to take what was in the box dishonestly; he **moved the box** (movable property in B's possession, without consent) in order to take the contents. On one view, the box itself is the property moved, and the dishonest intention to take **the box and its contents** (or to remove it from B's possession) makes the offence **complete on moving** (as in *K.N. Mehra* and the bullock illustration: the moving suffices). *Alternative view:* if A meant only to take jewels or cash from the box and had no intention to take the box itself, the property intended to be taken (the contents) did not exist; so the offence is **attempt to commit theft** (s. 62 with s. 303), exactly as in the IPC illustration of the box without jewels. The better answer is to present both and conclude that, at the least, there is an attempt, and that on the facts of dishonest moving there is theft of the box.
   *Trespass:* A entered B's house **at night** to commit theft. This is **house-trespass**, and if A took precautions to conceal it or entered in one of the ways listed in s. 330, **lurking house-trespass or house-breaking by night** to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment: s. 331(4) (up to 5 years and fine; up to 14 years if the intended offence is theft). If neither applies, s. 332 (house-trespass to commit an offence) † applies.
4. **Conclusion.** A is guilty of (i) house-trespass (lurking house-trespass or house-breaking) by night with intent to commit theft, s. 331(4), and (ii) either theft (if moving the box is theft of the box) or attempt to commit theft (s. 62). The two thefts are alternatives; the court will not convict of both for the same act.

### 11.2 Other problems

1. **A, a cab driver, promises to take Z to the airport for ₹1,000, collects the fare in advance and never turns up, having intended from the start not to come.** Cheating (s. 318(4)): dishonest intention at the inception, delivery of money. If he intended to come and failed through a breakdown, only a civil breach.
2. **A, the manager of a bank, uses a customer's deposit to buy shares for himself.** Criminal breach of trust by a banker: s. 316(5) (life, or up to 10 years, and fine).
3. **A finds a gold chain on the road and keeps it, knowing the owner's name from a tag.** Misappropriation (s. 314): not theft, as the chain was not in anyone's possession (the high-road ring illustration).
4. **A, B, C, D and E armed with sticks enter a house at night, threaten the owner with death unless he hands over his cash, and take it.** Extortion becoming robbery (s. 309(3)); with five persons, dacoity (s. 310(1)); if one kills the owner, all are liable under s. 310(3) (death, life or RI not less than 10 years).
5. **A, in a quarrel, throws Z's phone to the ground and breaks it, costing ₹25,000.** Mischief with damage of ₹20,000 or more: s. 324(4) (up to 2 years, or fine, or both).
6. **A snatches a necklace from a woman walking on the road and runs.** Snatching (s. 304): up to 3 years and fine. If he pushed her and caused hurt in doing so, robbery with hurt (s. 309(6)).

## 12. Criticism

1. **Theft.** The requirement of "moving" makes the offence turn on a technicality; the dishonest intention is the real test. The definition does not cover intangible property (data, services). The BNS's community service for petty first thefts and the new snatching clause have been welcomed and criticised (snatching is a separate offence with the same three-year maximum as theft) †.
2. **Extortion–robbery boundary.** Fine distinctions (presence, instant fear, immediate delivery).
3. **Cheating and breach of contract.** Courts regularly quash FIRs that are civil disputes dressed up as cheating (*Hridaya Ranjan*; *Palanitkar*); the BNS has added no safeguard.
4. **Mischief values.** The thresholds in s. 324 (₹20,000, ₹1 lakh) replace the old trivial ones; critics say they are still arbitrary.
5. **Dacoity.** A fixed number of five is arbitrary; the law of constructive liability turns on it.

## 13. Current developments (to 29 October 2026)

1. The BNS has re-numbered the property offences (ss. 303–334), added **snatching** (s. 304), **community service** for petty theft (s. 303), a **minimum** for misappropriation †, and raised the money thresholds of mischief ‡.
2. Cheating by personation is now up to five years (s. 319) ‡.
3. The new organised-crime provisions (s. 111) overlap with robbery and dacoity gangs.
4. I did not find a Supreme Court decision since 1 July 2024 that changes the law on theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity or cheating under the BNS. **Not checked** exhaustively.
