## 1. Background: from Macaulay to the BNS

1. **The IPC.** The first Law Commission under **T.B. Macaulay** drafted the Indian Penal Code in 1837. It was enacted as Act XLV of 1860 and came into force on **1 January 1862**. It was a code in the strict sense: one statute defining the general principles (Chapters I–V) and the offences (Chapters VI–XXIII) in short, numbered sections with Illustrations, Explanations and Exceptions.
2. **Why replace it.** The IPC was amended many times, kept colonial offences (sedition, s. 124A) and had no place for modern crime (organised crime, terrorism, mob violence, snatching, cyber-enabled fraud). The Government introduced the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita Bill, 2023 in August 2023; a revised Bill was passed by Parliament in December 2023.
3. **The BNS.** Assented to on **25 December 2023**; in force from **1 July 2024**; **358 sections in 20 chapters** (the IPC had 511 sections in 23 chapters) ‡. The BNS repeals the IPC (the repeal and savings clause is **s. 358**) ‡. An offence committed **before** 1 July 2024 is still tried under the IPC; an offence committed on or after that date is tried under the BNS. Procedure is now governed by the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)** (earlier the CrPC), and evidence by the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA)** (earlier the Evidence Act).
4. **Main changes (BNS against IPC).** New offences: **organised crime** (s. 111) and **petty organised crime** (s. 112), **terrorist act** (s. 113), **mob lynching** (s. 103(2)), **snatching** (s. 304), **sexual intercourse by deceitful means** (s. 69), **hit-and-run** (s. 106(2)) ‡. **Community service** is a new punishment (s. 4(f)) ‡. **Sedition (IPC s. 124A)** is gone, but s. 152 punishes acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India ‡. **Adultery (IPC s. 497)** and **unnatural offences (IPC s. 377)** are not in the BNS ‡. The IPC attempt-to-suicide offence (s. 309) is not carried over except for an attempt made to compel or restrain a public servant (s. 226) †.
5. **Same structure, same principles.** The BNS keeps most of the IPC's definitions and its general exceptions almost word for word, so IPC case law on a provision with the same wording remains persuasive, and courts continue to cite it †. In the exam, **write the BNS section first and the IPC equivalent in brackets**, for example "BNS s. 101 (IPC s. 300)".

| Feature | IPC, 1860 | BNS, 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Sections / chapters | 511 / 23 | 358 / 20 ‡ |
| In force | 1 January 1862 | 1 July 2024 ‡ |
| Gender-neutral "child" | Different ages for boys and girls in some offences | "Child" means a person under 18 (s. 2(3)) ‡ |
| "Gender" | Man and woman | Includes transgender persons (s. 2(10)) † |
| New punishment | – | Community service (s. 4(f)) ‡ |

## 2. Concept and definition of crime

1. **Word and idea.** *Crime* comes from the Latin *crimen*, a charge or accusation. A crime is a wrong that the State treats as an offence against the community and punishes. Unlike a tort, it is not settled by the victim's private choice: the State prosecutes, and only the State (through the court, the Government or the compounding provisions) can end the proceedings.
2. **No definition in the Code.** Neither the IPC nor the BNS defines "crime". They define **"offence"**: "a thing made punishable by this Sanhita" (BNS s. 2(24) ‡; IPC s. 40). The BNSS (earlier CrPC s. 2(n)) defines "offence" as an act or omission made punishable by any law for the time being in force †. A wrong becomes a crime when **the law makes it punishable**.
3. **Definitions by jurists.**

| Jurist | Definition | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Blackstone** (*Commentaries*) | An act committed or omitted in violation of a public law forbidding or commanding it | Stresses that the law is **public** |
| **Stephen** (*General View of the Criminal Law*) | An act forbidden by law and revolting to the moral sentiments of society | Joins law and morality |
| **Kenny** (*Outlines of Criminal Law*) | Wrongs whose sanction is punitive, and is in no way remissible by any private person, but is remissible by the Crown alone, if remissible at all | Stresses the **sanction** and who controls it |
| **Austin** | A wrong which is pursued by the sovereign or his subordinates | Stresses **who prosecutes** |
| **Russell / Glanville Williams** | A legal wrong that can be followed by criminal proceedings which may result in punishment | Williams: a crime is whatever the law treats as a crime; it is defined by the **procedure** and the **outcome** |

4. **Features.** (i) A **harm** to the public as well as to the victim; (ii) the harm is **forbidden by law**; (iii) a **guilty mind** is normally needed; (iv) **State prosecution** (the Magistrate takes cognisance on a police report or a complaint, BNSS); (v) **punishment** (death, imprisonment, fine, forfeiture, community service) is the aim, not compensation; (vi) the State, not the victim, decides on the termination (subject to compounding and withdrawal).
5. **Crime, tort and civil wrong.**

| Point | Crime | Tort |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of wrong | Against the community | Against a private person |
| Who proceeds | The State | The injured person |
| Aim | Punish and deter | Compensate |
| Mental element | *Mens rea* normally | Not always |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt | Balance of probabilities |
| Compromise | Only for compoundable offences (BNSS) | Freely allowed |
| Law | Codified (BNS) | Mostly common law |

The same act may be both: assault, defamation (BNS s. 356), and negligently causing death (BNS s. 106). The two proceedings are separate.

6. **Is "crime" fixed for all time?** No. What is punished changes: sedition (IPC s. 124A) and adultery (IPC s. 497) were crimes and are not offences under the BNS in that form; stalking and acid attack were added to the IPC in 2013 and are in the BNS (ss. 78, 124). In *Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India* (2018) 10 SCC 1 the Supreme Court read down IPC s. 377 as applied to consenting adults, and in *Joseph Shine v. Union of India* (2019) 3 SCC 39 it struck down IPC s. 497 (adultery) as unconstitutional; the BNS carries neither offence ‡.

## 3. Elements of a crime

A crime has four elements. In the words of the classical statement: **(1) a human being, (2) a guilty mind (*mens rea*), (3) a guilty act or omission (*actus reus*), and (4) injury to another.**

### 3.1 A human being

1. **Only a human being can commit a crime** (the old rule that animals could be tried belongs to history). The "person" who can be punished is any human being of the age and sanity the law requires. The BNS defines "person" (s. 2(26)) to include any company or association or body of persons, whether incorporated or not ‡.
2. **Corporations.** A company can be prosecuted. In *Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement* (2005) 4 SCC 530 the Supreme Court held that a company can be prosecuted even for an offence for which imprisonment is the mandatory punishment; the court can impose the **fine** that it can pay, and the company is not exempt †. In *Iridium India Telecom Ltd v. Motorola Inc* (2011) 1 SCC 74 the Court held that a company can be prosecuted for cheating, where the directors' state of mind is attributed to it †.
3. **Persons who cannot be held criminally liable** are those protected by the General Exceptions: children under seven, children from seven to twelve with immature understanding, persons of unsound mind, and others (BNS ss. 14–33; Unit 3). The President and the Governors are immune from criminal proceedings while in office (Art. 361).

### 3.2 *Mens rea*: the guilty mind

1. **The maxim.** ***Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*** — an act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty. It comes from the 12th-century *Leges Henrici Primi* and was stated by **Coke** (3 Inst. 10). In *Fowler v. Padget* (1798) 7 TR 509, Lord Kenyon: "it is a principle of natural justice, and of our law, that *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*. The intent and the act must both concur to constitute the crime."
2. **What it covers.** *Mens rea* is not "evil motive". It is the **mental element required by the definition of the offence**: intention, knowledge, recklessness or negligence, or a particular purpose such as dishonesty or fraud.

| State of mind | Meaning | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| **Intention** | A decision to bring about a result, foreseen as desired or certain | Murder, BNS s. 101(a) (IPC s. 300) |
| **Knowledge** | Awareness that a result is likely or certain, or that a fact exists | Culpable homicide, BNS s. 100 (IPC s. 299); "knowingly" |
| **Dishonestly** | With the intention of causing wrongful gain or wrongful loss (BNS s. 2(7); IPC s. 24) | Theft, s. 303; misappropriation, s. 314 |
| **Fraudulently** | With intent to defraud, and not otherwise (BNS s. 2(9); IPC s. 25) | Forgery, cheating |
| **Voluntarily** | By means the offender intended, or knew or had reason to believe likely (BNS s. 2(33); IPC s. 39) | Hurt, s. 115 |
| **Rash / negligent** | Conscious disregard of a risk / failure to take care | Death by negligence, s. 106 (IPC s. 304A) |
| **Malice** | Ill will; not a general requirement | Defamation, malicious prosecution |

3. **Motive and intention.** **Motive** is the reason for the act (why); **intention** is the purpose of the act (what). Motive is generally irrelevant to liability but is relevant to **proof** (identity of the offender) and **sentence**. A good motive is no defence: a mercy-killing is still murder (or culpable homicide) unless an Exception applies.
4. **How the IPC/BNS deals with *mens rea*.** The Code does not state a general rule that *mens rea* is needed. Instead the **definition of each offence** carries its own mental element (the words "intending", "knowing", "dishonestly", "fraudulently" and so on), and the **General Exceptions** (Chapter III) take away criminality where the mind could not have been guilty (infancy, insanity, mistake of fact, accident, absence of criminal intent in necessity). Stephen explained that the Code's definitions themselves set out the mental element, which is why it has no general clause on *mens rea* †. In *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* AIR 1966 SC 43 the Supreme Court held that *mens rea* is an essential ingredient of a criminal offence unless a statute excludes it expressly or by necessary implication †.
5. **Presumption in statutes.** For statutory offences the courts begin with a presumption that *mens rea* is required (*Sherras v. De Rutzen* [1895] 1 QB 918; *Sweet v. Parsley* [1970] AC 132; *B v. DPP* [2000] 2 AC 428), but the presumption can be rebutted (§5 on strict liability).
6. **Exceptions to the rule.** *Mens rea* is not required for (i) **strict-liability** statutory offences; (ii) **public nuisance** (as in *R v. Stephens* (1866) LR 1 QB 702, the owner of a quarry was liable though he did not know) †; (iii) offences where the Code replaces intent with **constructive liability** (ss. 3(5) and 190, Unit 1B).

### 3.3 *Actus reus*: the guilty act or omission

1. **Meaning.** *Actus reus* is the **external element**: the conduct, its circumstances and its consequence that the law prohibits. "Act" denotes a single act or a series of acts, and "omission" a series of omissions (BNS s. 2; IPC ss. 32–33) †. The Code is clear that an **illegal omission** can be an offence (BNS s. 3(4)) ‡.
2. **Positive act.** Most crimes need a positive act: striking, taking, shooting. A **voluntary** act is needed; a reflex or an act in sleep does not suffice (the cases on automatism, such as *Bratty v. A-G for Northern Ireland* [1963] AC 386 †).
3. **Omission.** Liability for failing to act arises only where there is a **legal duty** to act. The duty may come from (i) **statute** (BNS s. 106(2): the driver must report after an accident ‡); (ii) **contract** (*R v. Pittwood* (1902) 19 TLR 37, a railway gatekeeper †); (iii) **relationship** or assumption of care (*R v. Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) 13 Cr App R 134, parents who starved a child †; *R v. Stone and Dobinson* [1977] QB 354 †; *R v. Instan* [1893] 1 QB 450 †); (iv) **creating a danger** (*R v. Miller* [1983] 2 AC 161 †). Illustrations to the BNS also show omissions: a jailer who intentionally omits to supply food to a prisoner and so causes death commits murder or culpable homicide.
4. **Consequence and causation.** For result crimes the act must be the **cause** of the result: the "but for" test and the "operating and substantial cause" test (*R v. Smith* [1959] 2 QB 35 †; *R v. Jordan* (1956) 40 Cr App R 152 †). The offender takes the victim as he finds him (*R v. Blaue* [1975] 1 WLR 1411, the victim who refused a transfusion †). The BNS says so for homicide: **Explanation 1 to s. 100** (the victim already had a disease: death caused by hastening it) and **Explanation 2** (death is caused by the injury even if proper treatment might have saved the victim) ‡.
5. **Attempts and omissions together.** The next three heads of conduct are covered in later units: abetment and conspiracy (Unit 2), attempt (below and Unit 2).

### 3.4 Injury to another

1. **"Injury"** is "any harm whatever illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation or property" (BNS s. 2(14); IPC s. 44) ‡. The harm must be **illegal**: an act which the law permits (reasonable chastisement by a lawful authority, a surgeon's operation in good faith) is not an injury.
2. The harm need not be to an individual. Offences against the State, public tranquillity and public health injure the **community**.
3. **Inchoate offences.** Attempt, conspiracy and abetment are punished before any injury happens, because they threaten it (Unit 2).

### 3.5 Concurrence of the elements

The *actus reus* and the *mens rea* must **coincide in time**. In *Thabo Meli v. R* [1954] 1 WLR 228 (PC) the accused attacked a man intending to kill and, believing him dead, threw him over a cliff; the victim died of exposure. The Privy Council held that the series of acts was one transaction, so the guilty mind and the fatal act were joined †.

## 4. Stages of a crime

A crime that is carried through passes four stages:

| Stage | Meaning | Punishable? |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Intention** | The decision to commit the offence | **No**: the law does not punish bare thought (*cogitationis poenam nemo patitur*) |
| **2. Preparation** | Arranging or collecting the means | **Generally no**; yes where the Code expressly makes it an offence |
| **3. Attempt** | A direct movement towards the commission, going beyond preparation | **Yes**: BNS s. 62 for offences with no special provision (IPC s. 511), and special attempt provisions (for example s. 109, murder; s. 110, culpable homicide; s. 309(5), robbery) |
| **4. Accomplishment** | The offence is completed | **Yes**, under the section that defines it |

### 4.1 Intention

Thought alone, however wicked, is not a crime because it cannot be proved and has caused no harm. Once it is **expressed** (a threat, an agreement, an instigation) it may become criminal intimidation, conspiracy or abetment.

### 4.2 Preparation

1. **Meaning.** Preparation is arranging or collecting what is needed to commit the offence: buying a weapon, collecting poison, hiring a getaway vehicle.
2. **The general rule.** It is not punishable, because it is equivocal: the same act may be done for a lawful purpose.
3. **Preparation made an offence.** The Code makes some preparations punishable because the danger is high: **preparation to commit dacoity** (BNS s. 310(4); IPC s. 399) ‡; **collecting arms to wage war against the Government** (BNS s. 149 †; IPC s. 122); **possessing counterfeit coin or forgery implements** (IPC ss. 235, 242, 257, 266 and BNS equivalents †); and **theft, robbery or house-breaking after preparation to cause death or hurt** (BNS ss. 307, 331(5)–(6) ‡).
4. **Illustrations.** Buying a pistol with intent to shoot someone is preparation. Loading the pistol and pointing it at the victim is attempt.

### 4.3 Attempt

1. **The statute.** BNS s. 62: "Whoever attempts to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment for life or imprisonment, or to cause such an offence to be committed, and in such attempt does any act towards the commission of the offence, shall, where no express provision is made for the punishment of such attempt, be punished with imprisonment of any description provided for the offence, for a term which may extend to one-half of the imprisonment for life or, as the case may be, one-half of the longest term of imprisonment provided for that offence, or with such fine as is provided for the offence, or with both." (Wording closely paraphrased from the Act ‡.) It replaces IPC s. 511.
2. **Where the Code provides for attempt.** Some offences have their own attempt sections, which prevail over s. 62: attempt to murder (BNS s. 109; IPC s. 307), attempt to commit culpable homicide (s. 110; IPC s. 308), attempt to commit suicide to compel a public servant (s. 226), attempt to commit robbery (s. 309(5) ‡).
3. **Tests for the line between preparation and attempt.**

| Test | Idea | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Last act test** | The offender has done the last act he needs to do | Early English rule; too narrow (*R v. Eagleton* (1855) Dears CC 515 †) |
| **Proximity test** | The act is **proximate** to the offence, not remote | *R v. Eagleton*: acts remotely leading to the offence are preparation; the last proximate act is attempt |
| **Equivocality test** | The act on its face shows the intention to commit the offence | Stephen and Salmond; *R v. Robinson* [1915] 2 KB 342 †: the jeweller who staged a robbery of his own safe was convicted of attempting to obtain by false pretences |
| **Indian approach** | Preparation ends and attempt begins when the offender **commits an act which is directly connected** with the commission of the offence and which would, but for some interruption, result in it | *Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar* AIR 1961 SC 1698 (the candidate who applied for the M.A. examination with a forged certificate was guilty of an attempt to cheat; the attempt began when he applied) |
| | A person who **transports goods** to a place from which he plans to smuggle or sell them has not attempted until he is at the point of the offence | *Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab* (1991) 4 SCC 341 (paddy from Punjab to Delhi through a place where it could be exported; held preparation, not attempt) |
| | The offender **needs to do no more than complete the act** | *State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub* (1980) 3 SCC 57 (silver loaded in a truck near the coast, intercepted before it could be exported: attempt to export, because the acts were proximate) |

4. **Attempt of an impossible offence.** An attempt is complete even if the offence cannot be committed through some fact unknown to the offender (an empty pocket; a box without jewels). IPC s. 511 Illustration (a): A tries to steal jewels by breaking open a box and finds there are none in it. A has done an act towards theft and is guilty of attempt. The BNS keeps the Illustrations †. English law first rejected (*Haughton v. Smith* [1975] AC 476) and then accepted (*R v. Shivpuri* [1987] AC 1; Criminal Attempts Act 1981 s. 1(2)) liability for impossible attempts †.
5. **Punishment.** Up to one-half of the longest term or one-half of life imprisonment (reckoned as 20 years, BNS s. 6 †), or fine, or both.
6. **Attempt and the completed offence.** If the offence is completed, the accused is convicted of the completed offence, not attempt. If the evidence shows only an attempt, the court may convict of the attempt (BNSS, the provision on conviction of an offence of which the accused was not charged †).

## 5. Strict liability

1. **Meaning.** An offence of **strict liability** is one in which *mens rea* is not required as to one or more elements of the *actus reus*: the prosecution need not prove intention or knowledge as to that element, and honest mistake is no defence.
2. **Why used.** Strict liability is used for statutes that protect the public from **grave social danger**: sale of adulterated food, import and export controls, traffic and licensing laws, pollution, weights and measures. The argument is that (i) the penalty is small (often fine only) and the stigma slight; (ii) proof of intention would make enforcement impossible; (iii) the law encourages the highest standard of care.
3. **English authorities.**

| Case | Rule |
|---|---|
| *R v. Prince* (1875) LR 2 CCR 154 | Taking an unmarried girl under 16 out of her father's possession: honest belief that she was over 16 was no defence |
| *R v. Tolson* (1889) 23 QBD 168 | Bigamy: honest and reasonable belief that the husband was dead was a defence |
| *Hobbs v. Winchester Corporation* [1910] 2 KB 471 | Selling unsound meat: liability without knowledge |
| *Sherras v. De Rutzen* [1895] 1 QB 918 | Presumption that *mens rea* is required; rebutted only by clear words or the subject matter |
| *Harding v. Price* [1948] 1 KB 695 | A lorry driver who did not know he had hit a vehicle was not guilty of failing to report: knowledge needed |
| *Gammon (Hong Kong) Ltd v. A-G of Hong Kong* [1985] AC 1 (PC) | The presumption of *mens rea* is strong but displaced where the statute deals with an issue of social concern and strict liability promotes its objects |
| *Sweet v. Parsley* [1970] AC 132 | A schoolteacher who let her house to students who used cannabis there: *mens rea* required for "being concerned in the management" |

4. **Indian position.** The presumption is that *mens rea* is required, but it is not unlimited. The Supreme Court has said that where the language and the object of the statute exclude it, the offence is strict. In *State of Maharashtra v. M.H. George* AIR 1965 SC 722 the accused, who carried gold from Zurich to Manila and did not declare it at Bombay, was convicted of contravening the Reserve Bank of India notification issued under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947, since *mens rea* was not necessary †. In *Nathulal v. State of M.P.* AIR 1966 SC 43 the accused was a grain dealer who stocked grain in contravention of a licensing order; the Court held that *mens rea* was an ingredient and acquitted him †. In *Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab* (1994) 3 SCC 569 the Court read a mental element into the TADA offences †.
5. **Absolute liability.** Offences made absolute by statute: the trader is liable even if the act was done by an employee and without his knowledge. (Compare **vicarious liability** in the criminal law, a limited exception: a licensee or owner is liable for the acts of employees only where the statute so provides †.)
6. **Comparison.**

| | Mens rea offence | Strict-liability offence |
|---|---|---|
| Mental element | Proved by the prosecution | Not needed as to the strict element |
| Defence of honest mistake | Available | Generally not |
| Typical penalty | Heavy | Lighter, often fine |
| Examples | Murder, theft | Food, drugs, customs, licensing |

## 6. Punishments under the BNS (Chapter II)

1. **Kinds of punishment (s. 4; IPC s. 53)** ‡: (a) **death**; (b) **imprisonment for life**; (c) **imprisonment**, which is of two descriptions: **rigorous** (with hard labour) or **simple**; (d) **forfeiture of property**; (e) **fine**; and (f) **community service** (new). *Community service* is unpaid work that benefits the community, ordered by the court for some petty offences. These are a few petty offences: a public servant unlawfully engaging in trade (s. 202 †), non-attendance in obedience to an order of a public servant (s. 209 †), an attempt to commit suicide to compel a public servant (s. 226), **theft of property worth less than ₹5,000 by a first offender who restores the property** (s. 303(2) proviso ‡), a drunk person's misconduct in public (s. 355 †) and defamation (s. 356(2) †).
2. **Related sections (all ‡ by heading).** s. 5: commutation of sentence (death and life imprisonment); s. 6: fractions of terms; s. 7: imprisonment wholly or partly rigorous or simple; s. 8: amount of fine and imprisonment in default; s. 9: limit of punishment of an offence made up of several offences; s. 10: punishment of a person guilty of one of several offences where it is doubtful which; s. 11: **solitary confinement**; s. 12: limit of solitary confinement; s. 13: enhanced punishment after previous conviction.
3. **Solitary confinement (s. 11; IPC s. 73; Dec 2025 A1).** Where a person is convicted of an offence for which the court may impose **rigorous imprisonment**, the court may order that he be kept in **solitary confinement** for any part of the imprisonment, **not exceeding three months in the whole**, according to this scale: up to **one month** if the imprisonment is **six months or less**; up to **two months** if it is **over six months and not over one year**; up to **three months** if it is **over one year** ‡. **Limit (s. 12; IPC s. 74):** the solitary confinement must **not exceed 14 days at a time**, with intervals of at least equal duration between the periods; where the sentence exceeds **three months**, it must not exceed **seven days in any one month**, with similar intervals ‡. The Supreme Court has held that solitary confinement can be imposed only by the **court as part of the sentence**, within these limits, and that jail authorities cannot impose it on a prisoner as a matter of course (*Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration* (1978) 4 SCC 494) †.
4. **Death penalty.** Awarded in the rarest of rare cases (*Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab* (1980) 2 SCC 684 — the Constitution Bench upheld the death penalty and laid down the rarest-of-rare test, which balances the **aggravating and mitigating** circumstances (*Machhi Singh v. State of Punjab* (1983) 3 SCC 470 listed five categories). *Mithu v. State of Punjab* (1983) 2 SCC 277 struck down IPC s. 303 (mandatory death for a murder by a life convict). BNS s. 104 now makes the punishment for murder by a life-convict **death or life imprisonment** (the remainder of his natural life), so there is no mandatory death sentence ‡.
5. **Life imprisonment.** It means imprisonment for the **remainder of the convict's natural life** unless the appropriate Government remits or commutes it under the BNSS (*Gopal Vinayak Godse v. State of Maharashtra* AIR 1961 SC 600). The Supreme Court has held that a court may fix a **minimum term** (more than 14 years) beyond which remission cannot be sought (*Swamy Shraddananda (2) v. State of Karnataka* (2008) 13 SCC 767, and the Constitution Bench in *Union of India v. V. Sriharan* (2016) 7 SCC 1) †. For fractions of terms, imprisonment for life is reckoned as 20 years (s. 6 †).
6. **Theories of punishment.**

| Theory | Idea | Criticism |
|---|---|---|
| **Retributive** | Punishment because the wrong deserves it | Vengeful; ignores the offender's future |
| **Deterrent** | Punish to deter the offender (special) and others (general) | Weak evidence for its effect; may punish for the sake of others |
| **Preventive** | Remove the offender from society (imprisonment, death) | Does not reform him |
| **Reformative** | Reform the offender through probation, parole, juvenile justice | May be too lenient; costly |
| **Expiatory** | The offender atones | Moral rather than legal |

The Indian system mixes them. The community-service provision and probation (BNSS and the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958) reflect the reformative theory; minimum sentences and the death penalty for aggravated murder reflect deterrence and retribution.

## 7. Key definitions (BNS s. 2; IPC Chapter II) and the General Explanations (s. 3)

| Term | BNS | IPC | Meaning (short) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dishonestly** | 2(7) | 24 | Doing a thing with the intention of causing **wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another** ‡ |
| **Fraudulently** | 2(9) | 25 | With intent to defraud, and not otherwise ‡ |
| **Good faith** | 2(11) | 52 | Nothing is said to be done or believed in good faith which is done or believed **without due care and attention** ‡ |
| **Injury** | 2(14) | 44 | Any harm whatever illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation or property ‡ |
| **Movable property** | 2(21) | 22 | Corporeal property of every description, except land and things attached to the earth, or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth ‡ |
| **Voluntarily** | 2(33) | 39 | By means which the person intended or knew or had reason to believe likely ‡ |
| **Wrongful gain / loss** | 2(36), 2(37) | 23 | Gain / loss by unlawful means of property to which the person gaining / losing is not legally entitled; includes **wrongful retention** and **wrongful deprivation** ‡ |
| **Child** | 2(3) | – | A person under 18 ‡ |
| **Offence** | 2(24) | 40 | A thing made punishable by the Sanhita ‡ |
| **Person** | 2(26) | 11 | Includes any company or association or body of persons ‡ |
| **Public servant** | 2(28) | 21 | Persons in the listed descriptions of Government and similar service ‡ |
| **Document** | 2(8) | 29 | Includes electronic and digital records ‡ |
| **Common intention** | 3(5) | 34 | See Unit 1B ‡ |

1. **Dishonestly and fraudulently.** *Dishonestly* needs an intention to cause **wrongful gain or loss** of property; *fraudulently* needs an intention to **deceive and thereby obtain an advantage or cause non-economic injury**. The two overlap. In *Dr. Vimla v. Delhi Administration* AIR 1963 SC 1572 the Court said that "defraud" involves deceit and injury, either actual or likely, to a person or to the public (a deprivation or risk of deprivation) †.
2. **Good faith.** A belief or act must be honest **and** with **due care and attention** (BNS s. 2(11)); an honest but careless act is not in good faith. The test is objective in this sense. In *Harbhajan Singh v. State of Punjab* AIR 1966 SC 97, on defamation, the Court said that good faith requires care and caution and a reasonable inquiry †.
3. **Movable property.** "Movable property is intended to include corporeal property of every description, except land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything which is attached to the earth." Things attached to the earth become movable once **severed** (a tree cut down; a crop reaped), which is why Explanation 1 to the section on theft says property attached to the earth is not the subject of theft until severed ‡. Electricity is movable property for theft purposes by the Electricity Act, 2003 (s. 135) †; whether digital data is "corporeal" is doubtful. Intangible property such as a copyright is not corporeal.
4. **Injury, hurt and harm.** *Injury* is the widest word and includes injury to reputation and property. *Hurt* (BNS s. 114) is bodily pain, disease or infirmity. *Grievous hurt* is a defined class (s. 116; Unit 4A).
5. **Wrongful gain and loss.** The definitions are **by unlawful means** and to which the person is **not legally entitled**. A person who recovers his own debt by force does not cause wrongful loss (he has a claim). "Wrongful loss" includes keeping a person out of property to which he is lawfully entitled.

**Preparation and attempt**

| Point | Preparation | Attempt |
|---|---|---|
| Stage | Arranging the means | Direct movement towards the offence |
| Punishable | Generally no | Yes (s. 62 and special sections) |
| Equivocal | Usually | Unequivocal |

**Mens rea and motive**

| Point | *Mens rea* | Motive |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The mental state required by the definition | The reason or inducement |
| Relevance to guilt | Essential (unless strict liability) | Not an element |
| Proof | Must be proved | May help prove identity or intention |

| Point | Strict liability | Absolute liability |
|---|---|---|

## 10. Criticism and evaluation

1. **The BNS and continuity.** The BNS renumbers, reorders and adds, but its core definitions are the IPC's; critics say the change is more cosmetic than structural, and that the renumbering imposes heavy costs of confusion on courts and the Bar. Supporters say it removes colonial language and adds modern offences.
2. **Mens rea is spread through the Code.** There is no general part stating when intention, knowledge or negligence is required; the courts must read it into each offence. The Law Commission has more than once suggested a general part.
3. **Strict liability.** It is efficient but can punish the innocent; the better view requires a statutory indication and a social danger.
4. **Punishment policy.** Mandatory minimum sentences, increased fines, and community service raise questions of proportionality and prison conditions.

## 11. Current developments (to 29 October 2026)

1. **Transition.** Offences before 1 July 2024 continue under the IPC; courts now deal with both codes together. The repeal section is BNS s. 358 ‡.
2. **New offences.** Organised crime (s. 111), petty organised crime (s. 112), terrorist act (s. 113), mob lynching (s. 103(2)), snatching (s. 304) ‡.
3. **Hit and run (s. 106(2)).** A Tribune report of 23 July 2025 says that the provision had not been implemented a year after the BNS came into force ‡. I could not confirm the position since then.
4. **Section 152 BNS.** On 8 August 2025 the Supreme Court issued notice on a PIL challenging s. 152 and tagged it with the pending challenge to IPC s. 124A ‡ (Unit 4C).
5. **Community service.** Introduced; the details of how it is to be implemented are matters for State rules and are not clear to me †.

## 12. Quick-revision box

- **Crime** = a legally prohibited act or omission, with a guilty mind, causing injury, punishable by the State. The Code defines "offence", not "crime" (BNS s. 2(24)).
- **Four elements:** human being · *mens rea* · *actus reus* · injury (BNS s. 2(14)).
- ***Mens rea*:** intention · knowledge · recklessness/negligence · dishonesty/fraud. Exceptions: strict liability.
- **Four stages:** intention (not punishable) · preparation (generally not) · attempt (s. 62; special sections) · accomplishment.
- **Attempt:** proximity; *Abhayanand Mishra*, *Malkiat Singh*, *Mohd. Yakub*; impossibility, the box-without-jewels illustration to s. 62.
- **Strict liability:** *Prince*, *Hobbs*, *Sherras*, *Gammon*; India: *M.H. George*, *Nathulal*.
- **Solitary confinement:** s. 11 (up to 3 months in the whole; 1/2/3 months by length of sentence); s. 12 (14 days at a time; 7 days a month where the sentence exceeds 3 months).
- **Punishments (s. 4):** death · life · imprisonment (rigorous/simple) · forfeiture · fine · community service.
